Madge Capehorn Edwards: The Life Made From a Sleeping Stranger

← Moses Hunnecker · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Homer Capehorn →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Homer Capehorn · Jacqueline White · Madge Capehorn · Vera Cartwheel

She is asleep on a bus, a stranger, and before she wakes the narrator has given her a husband, a rival, a jealousy, and a whole ruined marriage because being a face someone else builds a life onto is the entirety of what she is.

A thin, faded girl, prematurely old, pregnant and resisting the pregnancy as if it were a deformity, her tiny face hidden under a mask of heavy makeup streaked with grey, her colorless eyes not filled with the light which comes from love. She is dressed in her finest, and her finest is an accidental chaos, cheap cameos and colored glass, brass and wooden bracelets, cascades of ten-cent-store pearls dripping from her ears, velvet slippers on glass heels which should not carry her far, a stuffed bird nodding on her hat, and among all the rings no wedding ring. Her orange silk dress is painted all over with little sailboats and red football players and white skiers, a whole world of images running across her body. She sleeps sitting stiffly upright so her hair will not be disarranged, and she cries in her sleep. Vera, a few seats away, watches, and out of the mask and the painted dress and the missing ring she builds her.

Radical Immanence holds two things that look separate. It says witnessing others is the central ethical act, and it says reality is made rather than found. Madge is where the book shows they are one thing. She is a stranger Vera will never speak to, Madge Capehorn, poor little Madge Edwards that has a husband called Homer, a dead rival called Jacqueline, a jealousy, a wedding, a grief. The question the book asks through her is whether that is witnessing or invention, and the answer it gives is that there is no difference. To see a stranger truly, in this book, is already to make her up.

Madge’s marriage is the proof. None of it is reported. All of it is built, in what the novel calls a border world between the waking and the sleeping, out of one sleeping woman's face. Homer Capehorn, the faded all-star athlete, lies dreaming of the dead girl he loved and never won, the ice-skater who could skate like a comet, and calls her name in his sleep. Madge lies beside him, older than the others, the fast one, heavy with another man's child, fiercely jealous of a rival who is already in the grave, insisting that the dead girl is nothing, that the bridegroom is the skeleton. Vera invents every word of this, and the book gives the invented marriage as much weight, as much detail, and as much ache as any marriage Vera knew about. The made-up Madge suffers as fully as the real Catherine.

There is a doubling worth noticing. Inside Vera's invention, Madge's own life is ruled by a dead woman. Homer's love for the dead Jacqueline is more alive than his marriage to the living Madge, so that even the constructed stranger is built around the book's oldest law, that the dead do not leave. Vera invents for Madge the exact haunting Vera herself lives under, the dead crowding out the living, which is a quiet sign that the life she builds for the stranger is a mirror held up to her own. The invention is a confession. Handed a blank face on a bus, the narrator fills it with her own grief and calls the result another woman.

And where Catherine is the woman to whom everything happens on the inside, Madge is the woman to whom everything happens on the outside, in a body, in a single lurching moment on a flooded road. She is pregnant and married and betrayed and coming to her early grave all at once, this dead center of life, about to be delivered of herself or of a stranger. She resists all of it, resists the child, resists sleep, wishes she were already in the ground with her little hands reaching above the earth. Catherine escaped into the dream and could not be reached. Madge cannot escape her body and cannot get out of the one terrible instant. The book sets the two women at opposite poles of the same helplessness, the one who is all hidden interior and the one who is all exposed surface, and it grants them equal seriousness, though it had to invent almost the whole of Madge to do it.

The lesson for a writer lives at the edge of what a narrator can know. A careful writer might keep a first-person narrator honest by letting her report only what she could actually learn, and fall silent before the stranger on the bus. Young does the opposite. She lets Vera build the stranger an entire life, and by refusing to mark that life as any less real than the reported ones, she makes her deepest claim in the plainest possible place. Every person anyone knows is partly built by the knower. The line between seeing someone and inventing her is itself a fiction. The honest response is not to stop inventing but to admit that seeing was always an invention, and then to do it with love. Madge is a woman made out of a sleeping face on a bus, and she is as real as anyone in the novel, because in this novel that is the only way anyone is real.
