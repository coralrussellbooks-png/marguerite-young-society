What This Book Has Followed

This book has followed one idea through many doors. What Miss MacIntosh, My Darling does with its sentences is what it believes about the mind, and what it believes about the mind is what it believes about how people should live together. The essays have walked from Plato's cave to Boethius's music, from Schopenhauer's blind will to Nietzsche's overman, from Dante's circles to Esther Longtree's lunch counter. Each time, the novel has done the same thing. It has taken one of the great old claims about reality and turned it down, gently, one sentence at a time.

Turning a claim down is not the same as giving up on truth. The easiest way to misread Young is to think she prefers illusion to reality, or dreams to the world. That is Miss MacIntosh's reading of Catherine, and it is wrong in the way Miss MacIntosh is often wrong: right about the facts and wrong about what they mean. Catherine is not celebrating unreason. She is surviving. The opium bed is not a rejection of the world. It is how one woman lives in a world that has never been big enough for what she needs.

From there comes the novel's quiet politics. Cruelty between people often begins with one claim: that there is a single right way to see the world and a single right kind of person to live in it, and that whoever does not fit is not just different but wrong. Young's sentences refuse to sort people that way. Every thing in her long lists gets its own “and.” Every voice gets a room. Nobody is put in the wrong column.

The novel says so outright in its first chapter: “Every heart is the other heart. Every soul is the other soul. Every face is the other face. The individual is the one illusion” (Chapter 1). Baruch Spinoza, the Dutch philosopher of the 1600s, taught that everything that exists is one substance, which he called God or Nature, and that each of us is a passing shape of it. Young gives him a bed. In Chapter 24, as Catherine's imaginary guests are given their rooms, “Spinoza and Shakespeare and Mrs. Sydington were already in the green.” The philosopher of oneness shares a room with a playwright and a Mrs. Sydington, and none of them outranks the others.

The Morning the Wig Came Off

The book's argument comes to a point on the morning after, in Chapter 63, when Vera finally speaks up to Miss MacIntosh. “What is the use of keeping house when we have no house to keep? You yourself have often asked,” she says. And a little later: “Miss MacIntosh, you are so busy remembering who you are, you forget who I am. And why should you forget me?”

That second question is the novel's plainest challenge to anyone who claims to know what is real. Miss MacIntosh has been so busy being Miss MacIntosh, keeping up the red wig and the plain good sense, that she has not looked at the girl in front of her.

Her answer starts in anger and ends somewhere much more honest. “Nonsense, oh, nonsense which kills us,” she moans. “It is you who dream,” she cries, “my life away. How shall I show my grief? Shall I tear my hair? Shall I scream and rage and cry out for help? Who can help me but God Himself Who has already failed and is not?” And she holds to the belief she has lived by: “imagination is nothing. The life is what counts.”

The life is what counts. But the life she has shown the world, the wig and the good humor, is not the life she has. Her real life is the one she has hidden: the bald head, and the question she asks that same morning, “What’s this salt taste of blood in my mouth this morning and every other morning? Oh, my heart, my poor, poor, poor heart so often cheated!”

An Umbrella for Every Man

Then Miss MacIntosh does something only she could do. She gives Vera a history of the umbrella. Jonas Hanway, she says, found it in the Orient, where it was a sign “of the superiority of one man over his fellowman.” She will have none of that: “Balderdash, nonsense, for none is better than his poorest brother, the beggar in the streets, and there have been many bald kings in mortal annals, though doubtless fewer queens who had no hair and no eyelashes.” The umbrella once held by a servant over an emperor “might now be carried by each man over himself. What a riot there was!” Now “even the poorest man might be his own coach, horse, covering, sail, coachman.”

It is funny, and it is meant to be. It is also the whole argument in a raincoat. Fairness between people does not come down from a throne. It is a small, practical arrangement: each person carrying a roof over their own head, each one entitled to shelter from whatever falls. The woman of plain sense ends up teaching the most generous idea in the book, and she cannot resist adding the one thing better than the umbrella, “the mackintosh raincoat which also covers the poor man and his chill bones.”

The Hint to Go

Then comes her farewell. She has already said, “I shall go with the setting moon.” Now she says: “Here’s the hint we sometimes need to go on. Give but a gentle shove, and all will be right. Let it not be said that I lingered beyond my period of usefulness. When you invite me to your wedding, dear, when the funeral bells are ringing over the water, invite me not to many more.” In Chapter 65, as Vera tells it, she walks into the sea.

So the going on in this novel belongs to the ones who stay. Esther Longtree names it at her lunch counter: “the way I go on here against all odds” (Chapter 72). The stone deaf man names it by stating his condition: “I am a stone deaf man” (Chapter 82). He does not complain. He has built a life on that fact, a life with a butterfly loud around his head and fishes whispering at the bottoms of rivers.

And Vera names what she is looking for in the first chapter: “What motive in this quest but the search for life, for love, for truth that does not fail?” Her reasons are a crowd of other people: “a dead girl’s love letters scattered on the floor of her empty bedroom,” “a deaf musician,” “a drunkard’s celestial dream of childhood,” and a blind man at an all-night lunch stand who asked his companion, “When shall the light, Peter, enter my soul?” Her search is everyone's search, as The Search for Life, for Love, for Truth That Does Not Fail traces. She carries all of them with her.

Truth That Does Not Fail

The great philosophers each offered a truth that does not fail. Plato's Good does not change. Augustine's City of God does not fall. Boethius's music of the spheres keeps perfect time. But each of these truths stays safe by staying far away from an actual life, from a brother's death, a blind man's coffee cup, a woman's stillborn child.

Young offers a different one: not a truth that escapes loss but a truth that keeps going through it. Each person in the novel holds it in a different way. Esther's way is her heart, as she describes it in Chapter 72: “I seem to have what is known as intuition, understanding, compassion, passion, a great strength to go on, and why no little child?” And: “It is because I have an enlarged heart with ample leakage and great sympathy like the sky which goes everywhere and has no ending.” It is also the seven thick white cups she sets in a row on the counter, “For seven skulls but no coffee for them, the little bird bones.” What these people share is not one belief. It is the willingness to keep living, honestly, with what is not there.

Gentle Disguises and a Fire on the Beach

Each of Young's people lives inside some kind of disguise: Miss MacIntosh's wig, Catherine's opium and her house of imaginary guests, Mr. Spitzer's silent music, Esther's pregnancy that never ends. The novel does not tear these off as lies. It shows them as shelters, the things people build to live in a world that would be too hard without them. Young does want us to see the bald head. She builds a whole chapter toward it. But seeing through a disguise does not leave a clean, clear mind behind. It leaves a person who knows that what she lives in was built, and who still has to live somewhere.

Miss MacIntosh's own plain advice, from Chapter 3, turns out to be the best answer: “One should mind his own knitting, his own cooking, his own household duties even though he had no house. One should learn to build a fire upon the beach, perhaps a signal fire for some poor sailor clinging to a life raft and searching the darkening waters and not knowing where the land was until he saw this fire or the gleamings of the embers as of the ember stars like ashes in the wind.” When there is no house, build a fire on the beach. Not because it will become a house. Not because the sailor will surely see it. Because that is what there is to do. And the right to a gentle disguise, a wig or a dream or a careful schedule for one's sorrow, belongs to everyone, whether the world thinks the disguise respectable or not.

No Hour to Go Back To

Under every other argument in this book runs one more: there is no perfect hour in the past to go back to.

Karl Popper, the Austrian-born philosopher, wrote The Open Society and Its Enemies in New Zealand during the war, and it was published in London in 1945, two years before Young began her novel. Popper read Plato as a man who saw history as a long fall from a better past and who wanted to stop all change. He gave a name to the unease of living in a free, changing society, where nobody's place is fixed and each person has to decide for themselves: “the strain of civilization.” Leaders who promise to end that strain by taking everyone back to an older, simpler order are offering something that cannot be had. “There is no return to a harmonious state of nature,” Popper wrote (The Open Society and Its Enemies, Routledge, 1945, volume 1, chapter 10).

Young knew people who had paid the price of that promise. Her friend the novelist Marianne Hauser was born in Strasbourg in 1910. In 1944 she married Fred Kirchberger, a concert pianist trained in Berlin and at Juilliard. Kirchberger had left Germany in 1938, when he could not give a recital in Berlin because his mother was Jewish. He served in the United States Army as a German language specialist in the American South. Their son Michael was born in 1945, and Young was his godmother. Young was a founding fiction editor of The Tiger’s Eye, where Hauser's story “The Mouse” appeared in 1949. What Young understood, knowing Kirchberger and Hauser, was how a world like theirs is taken apart: not first by armies but by a claim, that there is one right kind of person and one right kind of music, and that everyone else belongs in the wrong column. (Sources: mariannehauser.com, the Dark Dominion page; Wikipedia, “Marianne Hauser”; Encyclopedia.com; margueriteyoung.site.)

Young answers that claim with her clocks. Miss Havisham, in Dickens, stops every clock at the single hour of her wound. That one stopped hour is the shape of every promise of return: there was a perfect moment before the harm, I know what it was, give me the power to bring it back. In the Cartwheel house, as Each Clock Stopped at a Different Hour shows, each clock was stopped at a different hour, as Catherine went to bed “by slow degrees and not suddenly” (Chapter 12). There was never one pure hour. Catherine's wounds are real, her invisible birthmark among them (Chapter 2), but the time before them was never whole. The past in this novel is not a lost golden room. It is layers laid down one on another, and every layer is mixed.

That is the difference between a monument and a carrying. A monument says: here is the fixed point we lost and can rebuild. Carrying says: there is no fixed point, only the weight of everything handed down to us, which we carry and pass on. Everything in Young's book works against the single stopped hour: the clocks at many hours, the long sentences joined by “and,” the nearly 4,000 question marks left open. A person who knows she is made of many hours cannot easily be talked into going back to just one. The camps were built anyway. The music was driven out anyway. Young does not pretend that her way of seeing protects anyone. It is what remains when protection has failed, and it is still worth doing.

The Wedding and the Sign

In the last pages, Vera and the stone deaf man plan a wedding, and everyone is invited:

We would be married and would invite everybody to our wedding, little Johnny-jump-up and little jack-in-the-pulpit and the snapdragon and four o’clock and whippoorwill and bluejay and little jenny wren and bobolink and woodpecker and little robin redbreast, all the wild flowers and all the birds and little tommy titmouse and the waters and the clouds and the moon and the sun and the stars and the old mule that leaned over the fence and Esther Longtree, for she was also our love, all our loves, and she was the mother of the stillborn clapping their hands in clouds, and she loved us all and all things hurt or silent or broken or dead or living, and we were all so much like her little children that we might have been the children of her dreams. Esther Longtree would come.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, Chapter 82

Everyone is invited. Not only the ones who meet some standard, but all things “hurt or silent or broken or dead or living.” The old mule. The snapdragon. The stillborn clapping their hands in clouds. And Esther is not just a guest. She is “the mother of the stillborn,” and the guests are so much like her children “that we might have been the children of her dreams.” Then she gets a line of her own: “Esther Longtree would come.”

The novel ends on her sign: “She would hang a sign in the restaurant window—Owt to luntsch. Bee bak in a whale. For she could not spell either.”

The sign is wrong by every rule in Miss MacIntosh's schoolroom. It is spelled the way Esther spells, by someone no school ever served well. And it is a promise: I am going out for a while, and I will be back. Bee bak in a whale. The whale is the great sea of things the novel has gathered for 1,198 pages, “and” by “and,” and Esther will be back inside it, with her seven cups and the stillborn in the clouds. She has only stepped away from the counter for the little while a person needs between one way of going on and the next.

The Radical Immanence Reading

In the language of Radical Immanence, this closing is Pillar 11 (The Crossing Point): each person stands where the daily life of the present crosses everything handed down from the past, made of what passes through and passing it on. The clocks stopped at many hours are Pillar 8 (The Past Doesn't Go Away): nothing is cleared away, and there is no clean hour to return to. The wig, the umbrella, and the house of imaginary guests are Pillar 1 (Not One Self): “The individual is the one illusion,” and every disguise is one more shelter for a self that was never single. The fire on the beach is Pillar 5 (Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary). And Esther's seven cups, set out for children who will never drink from them, are Pillar 3 (Grief Doesn't End).

This Is Not a Consolation

Young does not deal in consolations, and it would be wrong to end this book with one. The crossing point is not a comfortable place to stand. Miss MacIntosh's hint was not a reason to go on. It was a hint to go. The ones who stay are left with very little: no destination, no promise that the next step will settle anything, only the step.

The cave, in the end, is not a prison. It is a house full of clocks stopped at different hours, each one the record of a life that chose many truths over the clean, hard ease of a single one. Young does not ask us to escape it. She asks us to live in it honestly, with the wig off, and to keep house however we can. To build the signal fire on the beach. To set out the cups. To speak to the silence we love, as Vera does under the trees on the last night of the novel: “I spelled out my name upon my lover’s hand, for surely a woman must find some way by which to talk to the silence that she loved.”

The sentence is the argument. The way Young writes is the way she believes people should live together. And that is not a place anyone arrives. It is a practice, done every day: holding all of what is here, building a fire though the house has no roof, and hanging the sign in the window that says, in the spelling of someone no rulebook ever served: I was here. I will be back. The whale is large. I am in it. And I am coming back.
