Chapter 16

Summary

On the night of her fourteenth birthday, Vera rides her old black pony, Falada, bareback through the moonlit grounds, laughing, imagining herself a circus rider in pink tights, and whispering her secrets into his ear: that she is an orphan, that Miss MacIntosh is “an old witch who kept me enchained in a dark tower.” Mr. Spitzer gave her the pony, which had been sent to him by an admirer of his dead brother, and he named its pedigree: the horse that inspired merry-go-rounds and Queen Anne chair legs, and the speaking head of an old fairy tale. Vera rides Falada into the house, past the doge’s lions, the funeral gondola, and the tapestries of angels healing the blind and the deaf. She does not dare enter her mother’s room, for fear Catherine would know they were real. She rides on to Miss MacIntosh, who would. The door, always barred from inside, swings open. In the moonlight Miss MacIntosh stands by the window, “unbelievably not herself,” and yet Vera knows her at once.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter begins with a powerful, almost dreamlike description of Vera Cartwheel riding her black pony, Falada, on the night of her fourteenth birthday. This passage captures the fierce pride and exhilaration of youth, the connection between rider and horse, and the blurring of reality and fantasy. The pony, though old and worn, moves with a spirit and grace that transcends its physical limitations, symbolizing both endurance and the imagination’s power to transform the mundane into the magical. This sets the tone for the chapter’s exploration of innocence, freedom, and escape.
  • As Vera and Falada ride through the dark allees, the narrative paints a haunting picture of the estate: burned trees, untended gardens, broken statues, faded tennis courts, gargoyles, and fountains. This imagery evokes decay, neglect, and the passage of time, contrasting with Vera’s youthful vitality. The path circles “like an eye through the garden of the blind,” the real garden the grandfather planted for his blind wife in Chapter 12.
  • Vera’s intimate bond with Falada is a central passage. She confides her deepest secrets to the horse about her orphanhood, her view of Miss MacIntosh as “an old witch who kept me enchained in a dark tower”, her hopes for the future. This reveals Vera’s vulnerability and longing for understanding and companionship. The horse is not just a companion but almost a confidant and extension of her inner world. The symbolic act of tying blue ribbons on Falada’s tail and whispering secrets reflects her need for connection and expression.
  • Another significant passage is the explanation of Falada’s pedigree and the importance Mr. Spitzer places on names. Falada is linked to what Mr. Spitzer calls an old Arabic fairy tale, in fact the Grimms’ German “The Goose Girl,” whose horse’s head spoke oracles after death, and also to historical influences on furniture design. This passage layers the narrative with mythic and historical resonance, emphasizing the power of names and stories to shape identity and legacy. It also ties the horse, and by extension Vera, to a continuum of cultural memory and symbolism.
  • Vera and Falada’s passage through the shadowy, cavernous house filled with marble statues, mosaics, and relics creates a surreal atmosphere, blending dream and reality. The house is described as a tomb or underwater castle, with muted sounds and mysterious forms, evoking themes of death, memory, and entrapment. Vera’s hesitation to confront her mother, who may be lost in her own dreams or visions, underscores the emotional distance and isolation within the household. This passage poignantly captures the tension between presence and absence, life and death, reality and fantasy.
  • The chapter reaches an emotional climax when Vera and Falada enter Miss MacIntosh’s bedroom. The door opens creakily, the pony whinnies, and Vera observes Miss MacIntosh standing by the window, bathed in moonlight. The description of Miss MacIntosh as changed and “unbelievably not herself,” yet instantly recognizable, suggests a profound, possibly unsettling transformation. This moment encapsulates the themes of recognition, change, and possibly impending confrontation or revelation. Vera’s reflection that this recognition affirms an older shock buried beneath layers of time adds a layer of mystery and foreshadowing.
  • Falada’s head speaks after death from above the gate. Vera’s pony carries her to the door behind which Miss MacIntosh is about to be revealed bald, in Chapter 17.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “I often did not know what I thought until I heard myself speaking the words.” What does Vera learn by telling Falada her secrets?
  • “He seemed to be my head, my intelligence. I was the headless rider.” Who is leading whom?
  • “Water knew no echo.” Why is the house so silent?

Characters

  • Vera tells Falada that Miss MacIntosh is a witch, yet rides to her because she “would know that we were real.” What does Vera need from her?
  • Mr. Spitzer believes “all things were in their names.” How does that fit a man who is mistaken for his brother?
  • Falada came from someone who “had not known… that Mr. Spitzer’s brother was dead.” What else in Mr. Spitzer’s life comes from Peron?

Connections

  • Falada’s head, nailed above a gate, speaks after death, and the sea returned “a strange marble head” for Saul in Chapter 15. Why do heads keep speaking in this novel?
  • The tapestries show angels who “restored sight to the blind and hearing to the deaf,” after the gardens of the blind and the deaf in Chapters 12 and 13. What is being healed?
  • Vera is “the headless rider,” after the headless horsemen of Chapter 12. What is she riding toward?

Reflection

  • Is a secret real before it is spoken?
  • Why might someone fear that being real would frighten another person more than being a phantom?
  • What is the first moment someone you knew became a stranger?

  • She is imaginative, free-spirited, and deeply connected to her pony, Falada.
  • She reflects on her orphaned status, her struggles, and her dreams.

Miss MacIntosh

  • Miss MacIntosh is her caretaker, described as an “old witch” by Vera, who holds a controlling influence over Vera’s life.
  • Her door, “usually locked and barred from the inside,” opens of itself, and she stands by the window, “greatly changed… unbelievably not herself.”

Falada

  • Vera’s old black pony, described with affection, mythic qualities, and a symbolic presence.
  • Falada is both a real animal and a figure of imagination and myth.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Falada

See: Horses and Carriages

  • Falada, the little black pony given to Vera by Mr. Spitzer, carries rich symbolic meaning that intertwines with Vera’s inner world and her journey.
  • Falada is described as an old, tired, wind-broken pony, light as a spirit, with a sagging back, a scrappy mane, and a knotted tail. Despite his physical frailty and advanced age, Falada moves with grace and seems almost magical, responding to Vera’s desires as if he were her own intelligence or head. The pony is said to be descended from a horse that inspired the jewel-studded horses of merry-go-rounds and carousels, connecting him to themes of childhood innocence and the cyclical nature of life and dreams.
  • Falada embodies a loyal, gentle companion who carries Vera through her internal struggles and the uncertain landscapes of her life. His responsiveness to her unspoken commands suggests an intimate connection between Vera’s mind and her means of navigating reality and illusion.
  • Although Falada is physically frail, he continues to carry Vera with grace and resilience. This mirrors Vera’s own vulnerability and strength as she faces the challenges of growing up and understanding complex realities.
  • Falada’s origin as a model for carousel horses evokes childhood fantasy and cyclical repetition, resonating with Vera’s oscillation between dream and reality. His presence blurs the lines between the tangible and the imaginary, reflecting the novel’s themes of perception and illusion.
  • Given by Mr. Spitzer, Falada also symbolizes a connection to a past that is both real and mythical, and perhaps a hope for guidance and stability amid chaos. His lineage to historical and artistic forms evokes continuity and the search for meaning.
  • Falada is described as seeming like Vera’s head or intelligence, suggesting that the pony is an extension or manifestation of her self-awareness and agency, her “headless rider” identity where the horse seems to guide the rider as much as the rider guides the horse.
  • Falada is a potent symbol of Vera’s complex inner life: her innocence, her challenges, her search for identity and direction, and the interplay between dream and reality that defines her experience in the novel.

The Night and the Grounds

See: Moon and Stars

  • The story is set at night, under the moonlight, creating a dreamlike atmosphere.
  • The surroundings include dark alleys, stunted trees, an old stable, gardens, fountains, statues, and a large, mysterious house with marble pillars, tapestries, and grand stairways.
  • The moonlit garden resembles a “garden of the blind,” and the house is described like a “water castle” with sea motifs and underwater imagery.
  • The atmosphere is eerie yet magical, with references to stars, fireflies, and phantoms.
  • Rich sensory descriptions evoke visual, tactile, and auditory experiences.

Research: Resources

  • “The fierce, exultant pride that was mine”: In “What Is It with Women and Horses?” (Intelligent Horsemanship, 2019), Dr. Rosie Jones-McVey, drawing on her PhD research among British horsewomen, writes: “In Victorian novels, as well as in the more modern genre of pony-girl books, it is often aboard a horse that girls find their wings, often proving some snot-nosed boy wrong in the process, or enabling them to escape various romantic dramas and find solace in the barn. Just like the pony-girl books, many of my participants’ life stories entwined horseyness with coming-of-age stories. Horses were the means through which many women first acted in a truly independent way, often pursuing equestrian endeavours despite/without their parents, or else, proudly taking up adult-like responsibilities in being the teacher, pilot, and carer of their ponies, rather than the one being looked after and told what to do.” She argues that horses give many women a space for independence and self-assertion that their everyday lives do not. See Freedom and Escape, Women, Gender, and Marriage, and Horses and Carriages.
  • “riding my black pony, Falada”: In the Brothers Grimm fairy tale “The Goose Girl” (1815), a princess is sent off to marry with her magical horse Falada, who can talk, and a waiting maid. The maid forces the princess to change places with her and, once she is the bride, has the prince kill Falada so he cannot tell. The princess has the horse’s head nailed under the dark gateway, and each morning it speaks to her as she passes: “Alas, young Queen, how ill you fare! / If this your tender mother knew, / Her heart would surely break in two.” In the end the king learns the truth and the false bride is punished. Falada means “spoken” in Portuguese. See Falada and Fairy Tales and Folklore.
    • Mr. Spitzer calls it “an old Arabic fairy tale which had its parallels in many countries, in many ages,” and tells of the head that “had spoken oracles and parables to the people passing through the gates.”
  • “a path that made a circle like an eye through the garden of the blind”: The grandmother’s garden of the blind. See Eyes and Blindness and the Chapter 12 resources.
  • “It was a steeplechase, and we would jump over all the church steeples”: The first steeplechase (1752) was a race across Irish country from one church steeple to the next. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “I was a bareback rider, wearing a plume, a pair of pink tights”: Philip Astley’s London circus (1768) began as trick riding in a ring, and the bareback equestrienne in tights became the star of American circuses. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “Falada could dance on his hind feet”: The levade and capriole of the Lipizzaners of Vienna’s Spanish Riding School, where horses rear and leap on command. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “Miss MacIntosh was an old witch who kept me enchained in a dark tower”: Rapunzel, locked in a tower by a witch. See Fairy Tales and Folklore, Miss MacIntosh, and the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “served as a model for merry-go-round horses because of his grace in keeping all fours off the earth at once”: In 1878 Eadweard Muybridge photographed Leland Stanford’s horse Sallie Gardner and proved that a galloping horse lifts all four hooves at once, though not stretched out, as on carousel horses. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “had been sent to Mr. Spitzer by someone who had admired Mr. Spitzer’s brother”: Peron, the gambler of the horse circles (Chapter 14). See Peron Spitzer, Joachim Spitzer, and the Chapter 14 resources.
  • “the cabriole, the furniture of good Queen Anne’s day, whole forests of Chippendale”: Queen Anne furniture stands on curved cabriole legs, shaped like an animal’s leg. The name comes from the French cabriole, a goat’s leap. In dressage, the capriole is the horse’s leap with all four feet off the ground. Thomas Chippendale published his Director in 1754. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “the legs of a chair were referred to as the limbs”: The story comes from Captain Frederick Marryat’s Diary in America (1839), where an American lady was shocked by the word “leg,” and piano legs were said to wear frilled trousers.
  • “a rose by any other name would smell as sweet”: Romeo and Juliet 2.2. Catherine forgot the coachman’s name in Chapter 14, while Mr. Spitzer believes “all things were in their names.” See Shakespeare and the Chapter 14 resources.
  • “the sleeping marble lions which had once guarded the palace of a Venetian doge”: The winged lion of St. Mark is the emblem of Venice, ruled by its doges for a thousand years.
  • “the mosaic emperors like the faces of old playing cards”: The Byzantine mosaics of Justinian and Theodora at San Vitale in Ravenna (547).
  • “the kings with the triple crowns”: The triple crown is the papal tiara. See the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “the black funeral gondola”: Venice’s black funeral gondolas. See Weddings and Funerals and the Chapter 14 resources.
  • “the great mother-of-pearl mollusk which might have contained the form of Venus”: Botticelli’s The Birth of Venus (c. 1485) shows the goddess rising from the sea on a scallop shell. See Greek Mythology and The Sea and Water.
  • “the phoenix rising from those cold ashes of the dream”: The phoenix, which dies in fire and rises from its own ashes. See Birds and the Chapter 7 resources.
  • “the effigy of a sleeping marble boy lying on his tomb”: Medieval tomb effigies often rest their feet on a dog, the sign of fidelity. See Dogs and Death and Mortality.
  • “angels who restored sight to the blind and hearing to the deaf”: “Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped” (Isaiah 35:5). The gardens of the blind and of the deaf were Chapters 12 and 13. See Angels and Archangels, Eyes and Blindness, Silence and Deafness, and The Bible.
  • “the stuffed lute birds”: The lyrebird of Australia, named for its lyre-shaped tail. See Birds and the Chapter 3 resources.
  • “the shell which was the great turbaned Turk, the phaeton, the winding stairway”: These are real shells. The turban shell is one, and the wentletrap takes its name from the Dutch for “winding staircase.” See The Sea and Water.
  • “I was the headless rider”: “The Legend of Sleepy Hollow” again. See Fairy Tales and Folklore and the Chapter 12 resources.
  • “as many husbands as Solomon had wives”: “And he had seven hundred wives, princesses, and three hundred concubines” (1 Kings 11:3). See The Bible and the Chapter 9 resources.

Vocabulary

Allée
A walk or path lined with trees or tall hedges. Young writes “allees.”
Ascertain
To find out for certain.
Bareback
Riding a horse without a saddle.
Bellrope
A rope pulled to ring a bell, such as one that summons a servant.
Brackish
Of water: slightly salty, as where a river meets the sea.
Cabriole
A curved furniture leg shaped like an animal’s leg, from the French for a goat’s leap.
Canter
A horse’s easy three-beat gait, between a trot and a gallop.
Carousel
A merry-go-round. Earlier, a tournament in which horsemen performed exercises and displays.
Centaur
In Greek mythology, a creature with the head and upper body of a man and the body and legs of a horse.
Cherubim
Winged angelic beings. In art they are often shown as chubby winged children. The singular is cherub.
Cheval glass
A tall mirror swung in a frame so that it can be tilted.
Chippendale
Furniture in the style of the English cabinetmaker Thomas Chippendale (1718-1779).
Cuirass
Armor for the chest and back.
Dank
Unpleasantly damp and cold.
Divan
A long, low couch without a back or arms.
Doge
The elected ruler of Venice.
Effigy
A sculpture or model of a person, especially one lying on a tomb.
Equine
A horse; of horses.
Ethereal
Light, airy, and delicate, as if not of this world; heavenly.
Exultant
Triumphantly joyful.
Gable
The triangular upper end of a wall under a pitched roof.
Gargoyle
A carved grotesque figure on a building, often serving as a waterspout.
Jaunty
Lively and self-confident; set at a rakish angle.
Livid
Bluish-gray, the color of a bruise. Also furiously angry.
Lute
A plucked string instrument with a rounded back shaped like half a pear, popular in Europe from the Middle Ages through the 1700s.
Marquee
A large tent or awning.
Medusa
A jellyfish in its free-swimming, bell-shaped stage, named for the Gorgon of Greek myth whose hair was snakes.
Mollusk
A soft-bodied creature, such as a snail or clam, usually with a shell.
Mosaic
A picture or pattern made by setting small pieces of colored stone, glass, or tile together.
Mother-of-pearl
The shining, rainbow-colored lining of some shells, used for inlay.
Oracle
A message from the gods; a source of prophecy.
Paddock
A small field where horses are kept.
Pallbearer
One who helps carry a coffin at a funeral.
Parable
A short story that teaches a lesson.
Pedigree
The recorded line of descent of an animal or person.
Peruke
A powdered wig of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.
Phaeton
A light, open, four-wheeled carriage; also a name for some seashells.
Phosphorescent
Glowing faintly in the dark.
Portico
A porch with a roof supported by columns.
Prism
A clear piece of glass with flat sides that splits light into the colors of the rainbow.
Scalloped
Edged with a series of curves like the rim of a scallop shell.
Steeplechase
A horse race over fences, ditches, and water jumps, first run cross-country from one church steeple to another.
Tapestry
A heavy cloth woven with pictures, hung on walls.
Tendon
A tough cord that joins muscle to bone.
Triton
A sea god of Greek myth, half man and half fish, who blows a conch shell.
Turret
A small tower on a building or castle.
Whinny
A horse’s gentle neigh.
Wind-broken
Of a horse: short of breath from damaged lungs.
