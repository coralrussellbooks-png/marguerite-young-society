Summary

Vera rides her old black pony, Falada, through the dark grounds, laughing and whispering secrets into his ear. She decides not to bring a real horse into her mother's dream-filled bedroom. Instead she rides him up the stairs of the great house, past mosaic emperors, a funeral gondola, and tapestries of angels healing the blind and the deaf. They reach Miss MacIntosh's door, always barred from inside, and it swings open. In the moonlight Miss MacIntosh stands by the window, greatly changed, unbelievably not herself, and yet Vera recognizes her instantly.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter begins with a powerful, almost dreamlike description of Vera Cartwheel riding her black pony, Falada, on the night of her fourteenth birthday. This passage captures the fierce pride and exhilaration of youth, the connection between rider and horse, and the blurring of reality and fantasy. The pony, though old and worn, moves with a spirit and grace that transcends its physical limitations, symbolizing both endurance and the imagination's power to transform the mundane into the magical. This sets the tone for the chapter’s exploration of innocence, freedom, and escape.
  • As Vera and Falada ride through the dark allees, the narrative paints a haunting picture of the estate: burned trees, untended gardens, broken statues, faded tennis courts, gargoyles, and fountains. This imagery evokes decay, neglect, and the passage of time, contrasting with Vera’s youthful vitality. The garden as “the garden of the blind” metaphorically suggests blindness to the present or to reality, deepening the theme of lost grandeur and forgotten history.
  • Vera’s intimate bond with Falada is a central passage. She confides her deepest secrets to the horse about her orphanhood, her dislike for Miss MacIntosh, her hopes for the future. This reveals Vera’s vulnerability and longing for understanding and companionship. The horse is not just a companion but almost a confidant and extension of her inner world. The symbolic act of tying blue ribbons on Falada’s tail and whispering secrets reflects her need for connection and expression.
  • Another significant passage is the explanation of Falada’s pedigree and the importance Mr. Spitzer places on names. Falada is linked to an old Arabic fairy tale horse whose head spoke oracles after death, and also to historical influences on furniture design. This passage layers the narrative with mythic and historical resonance, emphasizing the power of names and stories to shape identity and legacy. It also ties the horse, and by extension Vera, to a continuum of cultural memory and symbolism.
  • Vera and Falada’s passage through the shadowy, cavernous house filled with marble statues, mosaics, and relics creates a surreal atmosphere, blending dream and reality. The house is described as a tomb or underwater castle, with muted sounds and mysterious forms, evoking themes of death, memory, and entrapment. Vera’s hesitation to confront her mother, who may be lost in her own dreams or visions, underscores the emotional distance and isolation within the household. This passage poignantly captures the tension between presence and absence, life and death, reality and fantasy.
  • The chapter reaches an emotional climax when Vera and Falada enter Miss MacIntosh’s bedroom. The door opens creakily, the pony whinnies, and Vera observes Miss MacIntosh standing by the window, bathed in moonlight. The description of Miss MacIntosh as changed and “unbelievably not herself,” yet instantly recognizable, suggests a profound, possibly unsettling transformation. This moment encapsulates the themes of recognition, change, and possibly impending confrontation or revelation. Vera’s reflection that this recognition affirms an older shock buried beneath layers of time adds a layer of mystery and foreshadowing.

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • Vera, the protagonist, a fourteen-year-old girl.
  • She is imaginative, free-spirited, and deeply connected to her pony, Falada.
  • She reflects on her orphaned status, her struggles, and her dreams.
  • Mr. Spitzer gifted Falada to Vera and who has a deep knowledge of the pony’s pedigree and symbolic significance.

Miss MacIntosh

  • Miss MacIntosh is her caretaker, described as an “old witch” by Vera, who holds a controlling influence over Vera’s life.

Falada

  • Vera’s old black pony, described with affection, mythic qualities, and a symbolic presence.
  • Falada is both a real animal and a figure of imagination and myth.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Falada

See: Horses and Carriages

  • Falada, the little black pony given to Vera by Mr. Spitzer, carries rich symbolic meaning that intertwines with Vera's inner world and her journey.
  • Falada is described as an old, tired, wind-broken pony, light as a spirit, with a sagging back, a scrappy mane, and a knotted tail. Despite his physical frailty and advanced age, Falada moves with grace and seems almost magical, responding to Vera's desires as if he were her own intelligence or head. The pony is said to be descended from a horse that inspired the jewel-studded horses of merry-go-rounds and carousels, connecting him to themes of childhood innocence and the cyclical nature of life and dreams.
  • Falada embodies a loyal, gentle companion who carries Vera through her internal struggles and the uncertain landscapes of her life. His responsiveness to her unspoken commands suggests an intimate connection between Vera's mind and her means of navigating reality and illusion.
  • Although Falada is physically frail, he continues to carry Vera with grace and resilience. This mirrors Vera's own vulnerability and strength as she faces the challenges of growing up and understanding complex realities.
  • Falada's origin as a model for carousel horses evokes childhood fantasy and cyclical repetition, resonating with Vera's oscillation between dream and reality. His presence blurs the lines between the tangible and the imaginary, reflecting the novel's themes of perception and illusion.
  • Given by Mr. Spitzer, Falada also symbolizes a connection to a past that is both real and mythical, and perhaps a hope for guidance and stability amid chaos. His lineage to historical and artistic forms evokes continuity and the search for meaning.
  • Falada is described as seeming like Vera's head or intelligence, suggesting that the pony is an extension or manifestation of her self-awareness and agency, her "headless rider" identity where the horse seems to guide the rider as much as the rider guides the horse.
  • Falada is a potent symbol of Vera's complex inner life: her innocence, her challenges, her search for identity and direction, and the interplay between dream and reality that defines her experience in the novel.

Miss MacIntosh’s Bedroom Scene

See: The House

  • The climax of the chapter is the silent, dark meeting with Miss MacIntosh, where Vera hesitates to reveal herself fully, fearing the consequences. This scene contrasts the fantastical ride with a stark moment of reality and fear.
  • The story is set at night, under the moonlight, creating a dreamlike atmosphere.
  • The surroundings include dark alleys, stunted trees, an old stable, gardens, fountains, statues, and a large, mysterious house with marble pillars, tapestries, and grand stairways.
  • The moonlit garden resembles a “garden of the blind,” and the house is described like a “water castle” with sea motifs and underwater imagery.
  • The atmosphere is eerie yet magical, with references to stars, fireflies, and phantoms.
  • Rich sensory descriptions evoke visual, tactile, and auditory experiences.

Research: Resources

  • Horses have often featured as a means of women' s liberation often in literature. “In Victorian novels’, as well as in the more modern genre of pony-girl books, it is often aboard a horse that girls find their wings, often proving some snot-nosed boy wrong in the process, or enabling them to escape various romantic dramas and find solace in the barn. Just like the pony-girl books, many of my participants’ life stories entwined horseyness with coming-of-age stories. Horses were the means through which many women first acted in a truly independent way, often pursuing equestrian endeavours despite/without their parents, or else, proudly taking up adult-like responsibilities in being the teacher, pilot, and carer of their ponies, rather than the one being looked after and told what to do.”
  • In the Brother’s Grimm fairy tale, The Goose Girl, a princess is sent off to marry with her magical horse named Falada who can talk and a maid. The maid tricks the princess out of her marriage and kills Falada so he won’t tell on her. The princess becomes the maid and in some stories has Falada’s head nailed above the entryway so she can see it every day. Eventually the King and Prince find out she is the true Princess and the maid is killed. In the stories, Falada is a white stallion. Falada means ‘spoken’ in Portuguese.
  • Queen Anne’s furniture style has curved shapes.
  • Mr. Spitzer gives a summary of Falada from the story The Goose Girl.
