Research notes: Chapter 57 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 57

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

"he was like his brother, and he was many selves who had been none or self-contained like a mystery"

Mr. Spitzer is constitutionally multiple. He is not a unified subject who happens to be confused about identity; he is the demonstration that unified selfhood is a fiction. The chapter sustains this across every page:

  • He carries two wrist watches: one for each self, one for each brother
  • He fears he has clothed the wrong man, that it is Peron who wears his suit, his hat, his identity
  • He sees Peron's blue ring on his own finger, Peron's checkered sleeve on his own arm
  • He is simultaneously the lawyer and the defendant, the tailor and the tailored, the living and the dead

The self here is not multiple in the fashionable sense of having many moods. It is multiple in the ontological sense: there is no original. The Spitzer who pays the bills and the Spitzer who is dead and the Spitzer who is Peron are not versions of one man; they are irresolvably co-present, and the chapter's famous run-on syntax performs this irresolvability. You cannot locate where one self ends and another begins, because the sentence will not let you stop.

The pillar says: this is not a problem to fix. The chapter agrees, Mr. Spitzer does not seek therapy or resolution. He seeks a tailor, someone who might finally fit him to a single body. But the tailor does not exist. The plural self has no tailor.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

"that things were not what they appeared to be in dreams but were the coverings, the masks for other things which were hidden—that the pawn shop was not a pawn shop...that the barber shop was not a barber shop"

This passage is almost a direct statement of the pillar. Reality is constructed, layered, masked. But the chapter goes further than simple skepticism: it does not posit a hidden real beneath the masks. The pawn shop is not a pawn shop, but neither is there a true thing it conceals. There is only masking all the way down.

The cave metaphor of the pillar, only a larger cave more carefully lit, is literalized in the library scene, where:

"the electric lights in the reading room had made it almost impossible to tell the difference between day and night"

The library, the institution of knowledge and record, is a place of perpetual artificial daylight, not illumination but the simulation of illumination. The light that erases shadow is not truth; it is the horror of a world where the difference between knowing and not-knowing has been abolished. Mr. Spitzer cannot find the tailor in the city directory not because the tailor is hidden but because the directory, like all systems of naming, cannot contain what is real.

Furthermore, the chapter's treatment of dreams and waking is not hierarchical. Dreams do not distort reality; waking life is equally unreliable:

"as no one could be sure, in a broken dream such as this which he had dreamed so often that it was almost continuous"

The question is not which state is real. The question dissolves.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This may be the pillar most fully embodied in the chapter. Grief here is not an event with a beginning and an end. It is the climate of Mr. Spitzer's entire existence.

His brother Peron has been dead long enough that:

  • The black funeral suit would be rags and tatters
  • The tailor's bill has been outstanding for so many years
  • The city directories of several years show no listing
  • Old Floradora girls write to Peron long years after it was possible to do so

And yet Mr. Spitzer still:

  • Receives the tailor's bill as a fresh insult
  • Turns to Peron's death notice in old newspapers as if to reassure himself
  • Dreams of paying the funeral costs again, of dying twice, being buried twice in separate graves
  • Carries Peron's two-headed penny as a good luck piece that brings only bad luck

Grief has not moved through stages. It has accumulated. The chapter's syntax enacts this accumulation: the sentences pile clause upon clause, each one adding another layer of loss, another unpaid debt, another letter unanswered, another funeral expense re-examined. The grief does not resolve at the end of the sentence because the sentence does not end.

The proposed rose at the chapter's close, a red rose and a white rose growing on a single bush, is not resolution. It is a monument to permanent irresolution: both brothers, living and dead, on one stem, a hybrid that cannot be disentangled.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

The chapter does not ask us to solve Mr. Spitzer's problem. It asks us to stay in the room with him. This is the ethical demand of the prose style itself.

Young's sentences are famous for their length and their refusal to conclude. To read this chapter is to practice the ethics of the witness: you cannot skim, you cannot rush to the point, you cannot extract a thesis. The prose punishes the reader who wants resolution. It rewards the reader who is willing to remain.

Mr. Spitzer himself is a kind of witness; he witnesses his own dissolution without flinching:

"He had seen a cloud like a great skull. He had seen the ribs. He had seen those phosphorous eyes...He had seen the knuckles."

The repetition of he had seen is not rhetorical decoration. It is the practice of attention. Each seeing is discrete, present, unrushed. The cloud becomes a skull, a hand, a bird, a rose, and Mr. Spitzer watches each transformation without trying to arrest it or explain it. This is the witness in action.

Even his visit to the library, ostensibly a search for information, becomes an act of witnessing. He does not find the tailor's address. He finds instead:

  • A man arguing with a dead man about the soul
  • A Confederate soldier reconstructing a war he could not have fought in
  • Obituary notices of people who died before he was born

He witnesses all of this without resolving it, without reporting it to authorities, without deciding what it means. He nods sympathetically as if a high wind were blowing. This is not passivity. It is the refusal to simplify, which the pillar identifies as the central ethical act.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

"he had changed trains innumerable times at innumerable way stations or perhaps always at the same Grand Central Station—perhaps always that he had got on to the same train from which he had got off—for perhaps the journey was infinite"

This is Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary made explicit. Mr. Spitzer returns and returns. He searches for the tailor, fails, returns to the search, fails again. He reads old newspapers, finds the same notices, reads them again. He answers no letters but reads them over and over. He consults city directories of several years.

The building (the attempt to settle the debt, to pay what is owed, to close the account) is never finished. But crucially, the chapter does not present this as failure. It presents it as the structure of his existence. The journey is infinite not because Mr. Spitzer is inadequate but because the destination does not exist. You cannot arrive at a tailor who is dead, or never existed, or who is yourself.

The recursive structure is also visible in the chapter's treatment of the funeral:

"to die twice—to be buried twice—in separate graves"

He has already paid. He must pay again. The settlement is never settled. This is not bureaucratic incompetence; it is the ontological condition of a self that cannot locate its own boundaries. You cannot close an account when you are not sure which account is yours.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

he "had been the hope of his buried youth—he would have finished, doubtless, if he had not died in the midst of his elegy which he could not end until his life ended"

The great musical masterpiece, the elegy that could not be finished because finishing it would require the completion of the life it elegized, is the purest image of this pillar in the chapter. The unfinished work is not failure. It is honesty. To finish the elegy would be to lie about the life.

This applies to Mr. Spitzer's legal correspondence, his unanswered letters, his unsettled accounts. He is not negligent. He is honest:

"old letters he had not yet had time to answer or was afraid to answer, for he was still thinking of what might be the correct reply"

The correct reply requires certainty he does not have. To send an incorrect reply would be a violence against the truth of his uncertainty. The ongoing sentence, the letter still being composed in his mind, is more honest than any reply he could send.

The chapter itself is formally committed to this ethic. Its sentences do not conclude. Its paragraphs do not arrive. The chapter ends not with resolution but with Mr. Spitzer watching clouds dissolve and wondering whether the rose should be red or white, knowing he is not particularly a horticulturist. The ending is not an ending. It is an ongoing.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Mr. Spitzer is the marginal figure par excellence:

  • He is the twin of the dead; neither fully alive nor fully dead
  • He is the lawyer with no clients mentioned, the musician who did not finish his work
  • He is not listed in the social registers of the living (his name appears only once or twice in old society pages )
  • He carries no receipts, no cancelled checks, no proper seals; none of the documentation that certifies existence in the official record

But the chapter populates his world with marginal figures: the old librarian who never sees a page of print, the Confederate soldier who could not have fought in the battle he describes, the dead man arguing about whether the soul survives death, the Floradora girls with their horse-hair wigs writing letters to the long-dead.

These figures are not aberrations. They are the evidence. The Confederate soldier's impossibility, he is twenty years old but claims to have been at Appomattox, does not disqualify him from the conversation. It makes him the most honest person in the room, because his impossibility is visible, while everyone else's impossibility is concealed.

"There were the impossibilities which Mr. Spitzer recognized in others."

The implication is devastating: Mr. Spitzer recognizes these impossibilities in others because he carries his own. The marginal figures are mirrors. They show the constructed nature of the normal.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

"He had gone, after several days, to the Public Library reading room and consulted the city directory—indeed, not easily daunted, had consulted the city directories of several years"

The chapter's relationship to time is entirely sedimentary. The past does not recede; it presses upward. Mr. Spitzer moves through a city that is temporally layered:

  • Old newspapers advertise bustles, corsets, sleigh bells, China schooners alongside current events
  • The streets contain buildings under construction alongside ancient architecture
  • He reads of passengers believed lost at sea and ghost ships alongside contemporary obituaries
  • His accumulated mail contains letters from horse traders who had not forgotten Peron

The city is not a contemporary space. It is a geological formation in which every era is simultaneously present. Mr. Spitzer navigates not through space but through time-layers, and he can never be sure which layer he is standing on.

The most striking image of sedimentary time is the description of the city as:

"vast continent as if it were something still undiscovered, with untraversable mountains and unexpected canyons and dark rivers and great waterfalls roaring from the sky"

The continent is still being discovered. The past has not been mapped. Mr. Spitzer considers employing an Indian guide, a figure from a prior layer of time, to navigate the present. The sediment is so thick that the present cannot be navigated without the past.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely aware of the limits and constructions of language. Mr. Spitzer's search for the tailor fails in part because language cannot locate what it names:

  • The tailor's name is not in the city directory
  • The street does not appear on any map
  • His address exists only in Mr. Spitzer's address book: a private language, a personal map that does not correspond to the public city

The chapter's own language is self-consciously constructed. Young's prose does not pretend to speak from nowhere. It speaks from inside Mr. Spitzer's consciousness, and it marks that location constantly through its syntax: the dashes, the qualifications, the perhaps and as if and he sometimes thought that stud every sentence.

"He should have been, as he realized now, the cartographer of his dreams—he should have marked the streets upon a map"

The cartographer is the one who makes language correspond to territory. Mr. Spitzer failed to be his own cartographer, failed to fix his dreams in language, and so the territory dissolved. But the chapter suggests that even if he had mapped his dreams, the map would not have matched the city. Because the city is also a construction, also a language, also a map of something else.

The two-headed penny is an image of language that cannot tell the truth: a coin with heads on both sides cannot function as a coin. It cannot distinguish between options. It is a language with only one word, which means it is no language at all. Mr. Spitzer carries it as a good luck piece. It brings him nothing but bad luck.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter is entirely immanent; it lives in the grey and the ordinary and the broken:

"the sky was streaked with the pastel colors of twilight, trembling green and gold and mauve and rose"

"clouds seeming to be made of thousands of twinkling moths like separate particles of iridescent, gleaming consciousness"

"Music had come from the walls, the clouds, the pavements"

There is no transcendent beyond in this chapter. The great tailor (who might be read as God, the demiurge, the creator) is dead, or mad, or never existed. There is no divine clothier who fits the world to meaning. There is only the world itself, fiery plumage and night with its clouds, and the fragments of a dream that has no dreamer.

But the sacred is not absent. It is relocated. It lives in:

  • The fog that is Mr. Spitzer's only tailor: the grey and diffuse, not the sharp and defined
  • The hurdy-gurdy music, the wooden merry-go-round horses neighing in a windy

garden

  • The cloud that becomes a skull, a hand, a bird, a rose
  • The Sphinx that settles on his coat sleeve

The Sphinx (ancient, riddling, immanent) lands not in a temple but on a man's coat sleeve in a Boston evening. This is where the sacred lives: not beyond the world but in it, in the most ordinary and most impossible moments simultaneously.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is perhaps the pillar that most completely organizes the chapter's deepest logic.

"he was many selves who had been none or self-contained like a mystery which is completed and laid away"

Mr. Spitzer is not a self moving through time. He is where time crosses itself. He is constituted by:

  • His brother Peron, who deposited identity into him before dying
  • The old Presidents and railroads and shipping companies whose seals would certify his debts
  • The Floradora girls, the horse traders, the hireling mourners: all the sediment of a world that preceded him
  • The great musical masterpiece that was begun before it could be finished

And he is constituting the ground for:

  • The letters he has not yet answered, which will become someone else's inheritance
  • The rose, red and white on a single bush, that will be named after both brothers, a monument to the crossing point itself
  • The ongoing elegy that someone else will have to finish

The crossing point is made most explicit in the image of Mr. Spitzer on the escalator:

"passing himself on an escalator going up as he was going down"

He meets himself coming and going. He is both the ancestor and the descendant, both the depositor and the inheritor. The obligation is not chosen; it is structural. He did not choose to be Peron's twin, did not choose to carry the debt, did not choose to be the one who lived. But he carries it anyway. The carrying continues.

The chapter ends with him watching clouds dissolve into twilight, wondering about a rose named after both brothers. He does not plant the rose. He does not finish the thought. He is the crossing point, not the conclusion, not the origin, but the place where what was deposited meets what has not yet been born.
