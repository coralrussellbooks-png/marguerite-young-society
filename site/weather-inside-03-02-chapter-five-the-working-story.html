Chapter Three: The Working Story

← Chapter Two: The Chorus Model · The Weather Inside · Chapter Four: You Were Told This Would End →

You have been living by a story your whole life.

Not one story. Several. The story about what you are capable of. The story about what you deserve. The story about what the world owes you and what you owe it. The story about why the hard things happened and what they mean and whether they are finished or still unfolding. The story about who you are when nobody is watching and who you have to be when they are.

You did not choose most of these stories.

They were handed to you. By the family you were born into, which had its own stories about what people like you did and did not do. By the culture you grew up inside, which had stories about what success looked like and what failure looked like and which one you were headed toward. By the institutions you moved through: the school, the church, the workplace, the community, each one with its own narrative about what a person was supposed to be and what happened to the people who weren't.

You absorbed these stories before you had the capacity to audit them.

And then you lived by them.

Some of them served you. Some of them cost you everything. Most of them did both at different times, which is what makes the auditing hard: the story that kept you moving through the worst years is often the same story that eventually drives you into the ground, the virtue and the damage being two expressions of the same narrative running past its usefulness.

This chapter is about learning to tell the difference.

Not by asking: is this story true?

By asking: is this story working?

Here is what the self-help industry will tell you about the stories you live by.

It will tell you to face reality. It will tell you that the healthy mind sees things as they actually are, that hope which exceeds the evidence is delusion, that the stories you tell to survive are coping mechanisms to be outgrown on the way to clear-eyed acceptance of what is.

It will tell you this with great confidence.

It is wrong.

Not entirely wrong. The story that imprisons you, that explains you to yourself in ways that make action impossible, that organizes your history into a narrative of inevitability: that story needs to be examined and probably replaced. We will get to that.

But the research does not support the premise that psychological health means accurate self-assessment. The research says something more interesting and more useful: people with slightly inflated beliefs about their own capabilities, people who bet on a possible future self rather than reporting on the current one, are measurably more resilient, more creative, more able to sustain effort in the face of failure than people with perfectly accurate self-appraisal.

The slightly too-optimistic story is not a symptom.

It is a survival technology.

The psychologist Shelley Taylor spent decades studying what she called positive illusions: the small, functional inaccuracies in self-perception that mentally healthy people carry almost universally. The belief that you have more control over outcomes than you actually do. The assessment of your own abilities as slightly better than neutral observers would rate them. The tendency to imagine the future as more positive than the base rate of your experience would predict.

These are not pathologies to be corrected.

They are the stories that keep you in motion.

The question Taylor's research raises, and that this chapter answers, is not whether you should live by story. You always live by story. Consciousness is narrative. The self is the story the mind tells about the mind. The question is whether you are the author of your stories or whether your stories are authoring you. Whether you chose them or inherited them. Whether they are expanding your capacity to act and love and endure or contracting it. Whether they are working for you or working for the systems that shaped them.

The Working Story is the story that expands you.

The story that has stopped working is the one that contracts you.

Learning to tell the difference is the practice of this chapter.

Marguerite Young understood that illusion was not the enemy of truth.

She understood it as the medium through which consciousness does its work. In her philosophy, the one this book has been building from, productive illusion is not self-deception. It is the recognition that reality is always constructed, always multiple, always the consequence of the stories the mind uses to organize experience into something livable. The question is never whether you are constructing reality. You are always constructing it. The question is whether you are doing it consciously or having it done for you.

Young spent eighteen years writing a novel about a woman searching for the one real thing in a world made entirely of stories. What she found, and what the novel enacts rather than argues, is that the search itself is the truth. Not the destination. The searching. The holding of the story that the real thing exists even when every institution and every person and every piece of available evidence suggests it does not.

The narrative therapists Michael White and David Epston arrived at the same place from the clinical direction. Their framework, narrative therapy, begins with the recognition that the self is not a fixed thing to be diagnosed and treated. The self is a story being written in real time, shaped by the dominant narratives of the culture and the family and the institution, and the work of therapy is not to uncover the true self beneath the stories but to help the person become the author of their own narrative rather than a character in someone else's.

Not: what is the truth about you?

But: who is writing your story and is that who you want writing it?

This is the Working Story question.

Not is this true. Is this working. Is this story expanding me or contracting me. Is it mine or did I inherit it. Is it serving my life or serving the system that handed it to me.

Here is what the story that stops working looks like.

You were organized.

You kept things in order because order was the container that made everything else possible. The lists. The systems. The specific discipline of knowing where everything was and what needed to happen next and moving through the day with the efficiency of someone who had learned early that if you did not manage things carefully they would not be managed at all.

You worked hard. This was not in question. The work ethic being so deeply built into the operating system that you could not have identified it as a choice if someone had asked you. It was simply what you did. You worked until the work was done and then you found the next work because the alternative, the stopping, the rest, felt like a different kind of falling.

You knew the rules. And you operated within them with the specific fluency of someone who had learned that the rules were the container and the container was what made the bending possible. You could push the limits because you understood the limits. You could be trusted with more because you had demonstrated that you could be trusted with what you already had.

And you pushed through the pain.

Not the dramatic pain. The daily accumulation of it. The tiredness that was not addressed because there was no time. The body's requests for rest that were noted and deferred because the list was not finished and the list was never finished and finishing the list was the story and the story was that if you finished the list you would be safe and if you were safe you could rest and the rest was always one list away.

This story looked like virtue from the outside.

From the inside it was a managed performance of a self that did not need anything, that could sustain any load, that was valuable precisely because it was reliable and low-maintenance and never stopped.

The body stopped.

Not dramatically. In the way that systems fail when they have been running past capacity for long enough: the cumulative cost becoming visible all at once, the performance that had been sustained through will and organization and pain suppression finally exceeding the capacity of the instrument being pushed.

And then the family intervened.

This is the detail that matters most and that the story of self-sufficiency had made almost impossible: the people who loved you saw what the story would not let you see and they made you stop. They overrode the performance. They refused to let the story continue at the cost it was charging.

The stopping was not failure.

The stopping was the first true thing in a long time.

Because what the stopping revealed was that the story had never been about your wellbeing. It had been about your legibility. The organized, hardworking, rule-following, pain-suppressing self was the self that the systems could use: productive, reliable, manageable, asking nothing and delivering everything. The story was working for the systems. It was costing you the body it was running on.

A story that costs you your body has stopped working.

It may have worked once. It may have been the story that got you through the years when the alternative was worse. The pushing through being the form of the survival when survival was the only available goal. But survival stories have expiration dates. The story that keeps you alive in the hard years is not always the story that lets you live in the years after.

When the story stops working, the body knows first.

Listen to the body.

Here is what the working story looks like.

You were always searching.

Not casually. With the specific thoroughness of someone who finishes what they start, who reads to the end, who does not put a thing down until it has given up what it has to give. You moved through traditions and communities and frameworks and geographies with the hunger of someone looking for the place that was large enough, the container that could hold the full complexity of what you were without requiring you to leave parts of yourself at the door.

You found the ceiling every time.

Not immediately. You would enter a new tradition, a new community, a new framework, and for a time it would seem like the thing. The language would be close to right. The people would be moving toward something that felt like what you were moving toward. And then the center would appear: the doctrine, the limit, the specific place where the tradition drew its line and said: to stay here you must also believe this smaller version of things.

You could not believe the smaller version.

Not because you were difficult. Because you could picture something larger and the picturing was real and you had learned to trust the picture even when you could not find the place it described.

So you left.

Every time.

And you kept the story.

The story being: the thing that is large enough to hold me exists. I have not found it yet. I will keep looking.

This story was not verified by the evidence. The evidence was a long record of institutions that had eventually shown you their ceiling. The accurate conclusion from that evidence would have been: this thing does not exist, stop looking, make peace with the largest available container even if it does not fully fit.

You did not make that conclusion.

Because the story was working.

It was keeping you in motion toward something real that you could picture even when you could not find it. It was the bet on the future rather than the report on the present. It was the positive illusion functioning exactly as the research describes: not accurate, not verifiable, and absolutely necessary.

And then Marguerite Young.

Not an institution. Not a community with a center that required you to be smaller. A philosophy embedded in fiction that was itself as large as consciousness, as multiple as the self actually is, as resistant to the category as the thing you had been picturing all along.

You recognized it before you understood it.

In the body first.

The story had been true the whole time.

You had just been looking in the wrong places for the right thing.

The searching was not wasted. Every place you left was the story testing the institution and finding it insufficient and moving on intact. You were not lost in those years. You were in the longest possible audition for the framework that was actually large enough.

The working story got you there.

The difference between the two stories is not that one was true and one was false.

Both were false in the strict sense. The self-sufficiency story was not true: you needed rest and you needed other people and the body was not an instrument to be driven past its limits. The searching story was not true: the thing large enough did not yet exist in a form you could find, and the evidence for its existence was entirely internal.

The difference is what each story did with you.

The self-sufficiency story contracted you. It made you smaller over time: more managed, more isolated, more dependent on the performance of not needing anything. It was working for the systems that valued your productivity and your reliability and your low-maintenance operation. It was costing you the life it was supposed to be protecting.

The searching story expanded you. It kept you moving, kept you reading to the end, kept you leaving the insufficient places instead of making yourself smaller to fit them. It was working for you. It was expensive in its own ways: the leaving costs something, the not-staying costs something, the carrying of an unverified story across decades of institutional failure costs something. But the cost was generative. It was building something rather than depleting something.

This is the audit.

Not: is this story true?

But: what is this story doing with me?

Is it making me larger or smaller?

Is it serving my life or serving the systems that handed it to me?

Is it a story I chose or a story I inherited and never examined?

Is it still working or has it passed its expiration date?

These are the questions of the Working Story practice.

Not once. Repeatedly. Because the story that worked last year may not be working this year. Because the stories reconstitute themselves in new forms and the new forms require new audits. Because consciousness is narrative and the narrative is always in motion and the work is not to find the final true story but to stay in the authoring position: the one who is writing rather than the one being written.

You are the author.

You have always been the author.

The question is whether you know it.

The Science of the Story You Need

William James said the self was a story before anyone called it that.

He said it in the language available to him in 1890, which was the language of philosophy and early psychology, but the argument was the same one the narrative therapists would make a hundred years later and the same one the positive illusions researchers would confirm in the laboratory: that consciousness is not a mirror held up to reality. It is a construction. The mind does not record experience. It narrates it. The self is not the thing having the experience. The self is the story the mind tells about the thing having the experience.

This is not a problem to be solved.

It is the condition of being conscious.

The question is not whether you are narrating. You are always narrating. The question is whether you are doing it on purpose.

Shelley Taylor spent her career asking what happens when people's stories about themselves are slightly better than the evidence supports. The answer, confirmed across decades of research, was counterintuitive enough that it took years for the field to accept it: the slightly inflated story produces better outcomes than the accurate one. People who believe they have more control than they do are more resilient in crisis. People who assess their own abilities as slightly above average are more persistent in the face of failure. People who imagine the future as more positive than the past predicts are more likely to take the actions that make the positive future possible.

The positive illusion is not wishful thinking.

It is the story that keeps you in motion toward the thing you cannot yet verify.

Taylor was careful about the limits. The story has to be slightly inflated, not wildly disconnected from reality. The person who believes they can fly is not demonstrating positive illusion. They are demonstrating a break from reality that will cost them. The person who believes they can do the thing they have not yet done, who treats the possible future self as a present fact worth acting toward: that person is using the working story correctly.

The line between the productive illusion and the dangerous one is the line between expansion and contraction. The story that keeps you moving toward more of what you actually are is the working story. The story that keeps you moving away from what you actually need is the one that has stopped working, even if it looks like virtue from the outside.

Michael White and David Epston arrived at the same territory from the clinical direction.

They were family therapists in Australia and New Zealand in the 1980s who noticed that the dominant therapeutic model, find the pathology, treat the pathology, restore the baseline, was missing something fundamental. The people they worked with were not broken mechanisms to be repaired. They were people living inside stories that had been written for them by their families, their cultures, their traumas, their institutions, and that had become so naturalized they were invisible. The story was not something the person had. The story was something the person was inside of, so completely that they could not see it as a story at all. It simply was reality.

White and Epston called their framework narrative therapy.

The work was not to find the true self beneath the story. It was to help the person see the story as a story: authored, constructed, changeable. To find what they called the unique outcomes, the moments when the person had acted outside the dominant narrative, when the story that imprisoned them had briefly failed to determine their behavior. And to use those moments as the material for a new story: one the person was writing rather than one being written about them.

Not: what is wrong with you?

But: who wrote this story about you and is that who you want writing it?

The externalizing of the problem was White and Epston's most radical move. In narrative therapy the problem is not inside the person. The problem is the story. The person and the story are separate. The person can have a relationship with the story, can examine it, can choose to keep it or replace it, can author a different version. The person is not the diagnosis. The person is the author who has been living as a character in someone else's narrative.

You are not the story of the self-sufficient person who never needs anything.

You are the person who was handed that story and lived inside it until the body said no.

Those are different things.

The first makes the story your identity. The second makes you the author who can choose differently.

Terror management theory adds one more layer that the self-help industry almost never names because it is too large and too uncomfortable to be easily packaged.

Jeff Greenberg, Shelley Solomon, and Tom Pyszczynski spent decades studying what happens to human behavior when people are reminded of their own mortality. What they found was that the awareness of death, the knowledge that we are finite and that the finitude is coming and that we cannot know when, produces an anxiety so fundamental that human culture is largely organized around managing it. The stories we live by, the belief systems, the institutions, the ideologies, the sense that our lives have meaning and continuity and value beyond our individual existence: these are not incidental features of human culture. They are the terror management system. The stories that make life livable in the face of the knowledge that it ends.

This is not an argument for nihilism.

It is an argument for taking your stories seriously.

The stories you live by are doing more work than you know. They are not just organizing your daily behavior. They are holding the existential weight of being a conscious creature who knows it will die. The story that your life has meaning, that the work you are doing matters, that the people you love will continue after you are gone, that something of what you built will persist: these stories are the architecture of the livable life.

When a story stops working it is not a small thing.

It is the architecture shifting.

Which is why the body responds so strongly. Why the crash, when it comes, comes in the body first. Why the family has to intervene. Why the searching, when it finally finds its ground, produces recognition rather than just satisfaction. The working story is not a preference. It is the load-bearing structure of the self.

Handle it accordingly.

Not with credulity. With the specific attention of someone who understands that the stories they live by are doing the deepest work of their life and deserve to be examined with the same care you would bring to anything that load-bearing.

Is it working?

Is it yours?

Is it expanding you or contracting you?

These are not casual questions.

They are the most important questions available to you about the life you are living right now.

The Working Story

Here is the practice.

You already know how to audit a story. You have been doing it your whole life, every time you left a place that required you to be smaller, every time you pushed through the pain until the body said enough, every time you picked up a book and read it to the end looking for the thing that was large enough. You were auditing. You did not have a framework for it. You had the instinct.

The Working Story framework makes the instinct explicit.

It gives you three questions. Not as a checklist. As a practice of returning. Because the audit is not a single event. The story that was working last year may not be working this year. The story that cost you everything in your forties may have been the story that kept you alive in your twenties. The questions are not asked once and answered. They are asked repeatedly, at the hinges of the life, in the moments when something feels wrong in a way you cannot yet name, in the quiet after the crash, in the recognition that something has shifted and the old story no longer fits the new ground.

The three questions are these.

Is this story expanding me or contracting me?

The expanding story makes you larger over time. It keeps you in motion. It opens doors rather than closing them. It produces the specific quality of aliveness that comes from moving toward more of what you actually are rather than away from what you actually need. The expanding story is not always comfortable. The searching story was not comfortable. It cost something every time you left. But the cost was generative. It was building the life rather than depleting it.

The contracting story makes you smaller over time. It narrows the range of what you allow yourself to need and feel and ask for. It produces the specific quality of managed diminishment: the life getting quieter and more controlled and more defended, the self becoming the performance of the self, the body carrying the cost in the places the story will not let you look.

The contraction is often invisible from inside the story.

It looks like discipline. Like strength. Like the virtue of the person who never complains and always delivers and asks for nothing.

The body knows the difference.

The body is always the first reader of the story's actual cost.

Is this story mine or did I inherit it?

Most of the stories you live by were not chosen. They were handed to you before you had the capacity to examine them. The family story about what people like you did and did not do. The cultural story about what success looked like and which direction your life was supposed to move. The institutional story about what a good student was, what a good worker was, what a good person was, and how far you were from any of those definitions.

These stories are not yours simply because you have been living by them.

Inheritance is not ownership.

The question is not whether the inherited story is true. The question is whether you have examined it. Whether you have held it up to the light of your actual experience and asked: does this fit? Is this expanding me or contracting me? Did the person who handed me this story hand it to me for my benefit or for theirs?

Some inherited stories are worth keeping.

The story that the thing large enough exists: that one was worth keeping across decades of evidence that it didn't. The story that finishing the work matters, that reading to the end produces something that skimming does not: that one was worth keeping. The inherited stories that expand you are yours to claim. The ones that contract you are yours to put down.

You are allowed to put them down.

You do not owe the story your body.

Is this story working for me or for the systems that handed it to me?

This is the hardest question and the most important one.

Because the stories that are most deeply built into the operating system, the ones that feel most like identity rather than narrative, are often the ones that were designed to serve the systems rather than the self. The self-sufficiency story. The productivity story. The story that your value is your output and your output must be consistent and your consistency must be legible to the people and institutions that depend on it.

These stories feel like virtue because they were handed to you as virtue. The culture rewards them. The workplace rewards them. The family system rewards them. The mold rewards them especially: the person who keeps going, who does not complain, who manages everything and needs nothing, who is valuable because they are reliable and low-maintenance and never stop.

The reward is real.

The cost is also real.

And the cost falls entirely on you while the reward is distributed to the systems that benefit from your reliable, low-maintenance, never-stopping operation.

The working story serves your life.

The story that has stopped working serves the systems at the expense of your life.

The audit question is: who benefits when I live by this story?

If the answer is primarily the institution, the employer, the family system, the cultural norm, and only secondarily or not at all you: the story has stopped working.

It may have worked once. The story that made you legible and reliable and low-maintenance may have been the story that got you through the years when legibility was the price of survival. But survival stories have expiration dates. The story that kept you safe in the hard years is not automatically the story that lets you live in the years after.

One more thing about the Working Story that the framework requires you to hold.

The story you are telling right now, the one you are building from the work of these chapters, the one that says you are plural and your grief is climate and your nonlinear life is a structure rather than a failure: this story is also a working story. It is not the final truth. It is the story that is working right now, in this season, for this version of you.

Hold it accordingly.

Not with the grip of someone who has finally found the right answer after a long search and cannot afford to lose it.

With the open hand of someone who has learned that the story serves the life and the life will eventually require a new story and the new story is not a betrayal of this one but the next layer on the reef.

The story is not the destination.

The authoring is the destination.

You are not looking for the story that ends the search.

You are becoming the person who knows how to keep writing.

Practice: The Working Story

One.

Think of a story you are currently living by. Not a belief exactly. A narrative: the story about what you are capable of, or what you deserve, or what the work you are doing is for, or what the hard thing that happened means.

Write it down in one paragraph. Not the analyzed version. The raw version. The story as you actually tell it to yourself at two in the morning or in the car alone or in the moment before you walk into the difficult room.

Now ask: is this story expanding me or contracting me? Is it making me larger over time or smaller? Do not answer quickly. Sit with it. The body knows before the mind does. What does the body say?

Two.

Name a story you inherited.

Not a value. A story. The narrative that was handed to you by your family or your culture or the institution that shaped you most completely. The one that feels so much like identity that you have never examined it as a story at all.

Write down where it came from. Who handed it to you. What they needed you to believe in order for their world to work.

Now ask: is this story mine? Did I choose it or did I absorb it before I had the capacity to choose? Write one honest sentence about what it would cost to put it down. Write one honest sentence about what it is already costing you to keep carrying it.

Three.

Identify a story that stopped working.

Not the dramatic collapse. The quiet version: the story that was serving you in one season of your life and that you kept carrying into the next season after it had passed its usefulness. The story that looked like virtue from the outside while it was costing you something essential from the inside.

Ask: who was this story working for? Write down the honest answer even if the honest answer is uncomfortable. Even if it names a system or a person or an institution you have complicated feelings about.

Then write this: I carried this story as long as it was useful. It is not useful anymore. I am putting it down.

You do not have to mean it completely yet.

Write it anyway.

The writing is the beginning of the meaning it.

Coda: The Picture That Was Real

You carried a picture for a long time.

Not a literal image. The internal picture of the thing that was large enough: the framework, the community, the understanding of consciousness that could hold the full complexity of what you were without requiring you to leave parts of yourself outside. You carried it through every institution that showed you its ceiling. Through every tradition that eventually revealed its center and its demand for the limited version. Through every room that was close but not quite right, that had the language but not the largeness, that welcomed you until you exceeded its tolerance and then quietly showed you the door.

You kept the picture.

Not because you had evidence for it.

Because you could see it.

And the seeing was enough to keep you moving even when the evidence said stop. Even when the record of institutional failures was long enough that the reasonable conclusion would have been: this thing does not exist, make peace with the largest available container, stop carrying a picture of something you cannot find.

You did not make that conclusion.

This is the working story in its purest form.

Not the story that the evidence supports. The story that the picture requires. The bet on the possible rather than the report on the probable. The slightly inflated belief that kept you in motion toward something real that you could see even when you could not touch it.

The searching was not wasted.

Every place you left was not a failure. It was the picture being held up against the institution and the institution being found insufficient and the picture surviving intact. You were not lost in those years. You were in the longest possible audition for the thing you had been picturing. The picture was the working story. The working story was doing its work.

And then you found the place that was large enough.

Not an institution. Not a community with a center that required you to be smaller. A novel. Twelve hundred pages written by a woman who understood that the thing large enough to hold consciousness could not be smaller than consciousness itself. A philosophy embedded in fiction that was itself plural, recursive, excessive, resistant to every category the market offered it. A framework that had been there the whole time, waiting for the reader who would read it to the end.

You read it to the end.

Of course you did.

You had been reading to the end your whole life because you were looking for something and you had learned that the something was only available to the person who stayed with the text past the point where the insufficient reader turned back. The finishing being the form of the searching. The searching being the form of the working story. The working story being the thing that brought you here.

Here being: this chapter. This book. This moment of understanding that the picture you carried was not a delusion but a navigation system. That the story you could not verify was not a weakness but the most sophisticated survival technology available to a person whose plurality exceeded every institution they entered.

The picture was real.

It was always real.

You were simply looking for the place that matched it.

Now you have a different relationship with the looking.

Not the end of it. The plural self does not arrive at a destination and stop. The working story does not find its verification and retire. The searching continues because consciousness continues and consciousness is always larger than the current framework and the current framework is always, eventually, showing you its ceiling.

But now you know what the ceiling means.

It means the story is still working. It means the picture is still larger than the place. It means you are not lost and you are not failing and you are not the problem.

You are the reader who reads to the end.

Keep reading.

The next large thing is already waiting for you in a book you have not opened yet.
