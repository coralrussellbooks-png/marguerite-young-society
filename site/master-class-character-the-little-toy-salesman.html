The Little Toy Salesman: The Lone Survivor Who Tipped His Hat

← The Chicago Detective · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Esther Longtree's Parents →

Characters: Esther Longtree · Mr. Weed · The Chicago Detective

Of all the salesmen who passed through Esther Longtree's counter, she chose this one to be "the legal father" of the child she was carrying, and he is the strangest choice in the chapter, because he is not a father and not even a man, but an old baby. The little toy salesman "drank his milk from a baby bottle, and she had to wipe his chin." He "could not tie his own shoe-laces." He is bandy-legged, pot-bellied, goggle-eyed, "in his second childhood or his first," who could tell, wearing "a dead rosebud in his coat lapel and a black mourning band around his sleeve" for a dead mother, and he sells "toys to bereaved parents." He never had a childhood, was "always put to sleep in a dresser drawer or in a dark, cold place," and wants now only "the inside of a good woman," a home, to be rocked to sleep in her arms. He is the perpetual infant, the man who never got born into a life, and Esther, mother of the unborn, takes him for a husband.

He is also, like so many in this book, a man who does not see the woman in front of him. He "did not see her, so obvious, the stain of milk, her swelling breasts," and did not notice she was always pregnant. He came in "quack-quacking with his duck-billed mouth," blowing a tin horn, bouncing a rubber ball, and looked straight past the enormous fact of her. He is another of the book's anti-witnesses, in the softest and most pitiable key, too lost in his own infancy to register anyone else's grief.

And then the thing that makes him unforgivable. The little toy salesman is a murderer, and his murder is that he lived. He was the sole survivor of a shipwreck. In a great storm at sea "all others had drowned, the captain, the crew, the women, the children," and he alone "had been saved," floating "safe in a rubber life boat, gently rocking in the vasty deep, tipping his straw hat to women and children whose faces and hands crowded the sea." He offered his seat to no one. "He had not known how to save anyone, not even a pregnant woman, not even a dove." He is "the lone eye witness," the one who saw the whole catastrophe and could act on none of it, who tipped his hat, smiling, "to those who passed by, for they were far away, and he was safe."

This is the deepest cut of the philosophy the book keeps testing, the demand that a person stay in the room with what is actually there and not look away. The little toy salesman witnessed the drowning of the world and looked past it. He would have "looked beyond" Esther even in the lifeboat. His survival is itself the not-looking, the hat tipped to the dying as they went down. Esther's verdict comes in two beats, and both are true. First, she cannot forgive him "that he had not drowned with the others." Second, and finally, "he should be pitied that he lived." The man who survived by not seeing is both the guilty one and the most piteous, "a cold pigeon," an old baby who lost his tear ducts in the desert and his taste and his reason in the storm and cannot even weep for what he failed to save.

The turn for a writer is one of the hardest in the whole set, because the little toy salesman is the maker who survives his own material. He is the one who floats safe in the boat while the people go down, who witnesses the entire storm and renders it so vividly that "she could imagine it, the tumult of earth and heaven," and saved no one, offered his seat to no one, tipped his hat as the faces passed. He makes small consoling objects, "toys to bereaved parents," which is a fair description of a certain kind of art, the little comfort sold to the grieving. And he does not see the living woman across the counter because he is too busy being his own wound. Set him beside Mr. Weed, who reduces the living to weights, and beside the Chicago detective, who sees a corpse where a woman is sleeping. The toy salesman is the survivor whose survival is the crime, the witness whose witnessing saved nobody, the writer safe in his rubber boat among the drowned. The book gives him Esther's double sentence and lets it stand: he cannot be forgiven that he lived, and he must be pitied that he lived, and there is no third thing to say.
