Definition from research

Every piece of writing is made partly out of earlier writing. A novel borrows words, names, stories, and shapes that its readers already carry, and much of its meaning comes from what those borrowings bring with them. The study of that borrowing is called intertextuality. This section explains the few terms the volume uses, each tied to an example from Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.

Intertextuality

Julia Kristeva coined the word in her 1966 essay "Word, Dialogue and Novel." She described any text as "a mosaic of quotations." By that she meant that a text is built from, and changes, the texts that came before it.

Her idea joined two earlier lines of thought.

  • Saussure. Ferdinand de Saussure, the Swiss linguist, showed that words do not get their meaning from a direct link to things. They get it from their place in a system of other words, each defined by how it differs from the rest.

  • Bakhtin. Mikhail Bakhtin, the Russian critic, showed that a novel is full of many voices in conversation. Every word carries traces of the other places and speakers it has passed through. His name for this conversation was dialogism, and his name for the many kinds of speech inside a single novel was heteroglossia.

Kristeva put the two together. If words mean by their relation to other words, and every word carries its past with it, then a whole text means by its relation to other texts. Meaning does not pass straight from writer to reader. It passes through everything both of them have read.

Young's novel shows this on its first page. Vera sees a stranger driving a bus and reaches for Cervantes to explain him. The bus-driver never becomes Don Quixote, but the name changes how the reader sees him.

Hypotext and Hypertext

The French critic Gérard Genette gave two plain names to the parties in this exchange.

  • The hypotext is the earlier work.

  • The hypertext is the later work that draws on it and transforms it.

John Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress is a hypotext. Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is a hypertext that takes Bunyan's pilgrim and changes where the road leads. This volume uses the two terms sparingly. They are handy when a passage needs to say which book is doing the borrowing.

Three Kinds of Reference

Not every borrowing matters equally. The volume sorts each reference into one of three categories. The category depends on how firmly the reference is anchored in the novel and how much a reader loses by missing it.

Obligatory. The novel builds on the earlier text, and a reader who misses the link misses part of the meaning. Pilgrim's Progress is the clearest case. It is Miss MacIntosh's chosen guide through life. Vera later finds it on the beach, "her Pilgrim's Progress water-logged with marks of sea shells on its pages" (chapter 3). Near the end, Miss MacIntosh herself calls the journey "a Pilgrim's Progress from nowhere to nowhere" (chapter 63). Without Bunyan's straight road to the Celestial City, that phrase loses its weight.

Optional. The link is in the text and enriches the reading, but the passage still works without it. Vera's pony is named Falada (chapter 16). In the Grimm tale "The Goose Girl," Falada is the horse whose severed head, nailed above a city gate, goes on speaking the truth about a princess whose place has been stolen. A reader who knows the tale hears in the pony's name a witness who keeps telling the truth after death. A reader who does not still follows Vera's ride without trouble.

Young never cites them by title, but the novel names a figure, a phrase, or an idea that points straight to each one.

Friedrich Nietzsche's Thus Spoke Zarathustra is never named, but its central teaching is. Grieving his twin brother, Mr. Spitzer asks, "Should not eternal recurrence be the one law of life" (chapter 42). Nietzsche's other great image, the abyss, appears in thirty-three chapters of the novel and gathers most thickly around the Spitzer brothers. Instead of staring into the abyss, Mr. Spitzer prods the floor with his cane because the abyss is underneath. Zarathustra calls man "a rope over an abyss." Mr. Spitzer pictures his brother's last hours as a long hovering at that edge: "not suddenly had he come to the great abyss, the yawning chasm" (chapter 58). The dwarf in Young’s novel is given a rose in his lapel and a turret on its head.

The ancient Egyptian Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor tells of a sailor who is the lone survivor of a wreck and who lives on among the memories of the drowned. Young brings its figure into Catherine's bedroom as "a drowned Egyptian sailor, gold-turbaned" (chapter 2). His "hieroglyphic language" returns in chapter 40, and in chapter 54 "Some dead Egyptian sailors were unwinding their winding sheets." The tale's pattern returns in the toy salesman, the only survivor of his own wreck.

Thomas Paine's Common Sense lends its title to Miss MacIntosh's creed. The phrase "common sense" appears in eighteen chapters, and she declares, "Common sense is the finest sense" (chapter 1). Her version belongs to the American line that runs from Paine's appeal to plain reason to the pragmatism of William James, whose Pragmatism (1907) gives a whole lecture to "Pragmatism and Common Sense." Like the pragmatists, Miss MacIntosh judges ideas by what they do. She wants Vera to "lead a useful life" and to face "the clear reality of simple things" (chapter 1).

The pastoral poems form a chain of answers. Christopher Marlowe's shepherd invites his love, "Come live with me and be my love." Sir Walter Raleigh's nymph answers that such promises fade. Centuries later William Carlos Williams answers both in "Raleigh Was Right." Young brings Raleigh into Catherine's bedroom, where he "asked that he be addressed by his intimate name, Ocean or Water, for he had crossed many waters" (chapter 11). "Water" was Queen Elizabeth's pet name for him. "Ocean" is the name he takes in his unfinished poem of love and longing to the queen, "The Ocean to Cynthia." The novel's courtships answer one another in the same way, as invitation and sober reply.

Plato's Symposium is not named, but Plato is. Catherine's guests include "Platonists of the period of the intellectual flowering of Athens" (chapter 2), and one of her rooms is "already occupied by Plato's boat" (chapter 24). Her house of voices talking through the night about love and longing is its own banquet of speeches.

Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness is the faintest of these links. The words "heart of darkness" occur as an ordinary phrase in chapters 29, 44, 45, and 53. That is enough to call the echo possible, though not certain.

Accidental. The connection lives in the reader's mind, with no anchor in the text. Max Weber's The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism. Weber is never cited, and the word "Protestant" appears only twice, in passing (chapters 3 and 9). Readers still reach for him because Miss MacIntosh's plain, hard-working, unadorned faith fits his description so closely. That connection is real for those readers and worth exploring, but it belongs to them, not to Young, and the volume labels it that way.

Referential and Typological Borrowing

Borrowings also differ in kind.

Referential borrowing takes a fragment: a name, a phrase, a line. Tantalus is a referential borrowing. Vera names him outright: "All my life I had been reaching for the tangible, and it had evaded me, much like the myth of Tantalus" (chapter 1).

Typological borrowing takes a shape: a pattern or structure carried over from earlier writing, often with no name attached. Young's deepest debts are often of this kind.

  • The pilgrimage. The novel moves like a pilgrimage whose destination keeps receding.

  • The catalog. It gathers material the way an encyclopedia or a litany does. Catherine's opium reveries run in alphabetical order, as when "The P's were crowding upon her" (chapter 9).

The catalog and the pilgrimage are old forms. Young borrows them as surely as she borrows Tantalus, though she never names them.

Iterability and Presupposition

Two last terms describe how borrowing works over time.

Iterability is the capacity of a fragment to be repeated in a new setting and take on new meaning there. Young relies on it constantly. The genealogies of Israel appear in the novel as a schoolroom drill, a barroom lament, and a cry of personal loss. The words stay the same while the speaker and the moment change.

Presupposition is what a text assumes its reader already knows. Young assumes a great deal: the Bible, Mother Goose, Greek myth, the English poets, and American history. A reader of 1965 would have met most of it in school, in church, or at bedtime. Many readers today have not. The names of Issachar's sons, the Moody Bible, and a nursery rhyme carved into a roadside building no longer arrive with the weight Young expected them to carry. That gap between what the novel presupposes and what readers now bring is the main reason a guide like this one is needed.

How the Categories Are Marked

This volume does not use tables to sort its references. Categories are named in the prose where they matter.

  • Each essay on a major source text in Section 6 opens with a sentence stating its category: obligatory, optional, or accidental. A reader knows from the start how firmly that text is anchored in the novel.

  • Elsewhere, when a reference's category bears on the argument, the sentence that introduces it says so.

  • Reader connections are always identified as reader connections, so they are never mistaken for Young's own references.
