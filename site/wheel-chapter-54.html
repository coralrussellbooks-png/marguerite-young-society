Research notes: Chapter 54 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 54

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The chapter's central dramatic tension is not between Mr. Spitzer and the world, but between Mr. Spitzer and himself, or rather, between the multiple selves that occupy his single body.

"On one street, he was Peron. On another street, he was Joachim, the unchanging one."

This is not metaphor. The text presents it as ontological fact. Mr. Spitzer does not remember his brother or miss his brother; he becomes his brother, involuntarily, at street corners, in restaurants, when someone greets him with recognition. The plurality is not psychological disorder in the clinical sense; it is the structural condition of a self that has been constituted partly by another self.

The passport image crystallizes this:

He would "look at the passport picture which was taken in his youth but was Peron’s picture and was not his own, though serving equally well for him—he had never had his picture taken."

The document that certifies identity, the passport, already belongs to someone else. Identity as legal fiction, as administrative construction, collapses here. The plural self is not a problem to fix; it is what Mr. Spitzer is. He carries Peron's ticket stubs, Peron's pawn tickets, Peron's coin purse. He is haunted not by a ghost but by a co-tenant of his own body.

The text also presents the plurality as irresolvable. When a great physician offers to test his heart:

"Perhaps he would have found that Mr. Spitzer's was the beating heart, and perhaps he would have found that his was the resting heart... or perhaps, and this was what Mr. Spitzer had so greatly feared, he would have heard the beatings of two hearts."

Two hearts. The plural self, confirmed medically, refused medically. He declines the test precisely because resolution, knowing which heart beats, would destroy the ambiguity that constitutes him.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter does not present a real Boston against which Mr. Spitzer's hallucinations are measured. It presents only the hallucination, with no stable referent outside it.

"For all was so much like my mother's wilderness of opium dreams that Mr. Spitzer... was less and less able to distinguish between what my mother imagined and a great city where he walked through constantly changing streets, here in this world where everything which was not was and everything which was was not."

This is the Pillar's core claim: reality is not what we find; it is what we make. The city Mr. Spitzer walks through is assembled from sediment, from Peron's world, from the opium lady's dreams, from historical Boston, from the Boston of the dead. The Flatiron Building appears in the Boston fog. The old Mather's hill coexists with President Wilson's death announcement. St. Sophia's Dome rises over a blind alley.

The chapter refuses to mark these as errors. They are presented in the same syntactic register as the grasshopper weathervane on Faneuil Hall, which is a real landmark. The text will not tell you which is real because the distinction is itself the illusion.

The penny arcade moment is particularly precise:

"Often, he had done nothing but stop to inquire into the unfamiliar pleasures of a penny arcade — when suddenly, sometimes without his knowledge, he had changed, imperceptibly changed, another personality taking over his life."

The trigger for ontological shift is trivial: a penny arcade, a gramophone honking, a wind against his face. This is not dramatic transformation. It is the ordinary porousness of the constructed self, leaking into adjacent constructions without warning.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This is perhaps the Pillar most completely embodied by the chapter. Grief here is not a response to a specific loss. It is the climate.

"His sorrow was great enough to admit a city gate, great as a city with this long traffic roar. Nothing should diminish it. All things should increase it, even as it diminished."

Note the paradox: all things increase it, even as it diminishes. This is not the arc of mourning toward resolution. This is grief as self-sustaining atmospheric condition: the weather of a world where everything is always already changing.

The chapter makes this explicit through the structure of Mr. Spitzer's daily life. Every morning he searches through Peron's belongings (ticket stubs, pawn tickets, gate receipts) not to find anything, but because the search is the condition of being alive:

"Sorrowfully each morning when he awakened, putting aside those concerns which were not immediate, he would look over old horse scores, track figures, unintelligible turf accounts."

He carries a mourning band. He carries black-bordered handkerchiefs. He wears a white rose. These are not performances of grief; they are the costume of a man for whom grief has become identity. And crucially, the chapter acknowledges what the Pillar names: that the honest life stops pretending otherwise:

"And yet there was ineffable beauty in this grief, more precious than crown jewels or lost empires or ruined dynasties, this precious grief without which he could not live, could not die, for it illuminated him."

Grief is not something to pass through. It is the light source.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Mr. Spitzer is, structurally, a witness. He does not fix. He does not conclude. He watches, records, attends.

His legal practice has become this: not advocacy, not judgment, but the patient observation of the marginal:

"Never once, he believed, had he challenged another person's illusions or pretenses as his were so often challenged... For each man was a deposed king, surrounded by his own fallen glory — living by his own mistaken light or darkness."

This is the Witness ethic made explicit. He will not tell the man with the soap flake certificate that he is not the soap flake king. He will not tell the man with the Ivory stock that he does not own gold mines. He takes the matter under advisement. He says he will return. This is not cowardice or evasion; it is the refusal to simplify another person's reality, the refusal to impose resolution on an experience that has not resolved.

The witness stance extends to his own condition. He does not diagnose himself. He does not seek the physician's verdict. He refuses the heart test. He stays in the room of his own ambiguity, attending to it without rushing toward the conclusion that would end the attending.

His musical composition practice is also witnessing: catching inspiration on the wing, writing on shirt cuffs and handkerchiefs, not organizing, not concluding:

"He was waiting for perfect leisure, a moment which would be undisturbed by another moment, an interruption which would not be interrupted."

The moment of perfect witness never arrives, and he knows it will not. He composes anyway.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Mr. Spitzer builds. He composes music no one will hear, on scraps of paper that blow away. He searches streets for lost heirs he cannot find. He returns, again and again, to the same corners, the same searches, knowing they will yield the same non-results.

"He would seek out the rude, crude, bearded men... searching for him, that one brother he knew he would not find, either among the living or the dead, though he searched through all the avenues of time, all the avenues of space."

He knows he will not find Peron. The search continues. This is not delusion: the text is clear that he knows. It is recursive construction: returning to the beginning with what you learned, building anyway, knowing the building is provisional.

The musical composition is the purest instance:

"He had scarcely the time now to indulge himself in long hours of isolated concentration as to his echoic elegy — and it was written in so many fragments, he could scarcely imagine what the comprehensive thematic patterns of his music would be when the music was completed."

He cannot imagine the completed form because completion is not the point. The composition is the returning. The fragments are the structure, not the failure of structure.

The moment when the scraps blow out of his hat is devastating precisely because it is recursive: the music is lost, but he will write more music, on more scraps, which will also blow away. This is not tragedy in the classical sense. It is the honest description of how building works.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter is formally incomplete; it does not resolve. Mr. Spitzer does not find his brother, does not complete his elegy, does not discover whether the rajah's jewel was found, does not establish his identity. The chapter's final image is of his music blowing down an unending street:

"Saw his elegy blowing, winging down a long, dark, unending street — passing the omniscient angel of the meridian, passing the last street lamp which gave off a dim radiance outside itself? Passing all human knowledge?"

The question mark at the end of the chapter is Unfinished Is Honest made typographic. The text will not close. It passes beyond human knowledge and stops there, in the open.

The rajah's jewel subplot is a perfect structural emblem of this ethic:

"Suddenly, it had been forgotten — and there were other headlines... Only gradually, however, had Mr. Spitzer been able to dismiss from his mind the great mystery... everything was anticlimactic now, though there had been no climax."

No climax. The demand for resolution (who stole the jewel?) is quietly, ruthlessly denied. Life does not provide climaxes. The demand for them is its own violence against experience. The chapter enacts this formally: it raises mysteries and lets them dissolve, not into answers, but into other mysteries.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

The chapter is populated almost entirely by marginal figures: derelicts, exiled grand dukes working as doormen, old Lady Macbeths wandering hobo camps, cancer specialists dying anonymously among criminals, kings of Cracow living in city dumps.

"He had found a banker who had not known he was a banker, a lawyer who had not known he was a lawyer, a judge who had not known he was a judge, a Wall Street broker who had not known he was a Wall Street broker, an Arctic explorer who had not known he was an explorer until Mr. Spitzer came."

This list is not comic. It is ontological evidence. The marginal person (the deposed, the dispossessed, the unrecognized) reveals what the centered, recognized, socially legible person conceals: that identity is not inherent but assigned, constructed, and revocable. The banker does not know he is a banker because being a banker is not a property of his soul but a social construction that can be stripped away by circumstance.

Mr. Spitzer himself is the supreme marginal figure: a dead man still walking, a musician no one has heard, a lawyer with no clients, a twin who is simultaneously himself and his dead brother. He is not the exception to the social order. He is, as the Pillar states, the evidence of what the social order is made of.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The chapter's treatment of time is its most philosophically radical feature. Time does not move forward here. It accumulates.

He would "look at the passport picture which was taken in his youth but was Peron’s picture... see how many times his passport had been stamped, how many frontiers he had crossed, how far he had travelled."

The passport shows journeys he never took: to Siam, to Cracow, to Bangkok. The sediment of Peron's life has deposited itself into his documents, his pockets, his memories. The past is not behind him. It is in him, pressing up.

The newspaper scene is the Pillar's clearest illustration:

"He had realized that the newspapers he was reading with baited breath... were dated ten years ago, no matter which year he was counting from — that the crown jewel had been stolen in the same year as Peron's death."

He has been reading ten-year-old newspapers as if they were current. The past has not receded; it has remained at the surface, indistinguishable from the present. The sedimentary layers have not separated. They press together.

The Boston geography enacts this too: Paul Revere's lantern-bearer asks for horse racing tips. President Wilson dies in the streets. The British Red Coats are coming, and the last trolley has already passed,. All historical moments coexist in the same fog. The past is not behind Mr. Spitzer. It is the ground he walks on, and it is still moving.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is written in a style that performs the instability it describes. The long, recursive, clause-within-clause sentences do not move toward conclusions; they accumulate qualifications, reversals, parenthetical doubts. The syntax enacts the plural self, the sedimentary time, the grief as weather.

Consider this construction:

"He was not always aware of them until he had passed beyond them — or be aware of them, yet their significance was better seen in retrospect. Also, there were not always, as the years seemed to increase, those indications which should have warned him whether he was himself or his dead brother."

The language here is doing what it is describing: it is not sure of itself, it qualifies, it opens possibilities it does not close. This is language that names its own location: the location of a consciousness that cannot achieve stable perspective on itself.

The chapter also plays with the language of legal and financial documents (pawn tickets, certificates, passports, stock shares) showing how the language of official reality construction (law, finance, identity documents) is just as unstable as the language of dreams. The soap flake certificate and the Grand Union Pacific Railway stock are presented with the same syntactic weight as the grasshopper weathervane. Official language does not confer reality. It constructs a particular kind of claim to reality, which can dissolve as easily as any other.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter lives entirely within the ordinary: within fog, snow, street lamps, cigarettes handed to the dead, scraps of paper blowing down dark streets.

"And yet there was ineffable beauty in this grief, more precious than crown jewels or lost empires or ruined dynasties, this precious grief without which he could not live, could not die, for it illuminated him."

The illumination is grief itself, not a transcendence of grief, not a consolation beyond grief, but grief as the immanent sacred. The light source is the wound.

The two packs of cigarettes (one perfumed for himself, one cheap cheroot for the dead) are a small liturgy of immanent transcendence. He carries offerings for the dead as a matter of practical habit, the way one carries subway tokens. The sacred is not elevated above the ordinary. It is continuous with it, indistinguishable from it, practiced in the same gesture as buying a newspaper or checking a watch.

The music scattered in the wind is the chapter's final image of immanent transcendence: the elegy passes beyond human knowledge, not upward into a heaven, but deeper into the immanent, into the fog, the dark street, the unending. The sacred does not transcend the world. It goes further in.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is the Pillar that most completely describes what Mr. Spitzer is in this chapter. He is not a self moving through time. He is where time crosses itself.

"For years collapsed into a moment of sighing. Or a moment of sighing was extended to many years. At one moment, Mr. Spitzer was living — at the next moment, was translated among the dead — simply because he had paused for a moment to sigh."

He is constituted by what was deposited before he arrived, by Peron's life, by the opium lady's dreams, by the dead clients, by the drowned cities, by the historical Boston layered beneath the present Boston. And he is constituting ground: his wanderings, his music on scraps of paper, his legal advice to the grand duke doorman, his gifts of false teeth and wooden legs and second-hand overcoats, all of this is deposited into the sediment that the not-yet-born will stand on.

The chapter makes this structural obligation explicit:

"He would try to lose himself by helping others who were lost to find themselves."

This is not charity. It is the carrying forward that the Crossing Point demands. He cannot not do it. It is structural, not chosen. He carries Peron's errands because Peron is in the sediment of him. He carries music because music was deposited in him by dead composers whose buzzings live in his deaf ear.

The most devastating instance of the Crossing Point is the phone call to himself:

"He had even telephoned himself, though well knowing his phone had long ago been disconnected... and the phone had rung and rung, and he had waited with considerable trepidation, but no one had answered."

He is calling across the crossing point, from the living side to the dead side of himself, or from the present to the past, or from Joachim to Peron. No one answers because the crossing point is not a place where conversation is possible. It is a place where constitutions happen silently, below the level of speech, in the sediment, in the carrying.
