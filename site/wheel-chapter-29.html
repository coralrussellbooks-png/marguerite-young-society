Research notes: Chapter 29 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 29

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Mr. Spitzer is constitutively multiple. He cannot determine where he ends and his dead brother begins:

"he was not always sure, of course, whether his present ghostly thoughts were his own or his dead brother's thoughts moving now through him"

This is not a temporary confusion awaiting resolution. The text refuses to resolve it. He asks:

"Were these two souls which moved through him, old Joachim, the dreamless one? Were these three souls or the amalgamations of many souls"

The plural self here is not psychological fragmentation as pathology. It is presented as the structural condition of being a surviving twin, but by extension, of being any self at all. Peron lives through Joachim; Joachim cannot think without Peron thinking alongside him. The self is a haunted house, not a single occupant.

He is also multiple in his social roles: attorney, mourner, butterfly collector, musician, go-between, reluctant pander for his brother's desires. Each role is a different person, and none is the "real" Joachim. The chapter never locates a core self beneath these layers. There is no core. There are only layers.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The butterfly colors are the chapter's central philosophical image of this pillar:

"Yet butterflies were colorless — the colors being the effects not of pigmentation but of the illusion of light shattered on the gleaming mirror scales as if they were so many geometric prisms"

This is not incidental natural history. It is a thesis statement. The most beautiful things are not colored; they produce the illusion of color through structural refraction. Beauty, meaning, identity: all are effects of light on surfaces, not substances residing in things themselves.

The chapter extends this outward. My mother's visitors, "purple pharaohs in their swan boats, the Irish poets wrapped in bands of fog", are illusions she insists are real. But Mr. Spitzer's butterflies are also, increasingly, illusions:

"what he sensed now was probably the perfume of his thoughts. He swore that he could smell his thoughts."

The line between the butterfly in the room and the butterfly in the mind is never drawn. The chapter does not draw it because the chapter does not believe it exists. Reality is not what we find. It is what the prism of consciousness makes.

"perhaps everything was the devious imitation of everything"

This is Pillar 2 stated with almost brutal directness.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This is perhaps the pillar most fully embodied by the entire chapter. Mr. Spitzer's grief for Peron is not a phase he passed through. It is the climate in which everything else occurs:

"Only his silent music and his memories of his recurring butterflies consoled him now for his brother's early death and for his continuing life"

Note the syntax: his brother's early death and his continuing life are presented as parallel afflictions requiring equal consolation. To continue living after the death of one's twin is itself a form of grief, an ongoing wound.

The grief has not resolved in however many years have passed since Peron's death. It has instead accumulated: accumulating butterfly knowledge, accumulating memories, accumulating the dead brother's thoughts until Joachim is a sediment of grief more than a living man:

"he had suffered this great wound of death, and yet he had suffered more, this wound of life"

The wound of life is the wound that does not close because the person who inflicted it by dying is still present, in thoughts, in memories, in the face that mirrors Joachim's own face. Grief here is not weather that passes. It is the atmosphere itself.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Mr. Spitzer is the chapter's supreme practitioner of witnessing. He does not fix, conclude, or resolve. He observes, catalogs, remembers, and stays in the room:

"he had not disturbed the sleeper"

This moment, finding the mourning cloak butterfly hibernating in a hollow tree in winter and choosing not to disturb it, is a small but perfect enactment of the witness ethic. He sees fully. He does not intervene. He does not pin it down.

The same impulse governs his relationship to his brother's mystery:

"Peron had been, Mr. Spitzer still believed, the greater mystery, and death had enhanced his mystery"

He does not claim to have solved Peron. He witnesses him: incompletely, lovingly, without conclusion. And crucially, he witnesses himself with the same refusal to conclude:

"though he had never yet gotten a total glimpse of himself"

The witness ethic here extends to the self. He does not claim self-knowledge. He observes himself as he observes butterflies, with attention, without capture.

Later, directly:

"Who was Mr. Spitzer now to impale a butterfly, as he would sometimes ask, to pin it down?"

To pin down is to kill. The witness refuses to pin. This is the ethics of the pillar made literal through the central image of the chapter.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter is itself a recursive structure. Mr. Spitzer returns again and again to butterflies (from different angles, with different names, through different memories) never completing the catalog, always beginning again:

"He knew butterflies of every region and of both hemispheres... He knew Urania Sloanus, Ornithoptera Paradisea..."

The list is not a list moving toward completion. It is a list that generates more list. Each butterfly evokes another. The chapter's prose enacts this: sentences accumulate subordinate clauses, each clause opening onto another, the period always deferred.

He is also recursively constructing his understanding of his brother. Each return to Peron reveals a slightly different facet:

"He had been a being of so many volatile impressions and mad designs and fickle moods, so many sudden turns and twists, so many inexpressible ideas"

This is not the description of someone who has been understood. It is the description of someone being perpetually re-approached. The building of Peron's portrait is never finished, and Mr. Spitzer knows it, and continues anyway.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The sock meditation is the chapter's most explicit philosophical comedy of this pillar:

"as there had been so many holes, so many darns and patches, were these new socks or old he wore? The ghostly conformation of his old sock persisted, showing the ghostly shape of his leg, his foot — and yet it seemed to him that, technically, he might be considered to be wearing... new socks — or that he would be when the last original thread had disappeared."

This is the Ship of Theseus rendered in hosiery, and it is not merely comic. The question of identity, whether the patched thing is still the original thing, runs through the entire chapter. Is Joachim still Joachim after so many years of grief? Is his butterfly knowledge still his own after it has been saturated with his brother's absence? The sock is a figure for the self: perpetually repaired, never completed, never quite the same thing it was, never quite a new thing either.

The demand for completion (for a finished self, a concluded grief, a solved brother) is refused throughout. The chapter's length, its refusal to end, is itself an ethical stance. The ongoing sentence is the honest sentence.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

The chapter opens with the problem of the boundary between butterfly and moth:

"creatures who seemed to be at the boundary line between butterfly and moth, evasive creatures who, though they were of the dawn, were so nearly alike in their almost intangible features as to make it difficult for even a great expert like Mr. Spitzer to draw, with absolute certainty, a map or boundary line"

The marginal creature, the thing that cannot be classified, is not an anomaly. It is the evidence that the categories themselves are constructions. The butterfly/moth boundary is the same kind of construction as the happiness/sorrow boundary, which the chapter immediately invokes:

"just as it might at times be difficult to distinguish between brother and brother or between the ecstasy of happiness and that of sorrow"

Brother and brother. This is the chapter's deepest marginal figure: the identical twin, the person who exists at the boundary of selfhood itself. Joachim is not quite one person. Peron is not quite gone. They occupy the margin between self and other, between living and dead.

Mr. Spitzer himself is a marginal figure: too fat, too old, too grief-struck, too obsessed with butterflies to be a fully functional social being. He comes each evening to sit with a woman who may not be awake, in a house full of imaginary guests. He is the evidence that the category of "normal person" is a construction.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This is perhaps the pillar most structurally embedded in the chapter's form. The past does not recede for Mr. Spitzer. It accumulates:

"these butterfly memories, rather than expiring, had shown such durability, that these transitory and mutable things should seem immutable and lasting and protracted and prolonged"

The butterflies he saw decades ago are more present to him now than they were then. The dead brother is more present with each passing year. The grief deepens rather than resolves. This is not pathology in the chapter's terms; it is the honest description of how time actually works for a consciousness that does not pretend otherwise. The hibernating mourning cloak butterfly is the chapter's image of sedimentary time made literal:

"the beautiful mourning cloak butterfly hibernating in a hollow tree trunk... it was sleeping a long winter through, sleeping with folded wings, and he had not disturbed the sleeper"

The butterfly is the past, folded and sleeping inside the present, ready to emerge. When he sees a mourning cloak in spring:

"he had felt as if it were an old friend, and he had almost greeted it, had almost lifted his hat"

The past is not behind him. It wings toward him across the daisy-starred fields.

The geological metaphor becomes explicit in the discussion of original butterflies:

"They had come down through the long corridors of time"

Time is a corridor through which things travel toward us, not away from us. The ancestors are in the sediment. The butterflies of the first stars are in the wings of the butterflies of Boston Common.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely aware of its own linguistic performance. The butterfly names are not neutral labels; they are constructions that carry entire mythological worlds:

"the butterfly Aphrodite... the twelve-spotted butterfly Apollo, namesake of that god who was the twin of the virgin huntress Artemis... butterflies fashioned by Morpheus, god of dreams"

To name a butterfly after Aphrodite is to construct a connection between erotic beauty and the winged psyche. To name one after Apollo is to embed the sun god's twin-ness into the creature's identity. The naming is not description. It is world-making.

The chapter is also self-aware about the limits of language:

"so many inexpressible ideas"

And about the way language shapes what can be known:

the "hieroglyphs engraved upon their gleaming wings as by their names, the nameless dead should still be known and recognized"

The dead who have names can be known. The nameless dead are lost. Language is the condition of existence, not merely its description.

The pun on "ribbing", which Mr. Spitzer both makes and apologizes for, is a moment where the chapter becomes self-conscious about language as play, as construction, as something that can mean multiple things simultaneously, like a butterfly with two souls written on its wings.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter explicitly and repeatedly refuses transcendence in the conventional sense:

"Mr. Spitzer, though he had sought through the wide heavens and earth, had not found the face of God and had not found the location of immutable paradise, yet though he had visited such phantasmal places as the Happy Isles, the Fortunate Isles... Valhalla, Nirvana, the happy hunting grounds"

He has looked everywhere for the beyond. It is not there. And yet the chapter is saturated with the sacred. It is just located here, in the immanent:

"he was sure that it could hear... the thunder in the heart of the rose"

The sacred is inside the rose, not above it. The transcendence is immanent: pressing inward, not outward.

God is cataloged with the same exhaustive, accumulating energy as the butterflies:

"God, it had been said, was the True Vine, the Bread of Life, the Gate, the Door, the Wine... God was the Man of Sorrows, the Old Man, the King of Glory... God was the Narrow Way, the Rose, the Thorn"

This catalog does not affirm God. It dissolves God into multiplicity, into so many names that the name becomes meaningless as a pointer to a single transcendent being. What remains is the quality of attention that the names represent: the sacred is the act of naming with full attention, not the thing named.

The butterfly itself is the chapter's primary figure of immanent transcendence:

"they had always seemed to him the most perfect symbol of immortality, each being the image, the perfect image of himself"

Not immortality in heaven. Immortality as the image that repeats itself, the pattern that persists through metamorphosis. The sacred is the chrysalis, the emergence, the wing: all of it happening here, in the hollow tree, in the spring field, in the old man's clouded mind.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is the pillar that gives the chapter its deepest structure. Mr. Spitzer is explicitly figured as a crossing point between the living and the dead, between past and future, between his own self and his brother's:

"he enjoyed no after-life now except through Mr. Spitzer's literal-minded and mournful memory of him, the approximate repetition of this broken life through fragmentary gleamings until it should be no more, until Mr. Spitzer himself should be dead and beyond recall"

Peron exists only because Joachim carries him. Joachim is the medium through which Peron continues. This is not metaphor; it is the chapter's literal account of how the dead persist. The carrying is structural. It is not chosen.

And the butterflies themselves are crossing points: between the first stars and the present moment, between death and life, between caterpillar and winged creature:

"They had come down through the long corridors of time"

"They had survived the deaths of stars, the cyclic whirlwinds, the ages of fire, the ages of ice"

Mr. Spitzer is where butterfly time crosses human time. He is constituted by what was deposited before him: the butterfly knowledge that grew without his conscious attention, the brother whose thoughts move through him, the grief that accumulated without his permission.

The chapter's final image of this pillar is the lamp-lighter:

"an old lamp-lighter whose face was also streaked with tears and who had said... — He will never light another lamp"

And then:

"Mr. Spitzer had been somewhat surprised by the brevity of human memory, surprised to see the lamps going on one by one down a dark street where the old lamp-lighter moved like Jacob with his ladder of dreams reaching to the clouds"

The lamps go on. The dead man will never light them. But they go on anyway, because the structure of lamp-lighting continues, because others carry it forward, because the obligation is not personal but structural. This is the crossing point: the place where what was deposited before you continues through you into what comes after.
