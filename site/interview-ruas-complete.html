Young's Interviews Analyzed

The Complete Charles Ruas Interview, "Conversations with American Writers" (1985)

← Previous · Young's Interviews Analyzed · Next →

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The source at a glance

This is the full published text of Charles Ruas's interview with Young, collected in his book "Conversations with American Writers." It is the most complete of the three forms of one interview: the Paris Review's "Art of Fiction No. 66" was a shorter excerpt of it, and the WBAI "career" recording was the raw session, so where all three exist this is the master version to cite. Because the opium lady, the from-life method, the muralist imagination after William James, the sea-walk, the deaf painter, and the "prosaic sprite" were already analyzed through those shorter forms, this reading concentrates on the very large body of material the complete text adds, which is where its value lies. The headnote is fuller too, laying out the novel's four parts and its circular, Elizabethan-derived style, and it introduces two small bibliographic drifts worth logging: it dates "Prismatic Ground" to 1937 (the Paris Review said 1939) and Young dates the commission of Miss MacIntosh to 1945 (she says 1944 elsewhere).

The grandmother, the dead cousin, and the mother

The complete grandmother section is far richer than the excerpt and supplies the single most illuminating new fact in the whole archive. Young's parents separated when she was three, leaving her with no memory of them, and the grandmother who raised her had lost a grandson two weeks before Young was born, a golden-haired boy named Harry who died of diphtheria. The grandmother believed "little Harry had been born again in me," and Young grew up as the reincarnation of a dead child, singing a folk song about Harry gone to heaven, a belief she compares to Dali taking the place of a dead brother and credits with feeding her sense of "the dualities of life and the depths of dreams." That childhood identity as a returned dead child stands behind the doubles and revenants that fill her fiction. She adds the Bible memorized daily in the King James version "under the aegis of one of my Stuart ancestors," the grandmother's line that "the stone that was rejected" would be "the foundation stone of the new creation," and a comparison of her own displacement to "What Maisie Knew." The mother, largely absent elsewhere, is drawn directly here: a rebel "who had to have many loves," beautiful "as the Gish sisters," who married Young's father "almost as a wager" and, saying "I do," was already thinking "I don't." This is the mother Young later admits is inside the opium lady.

The New York literary world

The full text expands the arrival in New York into a portrait of a whole milieu. It documents the reception of her first two books: the Reynal and Hitchcock scout Frank Taylor tracking "the poet in this neighborhood" through a snowstorm, Mark Van Doren reading "Angel in the Forest" aloud all night to his wife before accepting it, the two books appearing together, and William Rose Benet's award citation for "the preservation of rarity," which he confirmed was an echo of Elinor Wylie, Young's "ideal writer among women." Her closest early friend was George Davis, editor of Mademoiselle, who refused to believe the young woman before him had written "Angel in the Forest," insisting its author must be "an elderly woman who has spent her entire life writing one great book," which Young found prophetic of her own long labor. She describes the Brooklyn Heights "House of Genius" where Davis lived among Auden, McCullers, and Richard Wright, complete with an animal trainer's chimpanzee that wore a dress suit, played piano, and "pays the highest income tax" in the house, and she remembers meeting Kay Boyle there in a cowboy suit. Leo Lerman, first taken for a traveler back from Tibet, becomes a friend whose uptown parties roared "like some great baseball field."

Yaddo and the friends

An entirely new stretch recalls a summer at Yaddo that seeded the novel. Truman Capote occupied Katrina Trask's tower, where a harp played when the door opened, and Young says "I used that harp in my description of the Opium Lady," as the device by which the dreamer tells whether a visitor is living or dead. Capote was writing "Other Voices, Other Rooms" and ran through the rose garden at night howling that the guests were to "create, create under the trees." Most useful for the novel, the group played a murder game in which a person was accused if caught "under a black umbrella," which is the literal source of Miss MacIntosh's umbrella and its charge of guilt. Agnes Smedley threatened to "hang Truman from the trees," meaning the president, not the writer, who was offended not to matter enough to hang; and seeing an early television, Capote pronounced "this has no future." The section darkens into loss: Reeves McCullers's later suicide prompts Young's reflection that murder and suicide differ only in whether the gun turns outward or inward, and that Reeves was "just wrecked" from childhood. She recounts the friendship with Mari Sandoz, each first taking the other for a madwoman writing in a cafe, "the Sand Hills Kid" who told the Nebraska governor he had "no jurisdiction here, nor does God." And she deepens the Anais Nin relationship, Nin championing Miss MacIntosh across the country while dying of a cancer she called a "death warrant" delivered the very day her fame arrived.

John Gardner and the blood contract

The full text carries a major literary-history episode absent from the excerpt: Young taught John Gardner at Iowa, among students just out of the Korean War, when two ex-Marine instructors were pushing "bad imitations of Hemingway." Gardner, torn between that hard-boiled style and "the deeper resources of his cultivated mind," was told by Young that he was "too gifted to write in this bald way." To seal it they cut their fingers and signed a contract in blood in which Gardner promised to follow her advice and become a famous writer. He wrote his first two novels in her class, and the Beowulf novel Young dismissed as "kid stuff" became "Grendel." When Gardner died in a motorcycle crash, Young said she had kept him from becoming "a motorcycle writer" but not "a motorcycle rider." The passage is the clearest evidence in the set of her actual influence as a teacher, beside the students named in the Dowling chapter.

The rewrite, and the sacredness of the word

The complete interview settles the question of whether Young revised. Asked whether the novel was "written and rewritten in layers," she says she wrote it through once, then found "something was wrong," because Mr. Spitzer was pretending to be his dead twin while secretly knowing which he was. When she realized he must not be sure, she "went back and rewrote," and "that's where the book took on its cosmic magnitude," a change that cost "months and months." Before the rewrite, she says, it was "almost a de Maupassant joke." This confirms the same story she tells in the 1983 interview and shows that the flat claim of the 1965 interviews, that "not one word has been rewritten," describes her feeling about language, not the structural rewriting she plainly did. She frames the labor as moral: she could be clever but wants "to be real," "sincere and profound," and calls her comedy something "based upon a greater tragic sense." She offers her emblem of art's risk, a violinist who bowed without touching the strings for twenty years and was fired the day he made one true note, "you hit reality once and die," and insists there is "not one single invented moment" in the book. The Debs work, begun as a short protest against Vietnam and grown "like Topsy," extends the same creed into nonfiction "stranger than fiction," peopled by real figures like Victoria Woodhull and rooted in a "transcendental socialism" that "preceded Marx."

Set against the other sources

For the compilation this is the anchor text among the Ruas materials, and the three forms, this book, the Paris Review excerpt, and the WBAI recording, should be treated as one interview with this as the fullest and most reliable. It resolves a name the other versions garble: the psychiatrist who explained the black gloves is here "Dr. Ernst Hammerschlag, a friend who was a psychiatrist from Vienna," which should correct both the transcript's "Harberstock" and the Paris Review's "Hammershlagd." It names the deaf painter Howard Mitcham and gives the fullest form of the Henry James dream visitations, of the autobiography admission that the opium lady was "in a way" her mother, and of the Norman Mailer story and its "gentle Hercules in high heels." Its genuinely new contributions are large: the dead cousin Harry and the reincarnation belief, which reframes the doubles and revenants of the fiction as rooted in Young's own childhood identity; the full New York and Yaddo milieu, including the black-umbrella game that is the source of a central image; and the John Gardner blood contract. Two drifts to log: the commission date given here as 1945 against 1944 elsewhere, and "Prismatic Ground" dated 1937 against 1939 in the Paris Review headnote. Where the Ruas versions differ in wording, this complete text is the one to quote.
