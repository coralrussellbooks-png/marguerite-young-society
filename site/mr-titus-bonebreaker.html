Mr. Titus Bonebreaker

Part of To All My Darlings, a study of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965).
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

Home

In the research: Chapter 64, Chapter 65, Chapter 66, Chapter 68

Definition from research

Chapter 64

  • “she having refused the hand of the true King of Sweden”: King of Sweden refers to Mr. Bonebreaker and his belief that he was descended from royalty.

Chapter 65

  • A passionate, zealous Gospel salesman from Missouri, characterized by his fervor and eccentricity.
  • He preaches about the impending end of the world, salvation, and the need for repentance.
  • He is physically gaunt and stoop-shouldered, with distinctive features like a beaked nose and bright eyes.
  • Despite his religious zeal, he is inconsistent and flawed (e.g., his diet, brushes with police, personal hygiene).
  • He represents religious fanaticism and the human desire for meaning and redemption amid decay and despair.
  • His relationship with Miss MacIntosh is complex: he idealizes her, but ultimately fails to accept her fully.
  • His preaching is openly racist and antisemitic: Black men are to be shipped back to Africa as “the sons of Ham,” and each Jew he saves earns him a star twice as bright. Miss MacIntosh disagrees with him, “quite flatly.”
  • His first name may refer to Titus the Roman emperor from 79 to 81 AD, who was also a celebrated military general. He is known for leading the Roman army to victory in the First Jewish-Roman War, which resulted in the destruction of Jerusalem and its temple. His brief reign as emperor is notable for the eruption of Mount Vesuvius and the opening of the Colosseum. Titus is also the companion to whom Paul wrote the Epistle to Titus.
  • “just as she had done to the King of Sweden”: See the King of Sweden in the Chapter 64 resources, and Mr. Bonebreaker below.
  • “Mr. Bonebreaker, a Gospel salesman, saw her reeling”: The surname Bonebrake is an Americanized version of the German name Beinbrech and means “bone break.” The original Beinbrech family lineage began in Switzerland in the 17th century, and the name evolved through various spellings before becoming Bonebrake in America. The name likely served as a metonymic occupational name for a herbalist, possibly referring to medicinal plants.
  • “the bar sinister”: The bar sinister, properly a bend or baton sinister, is popularly taken as the heraldic mark of illegitimate birth. Mr. Bonebreaker believes it shut him out from a throne, and decides that all the kings who sit on thrones are crossed by it as he is.

Chapter 66

Listed as: Mr. Bonebreaker

  • Miss MacIntosh reflects that Mr. Bonebreaker belonged to an old religion where women must kneel before men, similar to the customs of What Cheer, Iowa, where women wash men’s feet. She contrasts this with her own situation, noting her baldness and lack of traditionally feminine features, which prevent her from fulfilling such a role.
  • Mr. Bonebreaker seems to represent a lost opportunity for love and connection in Miss MacIntosh’s life. He is associated with the idea that she could never kneel before a man, implying a rejection of traditional gender roles and a sense of isolation.
  • It is said that Mr. Bonebreaker fled leaving his umbrella behind.
  • Miss MacIntosh’s baldness is a significant factor in her perception of her relationship with Mr. Bonebreaker. She believes her lack of hair and other physical attributes made her unattractive and contributed to the failure of their relationship.
  • Mr. Bonebreaker is mentioned as one of the people who knew Miss MacIntosh was bald.
  • “Had she not once had a love affair with the King of Sweden”: Mr. Bonebreaker, who believed he was the true King of Sweden.
  • “But Mr. Bonebreaker had belonged to that old religion”: This passage critiques patriarchal traditions by illustrating the extreme expectations placed on women to be subservient to men, using vivid, almost grotesque imagery of kneeling and hair used as a towel for washing men’s feet. “that old religion” and its description here refers to patriarchy of which Christianity is a part and instances of the changes from a matrilineal society and traditions to a patrilineal one are in the Bible (Marilyn French, From Eve to Dawn: A History of Women in the World, 2002). Patriarchy entitles men to admiration, sex, consent, and domestic labor from women. Men also receive better medical care and pain management and overall focus of health science for their bodies. Men also are able to practice bodily control which is often and routinely denied women. Misogyny and sexism go hand in hand with patriarchy although it is possible for men to practice one or the other and both need not be present at the same time. Sexism is the theoretical and ideological branch where beliefs, ideas and assumptions are used to rationalize and naturalize patriarchal norms and expectations. Misogyny is the “law enforcement” branch of patriarchy which polices and enforces gendered norms and expectations for girls and women (Kate Manne, Down Girl, 2017, and Entitled, 2020). Gerda Lerner documented men’s withholding knowledge and power from women through denying education for centuries (The Creation of Feminist Consciousness, 1993). Young upends many of these gendered norms in the novel.

Chapter 68

  • “a dead man’s hat”: Mr. Weed mirrors Mr. Bonebreaker, who also wears dead people’s clothes: a dead waiter’s coat and “a dead man’s shoes.”
