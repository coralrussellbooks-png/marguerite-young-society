Young's Interviews Analyzed

The American Audio Prose Library Interview, New York, May 1983

← Previous · Young's Interviews Analyzed · Next →

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The interview at a glance

The recording was made in New York City in May 1983 for the American Audio Prose Library. The interviewer is credited in the transcript as "Cabanetti," almost certainly a mis-hearing of Kay Bonetti, who founded that library and conducted its author interviews. The transcript is dense and roaming, and it garbles a fair number of proper names by ear. The corrections most useful for the record: the New School co-founder is Horace Kallen (given as "Callum"), the settlement house is Hull House (given as "Hall House"), the deathbed scene traces to the poet Elinor Wylie and her husband William Rose Benet (given as "Eleanor Wiley" and "William Rosemanet"), the Chicago thesis subject is John Lyly's "Euphues" (given as "Euphueus"), and the Carlyle book is "Sartor Resartus" (given as "Sartre, Ressartius"). With those fixed, the factual content is clear and consistent. The talk moves in two long stretches: her Midwestern formation and reading, then the years that made Miss MacIntosh and the Debs book she was writing at the time.

Formation: Indiana, books, and a grandmother who believed in metaphor

Young was born in Indianapolis and moved to New York in 1943. She corrects a common paraphrase of her own view: she did not say Midwesterners must become sophisticated, but that those who become literary are "more likely to be very sophisticated," because they grow up in isolation from other writers, "often a thousand miles away from another writer," and "only dream they're going to be writers." Thrown on books, they become "voracious readers of books from the beginning." She paints Indianapolis as a Victorian town proud of its literary life, with Browning and Holmes societies and people who had known Mark Twain or studied with William James. At about eighteen she read all of Browning under Catherine Merrill Graydon, who had known him in London, and worked through "The Ring and the Book."

The grandmother matters most. Young read Dickens, "Middlemarch," and Matthew Arnold by ten or twelve because this grandmother "encouraged the reading of serious books from the beginning" and, crucially, "never questioned the validity of anything in a poem." A talking frog spoke. "It was Bible truth as far as she was concerned." Young defends fairy tales on the same ground, calling "the fairy tale the psychology of its time," carrying moral and spiritual truth "in the realm of the talking bird, the talking beast." That single idea is a key to the nature imagery of Miss MacIntosh.

The University of Chicago years

At Chicago she wrote her thesis on "the birds and beasts" of Lyly's "Euphues, His England," the book she credits with carrying the bestiary tradition of Herodotus into English prose. Her major subject was Milton and "The Fall of Man," studied beside a comparative survey of epics, and she describes herself as a seventeenth-century and Baroque scholar. Two through-lines begin here. Milton's blindness seeded her lasting interest in "what happens when a sense is missing," which becomes the blind and deaf characters of the novel and their gifts of compensation. The epic catalog and the epic simile become her method of piling up people and correspondences. She worked her way through school in the depths of the Depression, a fact she returns to whenever money and time come up.

The opium lady: Mette Weissenbach

The single most important encounter in the interview is the "opium lady." Sent by the vocational guidance office to a room at Billings Hospital, Young was hired to read Shakespeare aloud to a bedridden woman who wanted "a vacation away from the modern world." She read to her for two years, through opium dreams and near-comas, "during the visitations of the elephant or balloon fish swimming over her bed." Already steeped in De Quincey and Coleridge, Young calls it "a marvelous education" and insists she was never tempted by the drug herself.

The woman was Mette Weissenbach of Chicago, a patron of Hull House and of Harriet Monroe's "Poetry," a friend of Thornton Wilder, a discoverer of Edna St. Vincent Millay, and part of the New School circle around Horace Kallen. She "had never shown the mark of time" while bedridden and aged overnight once she rose, the exact biography Young gives Catherine Cartwheel, Vera's mother in the novel. Weissenbach died in 1943, and part of what drew Young to New York was the hope of seeing her again; she arrived to find her recently dead. Young dedicated her poetry book "Moderate Fable" to her and calls the family connection "the most important connection of my entire life." When Young later told Wilder she had been writing about the opium lady, he answered at once, as she reports it, that it would be a novel "revealing the imagery of the unconscious."

What Young read and carried

Young is emphatic that she owes "nothing to any other fiction writer" for her characters, then names a long line of masters for method and vision. William James runs deepest: she studied his psychology "as if it were a Bible" and takes from him stream of consciousness (his coinage, first applied to his brother Henry's novels), false memory and substitution, the power of habit, and the many selves "under one roof or dome or hat." Two Jamesian phrases sit at the novel's moral center: "the person you did not become," which she pairs with Frost's "the road not taken."

Browning gives her the creed "the poet is the great reporter," which underwrites her rule that everything in the book is drawn from life. Gogol's "Dead Souls" and Dickens teach her to "give a being to any minor character" and to build a novel as a road of many meetings. Don Quixote stands behind the quest and the play of dream against reality. For temperament she places herself nearer Sterne and Joyce than Henry James, because she sees "a more chaotic world," and she reaches past James toward St. Augustine, Plutarch's "Lives," Carlyle's "Sartor Resartus," Melville's "Moby Dick," the Bible, and the "Lives of the Saints." She names Dylan Thomas as closer kin than James and calls herself, borrowing her own phrase for Allen Ginsberg, "a sad Walt Whitman." Visual and comic sources surface too: Chagall behind Esther, W.C. Fields behind the passive policeman and Esther's father, Ethel Barrymore behind Miss MacIntosh's walk, and Charlie Chaplin, who "has influenced every book I've ever written."

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling: the governing ideas

Young began the novel in 1943 and worked on it for about eighteen years. She set it in the 1930s on purpose, wanting the distance of memory rather than a current book; the bus driver who will not cut his hair "until Roosevelt gets out of the White House" fixes the period. On the first day she "listed all the strangest people I had ever known" and wrote out a page of questions: "Is it real? Or is it a dream? How much is real? How much is dream? What is man's fate on this planet?" Her aim was "a novel that would show what it was like to be alive in my period."

The book's recurring question is whether a thing is really there or rests on a false assumption, dramatized when Miss MacIntosh removes her wig and life delivers "a cold surprise." Around that turn several themes braid together: illusion against reality; how consciousness apprehends the world; loss and the compensations of a missing sense; multiplicity, the many selves and many people held in one design; and interdependence, the truth Vera reaches at the end. Young accepts the reading that the novel is political, "the exact theme of my Deb's book" and of "Angel in the Forest," and describes Miss MacIntosh herself as a Christian socialist who "can't wait for evolution" and "wants revolution." What she calls her "muralist imagination" is the formal expression of all this: she wants her characters "to flash back and forth," lighting one another so the past reads differently in the light of each new figure, in "reverberations, chords, and themes."

Miss MacIntosh: characters drawn from life

Young insists "there isn't a single character or event in that book that isn't based upon something which did happen somewhere." The interview supplies an unusually full key:

  • Catherine Cartwheel, the opium-dreaming mother, is Mette Weissenbach, down to the agelessness in bed and the sudden aging on rising. Mr. Spitzer's confession to a woman who has already died draws on the death of Elinor Wylie, who died at the table as her husband William Rose Benet spoke.

  • Vera Cartwheel, the narrator, is effectively motherless, since Catherine "denied her child's existence." Young says Miss MacIntosh becomes "the mother of us all," and the stone deaf man a kind of father.

  • Miss MacIntosh is a composite of Indianapolis spinsters, one of whom wore a wig and kept a spare. Her walk comes from Ethel Barrymore. She is the novel's labor advocate and Christian socialist.

  • Mr. Spitzer and his dead twin Peron carry the book's central uncertainty of identity. In the first draft Spitzer knew he was really Peron; Young rejected that as too neat, "almost like O'Henry," and rewrote it so he does not know. His silent, inward music mirrors her own.

  • Esther Longtree, who claims children she never bore, comes from a real Chicago case Young read in the paper: a man suing to prove he was not sterile, and a detective on the case. Her origin in a crude artificial insemination sets up her endless pregnancies as a search to replace a lost connection. Chagall and W.C. Fields feed her world.

  • The Christian hangman is based on a real hangman "who had no animals on his farm." Young read confessions and lives of hangmen as research, and every criminal case in the book rests on a real one. The lynched man who survives and pushes a baby carriage, later the father of Esther's first child, comes from a rope still hanging in a Southern town where she once lived.

  • Jackie, the tubercular homecoming queen, grew from a girl in New Harmony and a stranger's overheard line, "Poor Robert, he tried so hard to live long enough to see his sister graduate." Young now calls the roughly one hundred and forty page section "almost a book by itself" and thinks she might cut it.

  • Howard Mitchum, the stone deaf painter Vera marries, is a real friend who could "see music." His remembered refrain runs "little boydies don't have no tits... I'm glad I'm not a little boydy." Young calls it "a very loving portrait," made with his knowledge and blessing.

How the book was made

Young wrote slowly, "maybe a page or two a day," and revised for insight rather than to fix careless prose. When a passage "seemed to have gone dead, like a light bulb blowing out," she traced it to a character she had held to a "rigid preconception," then let the character "run away with the thing," which is why Spitzer's not-knowing cost her two or three years. She did not fear finishing; she was "hysterically anxious to finish it," pressed by the cost of the years and by teaching loads that put her on the subway seven hours a week. She refused to "part with anything until I've done the best I can," while insisting the result is not perfect but "just the best I could do." The ending was fixed early. She knew from near the start that Vera would marry Howard Mitchum, so "the end was in the beginning," a courtship she never expected to run eighteen years.

On writing, teaching, and the writer's life

Young does not intellectualize while composing. She listens "until I heard a kind of inner music," an inner voice she attributes partly to an ear injury at age three that, some musical friends told her, might otherwise have made her a composer. She rejects a purely regional idea of American literature, calling it "cosmic" and bound to millennial and religious dreams, while still telling students to "start where you are" and write "that which we know best." She believes firmly in "educated writers" and distrusts the unschooled, teaches poetry as reliably teachable, and is wary of fiction workshops because a teacher can "do great harm."

Money runs through all of it. She thinks "all writers should be rich," mourns the talents lost to rent and taxes and day jobs, and says only money kept her from becoming a criminologist, a comparative-religion scholar, or a labor reporter. She frames writing as protection, "like an island," where "no one can really hurt me," and as happiness rather than agony. Every book, she says, has been about utopia and about the fact that "we are a brother's keeper": "Angel in the Forest" a communal utopia, Miss MacIntosh "the search for perfection and happiness in the individual life," and the Debs book a "political and economic utopia."

The person in the room

The voice is erudite and associative, quick to reach for a dead writer's company. Young says she sees Poe at Sheridan Square "any rainy night," at least "his astral body," keeps portraits of dead writers in her apartment, loves graveyards, and jokes that she is a "necrophiliac... Dead writers," because "writers write because they want to be remembered 100 years from now." She dreamed nightly that Henry James looked over her shoulder and said "That's very good, Marguerite," yet insists she does not write "under the glass bell world that James had," since "42nd Street is my daily lot." She is warm and self-deprecating, claiming that with "naturally curly hair" she would have had more fun and never written, and that a fitting title for any memoir would be "Nothing Ever Happened to Me," since she is "not introspective" and would rather talk politics than dream about her inner life. The Kennedy anecdote gathers all of this at once: she wrote the president about the Pacific atoll tests entirely in the imagery of the ancient mariner and the albatross, asking how a mother could keep her baby from opening its eyes at the blast.

Threads to carry into the next interviews

Several claims here are load-bearing and worth tracking for consistency across the other interviews: the eighteen-year span and 1943 start of Miss MacIntosh; the flat assertion that every character and event is drawn from life; the primacy of William James and the missing-sense theme; the opium lady as the novel's origin and emotional root; the reading of the book as political and as one utopia among her three; and the self-portrait as a Midwestern, book-raised, deliberately unautobiographical writer who prizes education, reveres the dead, and works from inner music rather than theory. Where later interviews repeat these, the repetition will show which parts of the story Young had settled into a fixed telling; where they diverge will mark the places worth weighing.
