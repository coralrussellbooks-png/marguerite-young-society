Lesson 11: The Crossing Point

Pillar: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point · Across every book

Act III: The Flat Horizon · Course Finale

You are the axis where infinity meets.

This course began with a crowded room of inner voices. Since then it has walked through illusion, grief, witness, loops, unfinished truths, the margins, buried time, language, and the sacred in the mud. The last question is simple. Where do all these forces actually meet? Not in heaven, not in history books, not in theory. They meet in one place at a time: a single human heart, on an ordinary day.

The Framework

Around the year 524, the Roman statesman Boethius sat in prison awaiting execution and wrote The Consolation of Philosophy. In its second book, Lady Philosophy gives voice to Fortune. Fortune explains that change is her very nature. She turns her wheel, lifting the low and throwing down the high, and if she ever stopped turning she would no longer be Fortune. The lesson for Boethius is to stop expecting the wheel to hold still.

This lesson reimagines Fortune's speech this way: "In constancy is my very essence," says Fortune. "Turn my wheel with a rush of wings... but if you try to stop it, you are no longer Fortune's master." The one constant is the turning itself, and mastery comes only from moving with it.

For Boethius and the medieval artists who followed him, the wheel stood outside the person, a cosmic machine. Marguerite Young moved the wheel entirely inside the human heart. In Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, Vera Cartwheel, whose very name holds a wheel, becomes the place where everything crosses: her mother's opium dreams, the nursemaid's secret body, the strangers' confessions, the sea, the long bus ride, and her own uncertain self. Vera does not stand above these forces. She is where they intersect. That intersection is what Radical Immanence calls the crossing point.

The last lesson ended with a seam. Deleuze's plane of immanence is impersonal. Young's world is spoken by a single, trembling voice. The crossing point stitches that seam. The forces are impersonal, vast, and older than anyone. Yet they only ever meet somewhere particular, in a body, on a Tuesday, in a kitchen. The individual is not the owner of the forces. The individual is their meeting place.

All Ten Pillars, Meeting in One Heart

  1. Not One Self: the heart is crowded, and every voice has a seat at the hub.

  2. Reality Is Constructed: the heart's private myths are part of the wheel's real spokes.

  3. Grief Doesn't End: loss becomes the air that moves the wheel.

  4. The Witness: the hub holds still enough to watch every spoke turn.

  5. Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary: each turn passes through old ground and builds on it.

  6. Unfinished Is Honest: the wheel never finishes turning, and that is its truth.

  7. The Margins Reveal the Truth: the rim, the outermost edge, keeps the wheel whole.

  8. The Past Doesn't Go Away: every past turn is pressed into the axle.

  9. Language Shapes What's Possible: each word spoken adds a spoke.

  10. The Sacred Is Here: the holy is not above the wheel. It is the turning itself.

The Art Companion

A thirteenth-century manuscript of songs known as the Carmina Burana contains a painted Wheel of Fortune. Four figures ride its rim, marked in Latin: I shall reign, I reign, I have reigned, I am without a kingdom. In 1937 the composer Carl Orff set poems from the manuscript to music, opening with the thundering chorus "O Fortuna." Listen to it as an outer wheel, loud and cosmic, then sit in silence afterward and notice the same turning inside the chest.

William Blake's poem "Auguries of Innocence," written around 1803, begins: "To see a World in a Grain of Sand / And a Heaven in a Wild Flower." Blake places infinity in the smallest particular thing. That is the crossing point in two lines.

Reflection Questions

  • Which pillar changed how I see my own life the most, and which one did I resist?

  • Where in an ordinary week do all these forces meet in me at once?

  • What would it mean to stop trying to step off the wheel and learn to move with it?

  • If I wrote one sentence at the hub of my own wheel, what would it say?

The Exercise: The Wheel of the Heart

  1. Draw a large circle on a sheet of paper, with a small circle at its center for the hub.

  2. Draw ten spokes from hub to rim. Label each spoke with one pillar from this course.

  3. Along each spoke, write one line from the course's work: an answer to a reflection question, a result from an exercise, or something noticed since.

  4. In the hub, complete this sentence: "I am the place where ___ meets ___."
