The WNYC Interview (undated, the out-of-print years)

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The interview at a glance

This is a WNYC radio conversation, labeled part one of three, and only part one has surfaced, so this reading covers what survives. The transcript carries no date, but Young says the novel "was published in 1965 by Scribners," went into paperback "the following year," and is now "out of print" with the reputation of "a great masterpiece," while a new edition is "always promised, always deferred like millennium." That places the talk well after 1965 and 1966, in the years the book was unavailable and awaiting a reissue. The recording degrades at the very end into unrelated audio, weather and a cat and a stray line about a "grandson of Eleanor Wiley," which is a bleed from another source and is set aside here. What remains is the densest of the three interviews on her childhood and her family, and it reworks the familiar Miss MacIntosh material through a new lens: the quarrel between Calvinism and Catholicism.

Indianapolis, the Athens of the Middle West

Young calls Indianapolis "the Athens of the Middle West" and jokes that it was a Greek city built on an Indian trading post. She adds a local legend, that the city's first banker, an ancestor of Kurt Vonnegut, kept settlers' money in a desk drawer so trustworthily that the drawer "grew to be the first bank." The point of the passage is her reversal of the usual story of a provincial leaving for the city. Moving to Greenwich Village was a "cultural shock" downward, she says, because the level "was so much higher in Indianapolis," where her childhood was spent among "brilliant older people, scholars, readers of books." In the Village, by contrast, "if you said you were a poet, you automatically were a poet."

The Bible as training, and a claimed lineage

The grandmother's program of reading is spelled out further here. Young says she was "a biblical scholar" at about eleven, taking weekly lessons in "the geography of the Holy Land," and that she read the Bible "for its literary style" because her grandmother thought she would be a writer and considered "the beautiful structures of the Bible" the best training there was, a view Young still holds. She then lays out an unusually specific family mythology, which is best read as her stated lineage rather than as verified fact. On her father's side she claims to be "a collateral descendant of Brigham Young," and on her mother's "a direct descendant of John Knox," whose line, she says, runs through James Boswell, Lord Byron, Herman Melville, and Robert Louis Stevenson. She also claims a French descent through the family of Madame de Sevigne, naming a sainted ancestor who "left her house and put on rags and lived with the beggars of Paris." She feels "very Scottish and also very French," has "a feeling of deja vu about Edinburgh," and reports that a woman in Scotland gives public readings of Miss MacIntosh, whose only complaint was that the novel, at "1135 pages," is "too short."

Calvin against Rome: the novel's two women

The genealogy is not idle, because Young uses it to open the interpretation that sets this interview apart. She reads Melville's "Moby Dick" as a struggle between "the Calvinism of his Scottish Presbyterian ancestors" and "his yearning for some faith like Catholicism," and calls that "my own conflict," since she is "very, very drawn to the Catholic Church" yet will "never go to any church." She then maps the conflict onto the two women at the center of Miss MacIntosh. Miss MacIntosh is the Calvinist Scottish nursemaid, "the stern voice of duty and the love of truth and common sense, none of which she had." The opium lady is "the irrational," the belief that "life itself is a play of illusions." Young's twist is that the opium lady is "more near to reality," because the nursemaid is "the one illusionist" in her faith that there is "a simple foundation of truth." Young also notes that Miss MacIntosh calls herself a descendant of John Knox in the novel, "so at least that much of it seems to be an autobiographical note," a small crack in her steady insistence that the book is not autobiographical.

Miss MacIntosh as the bald prince

The nursemaid's physical description is fuller here than anywhere else. She wears red curls, the mackintosh raincoat, sea boots and sea hat, and a black umbrella that "casts a shadow around her and forbids intimacy." Young calls her "an orphan angel," borrowing the phrase from Elinor Wylie's book about Shelley surviving and reaching America, and reveals a hidden ancestry inside the fiction: while writing, Young "discovered" that the MacIntoshes descend from "a bald prince, who was washed up on the shores of Scotland," presumed dead in France, who became a king. So Miss MacIntosh "is really a bald prince, disguised with a red wig and the woman's gear," able to appear "as a beautiful man when she takes off her wig," which makes her "the very crumbling of the illusion of reality." The sea-walk is described in detail: she carries the umbrella "until her bald head reaches sea level," then goes down "wearing nothing but a pair of black gloves," her wig left "on the back of a horseshoe crab," gone "back to the sea to get her hair." The baldness carries a homemade theology. God "took 6 days to create the earth and rested on the 7th," and she complains he "should have taken 7 days and rested on the 8th," because he left things unfinished, her hair, eyelashes, and eyebrows among them. Light burns her skin, so she carries the umbrella even on sunny days, and she is so compulsive that she sweeps seashells back into the waves. Young says she "had a great deal of fun" with "the one normal character" in a book where "none of the others even pretend to be normal." An editor loved the relief of her, then stopped short: "she committed suicide on page 6. If she was normal, why did she kill herself?" That question, Young says, is "the entire interest of this book," why the one supposedly normal person dies while "the mad lived and flourished like the green bay tree or the red, red rose." She frames the result as "an American existentialism" carried with joy and "a certain exuberance."

The child who woke up a poet

The interview is a trove of childhood writing. Young wrote a serial "about the adventures of a little yellow chicken" that kept her sister "in a state of nervous agitation for years," and a six-act play, six because a rival down the street was writing six and because she had heard plays had five and thought she had "better have six." The play, "The Black Box," turned on a switched-identity plot in which a nursemaid steals a rich baby from the cradle and leaves a poor one, so that a father unknowingly courts his own daughter. With five dollars of birthday money she joined the Authors League of America after an advertisement promising "instructions easy enough for a child of 12 to understand," and received "the most fabulous mail," including the counsel that "to write a story, you must find a germ," which she and her sister took literally, hunting motes in sunbeams and trying to catch them "like lightning bugs." At sixteen she went to sleep "a perfectly normal human being" and woke "with the absolute resolve that I would be a poet," then asked an older student how, and was shown iambs and stresses "that looked like basting stitches." Her first poem was about "preparing a layette for my unborn fatherless baby," an image she connects directly to Esther Longtree, who prepares layettes for "the children of her dreams," and which drew a scolding from an English teacher who said "a young girl should not have such thoughts."

Untermeyer, the opium lady's tutelage, and the Wylie circle

Louis Untermeyer came to town and Young served as his "girl guide," showing him everything of note, "including the mortuary establishment from which my family had been buried." He promised and delivered a good review of her first book, which Macmillan had "instantly accepted," an acceptance she took to be simply normal. Young says her second book, "Moderate Fable," was written "under the influence of a fabulous opium lady," the Chicago recluse she here names Minna Weissenbach, who urged her to read Darwin's journal of the voyage of the Beagle and filled her with bestiaries and books on birds, serpents, and butterflies, so as to bring the forms of nature "into the field of art." As the opium lady talked, "a beautiful golden cockerel" perched at the foot of her bed and issued "messages in the Greek language." Because Young was in and out of that house for years and "never once" accepted the opium she was offered, the lady called her "the prosaic sprite," and Young slept in a bed that "had once been occupied by Edna St. Vincent Millay," though she "did prefer Eleanor Wiley." The Wylie thread runs on: "Moderate Fable" won "the National Academy Award for the best book of the year," judged in part by William Rose Benet, Elinor Wylie's widower, with a citation "for preserving rarity in modern literature" that Benet linked to something reminiscent of Wylie. Young closes the circle with an anecdote about a socialist friend, Eleanor Robinson, who married Wylie's former husband and sent word that she was now "Eleanor Wiley," having inherited the poet's dresses and silver.

Set against the earlier interviews

This interview confirms the load-bearing story and adds a great deal that the other two do not carry. It repeats the opium lady as origin, the baldness and sea-walk, the suicide on page six, and the editor's puzzle over a normal woman who kills herself. What is new is substantial: the claimed descent from Knox, Byron, Melville, and Stevenson; the Bible as deliberate literary training; the Calvinist against Catholic reading that turns Miss MacIntosh and the opium lady into two halves of one religious conflict; the childhood writings; Louis Untermeyer and Macmillan; and the National Academy Award for "Moderate Fable." Several drifts are worth tracking. The opium lady is "Minna Weissenbach" here and "Mette Weissenbach" in 1983, and unnamed in 1965. The origin poem shifts again: in 1965 it is a bird-and-nest poem written at six that holds "all the themes"; here the formative first poem is the layette poem of her teens that seeds Esther Longtree. The page count she cites, "1135," is her own round figure. And the flat "not autobiographical" line of the earlier talks is qualified by her own admission that the Knox descent is "an autobiographical note." Across all three, Elinor Wylie keeps returning, as the "orphan angel" phrase, as Benet's citation, and, in 1983, as the model for the unheard deathbed confession behind Mr. Spitzer, which marks her as one of Young's steadiest touchstones.
