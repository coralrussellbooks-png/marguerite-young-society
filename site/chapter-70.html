To All My Darlings

Chapter 70

On Chapter 70 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Chapter 69 · Home · Chapter 71 →

Summary

In the silence after the doctor’s quarrel, Vera half dreams of Mrs. Hogden, a woman she never saw, whose name slides from Hogden to Logden, Fogden, Dogden, and Bogden. During Vera’s absence Mrs. Hogden arrived at Catherine’s house in four coats, hung with temple bells and packages, carrying an empty bird cage and a basket of wormy apples. She had sold her Dutch windmill on Long Island to become the arithmetic teacher Catherine says she never hired. Lisa Lunde, the would-be physicist who replaced Vera as Catherine’s companion, carries messages between the two women and interprets neither to the other. Lodged in the deserted playroom, Mrs. Hogden explores the house, plays chess alone with an atomizer for the missing bishop, drinks, and at last takes to bed as a rival invalid, dreaming the household’s dreams out loud. Mr. Spitzer offers to restore her windmill and more, and she rides away in the mist. In the empty playroom Vera finds, on the little blackboard, a message in red chalk ending, “and therefore, dear heart, all a’s are c’s. You know me.”

Key passages

From the research

  • Vera describes the mysterious and unsettling arrival of Mrs. Hogden, a corporeal yet ghostly figure who invades Vera’s dreams and memories, contrasting sharply with Miss MacIntosh, the former nursemaid. Mrs. Hogden is portrayed as a confusing and contradictory figure (fat, over-dressed, and intrusive) acting almost like an apparition haunting the household and Vera’s psyche. This passage sets the tone of ambiguity and surrealism, highlighting the blurred lines between reality and dream, presence and absence.
  • Mrs. Hogden arrives unexpectedly, burdened with numerous packages and personal belongings, having sold her Dutch windmill home to take up a position supposedly offered by Vera’s mother. Despite her chaotic appearance and peculiar behavior, she is convinced of her rightful place in the household, promising to teach arithmetic and “reflect my mother’s every mood.” The tension between Mrs. Hogden’s assured intrusion and the household’s skepticism is introduced here, particularly through Lisa Lunde’s suspicion and Catherine’s disbelief and rejection.
  • A key passage details the growing argument between Mrs. Hogden and Vera’s mother, who denies ever inviting her. Despite this, Mrs. Hogden knows intimate details of Catherine’s life, suggesting a mysterious or imagined connection. Catherine’s refusal to accept Mrs. Hogden, calling her a liar and impostor, contrasts with Mrs. Hogden’s unwavering optimism and strange knowledge. This conflict symbolizes themes of reality versus illusion, sanity versus madness, and the intrusion of unwanted presences into private life.
  • Mrs. Hogden’s life in the playroom (a place full of forgotten objects and memories) is described in detail. She is isolated, eccentric, and increasingly despondent yet strangely content with her condition. Her behavior of talking to herself, playing chess alone, drinking secretly, and dressing and undressing depicts a tragic figure clinging to illusions of importance and social connection. The portrayal evokes themes of aging, mental decline, and the human need for companionship and recognition.
  • As Mrs. Hogden feigns or experiences illness, taking to her uncomfortable bed, Catherine’s revulsion grows. Catherine refuses to tolerate a “rival invalid” and orders her removal through Mr. Spitzer, the lawyer. Mrs. Hogden’s persistence and Catherine’s harshness underscore the breakdown of relationships and the cruelty often directed at the vulnerable or unwanted. This passage reflects the themes of illness, dependency, and social rejection.
  • Eventually, Mrs. Hogden is expelled from the house with a generous financial settlement arranged by Mr. Spitzer. The aftermath reveals the house’s return to emptiness, the closure of the playroom, and the lingering mystery of Mrs. Hogden’s identity and presence. Vera contemplates Mrs. Hogden’s role as both a real and dreamlike figure, a substitute for lost connections, and a symbol of the fragile boundary between memory and imagination.
  • The chapter closes with the image of the little three-legged blackboard left behind by Mrs. Hogden, inscribed with a cryptic message in red chalk: “All a’s are b’s, it read—all b’s are c’s—and therefore, dear heart, all a’s are c’s. You know me.” The message itself, though logically sound in its first part, becomes ambiguous with the personal address. It’s a statement of universal truth followed by a claim of personal recognition, forcing a re-evaluation of what “knowing” entails in a world where appearances are constantly deceiving. The sentence uses a foundational logical principle only to immediately subvert it with a personal claim of recognition, deeply resonating with the novel’s central themes of illusion, identity, and the elusive nature of truth and self-knowledge.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “She was my mind’s betrayal of my mind.” What does Vera mean by calling Mrs. Hogden a substitute?
  • “If two and two made only four, my mother said, what else was there to talk about?” Why does Catherine want, and then refuse, simple arithmetic?
  • The blackboard reads, “All a’s are b’s, it read—all b’s are c’s—and therefore, dear heart, all a’s are c’s. You know me.” What does “You know me” add to the logic?

Characters

  • Mrs. Hogden “was the least critical human being.” Is that a virtue or a madness?
  • Lisa Lunde “would do nothing to interpret either character to the other.” Why does she enjoy the quarrel?
  • Catherine “would not have a rival invalid in the house.” What is she protecting?

Connections

  • “Her connection with Miss MacIntosh, her predecessor, is only one of a shadow-haunted place, after all.” How are the two women alike and unlike?
  • Mrs. Hogden “dreamed our dreams out loud as if they were hers instead of ours.” Why does that feel like an insult to the household?
  • “Home, James, my good man. Home, James.” Where is home for Mrs. Hogden?

Reflection

  • “Everybody is lonely.” Does the chapter prove it?
  • “She was one of those persons who, disappearing from the earth, would have been missed by nobody at all.” Who in the novel is like her?
  • “Is it possible that any life can be the surface impression only, just no more than it seems to others?” What is your answer?

Research: Characters

Mrs. Hogden/Logden/Fogden/Dogden/Bogden

  • Fat, corpulent, with bleached, plastered curls.
  • Wears multiple coats stacked over each other, eccentric hats, and carries a variety of odd objects (empty birdcage, packages, temple bells).
  • Her face is blandly expressionless except for an aggressive chin and abnormally large, unfocused eyes.
  • Smells of over-ripe apples and is associated with decay and disorder.
  • Wears a water-stained yellow satin nightgown, black fur slippers, and a frilled white night-cap with an aggressive chin bow.
  • Optimistic despite her apparent destitution and displacement.
  • Talks to herself and answers her own questions, which others interpret as madness.
  • Displays a strange serenity and is described as the “least critical human being.”
  • Has an air of maudlin dignity and commands a dubious respect.
  • Exhibits contradictory behavior: vain but neglected, hopeful but delusional.
  • Plays chess alone, often crying over imagined victories or defeats.
  • Has a strong attachment to visual education and arithmetic, though she never succeeds in teaching.
  • Claims to be descended from notable historical figures (Daniel Boone, William the Conqueror, Alexander Graham Bell).
  • Sold a Dutch windmill home to come to Catherine’s house based on a letter of employment that Catherine denies writing.
  • Possibly a shoplifter or of dubious character as viewed by Lisa Lunde, but not confirmed.
  • Mysterious in origin, with no known relatives or friends, disappearing without trace after leaving.

Vera Cartwheel

  • Vera emerges as a deeply introspective, highly imaginative, and somewhat detached individual whose internal world often blurs with external reality. Her character is defined by a profound engagement with dreams, a complex relationship with her past, and a unique way of perceiving and constructing reality.
  • She explicitly states that Mrs. Hogden, a central figure in this chapter, is someone she “never saw,” but rather a “trick of my imagination, this substituting of the one for the other.” Vera acknowledges Mrs. Hogden as a “substitute... for the dream life I wanted” and even calls her “my mind’s betrayal of my mind.” This self-awareness about her mental constructs highlights her active internal world, where figures like Mrs. Hogden go “waddling in and out of my dreams.” She can “see” Mrs. Hogden by simply “closing my eyes, by concentrating.” This constant blurring of waking life, dreams, and imaginative constructs suggests that Vera’s consciousness is the primary lens through which the narrative unfolds, making her a subjective and unreliable, yet compelling, narrator.
  • Vera’s character is also deeply shaped by her past and the figures who influenced her upbringing, particularly Miss MacIntosh and her mother. Miss MacIntosh, Vera’s former nursemaid, “brought me up to understand that my mother’s way of life was far from average and was not to be imitated by a good woman such as she saw me becoming, a woman like herself, simple and average and clear-eyed.” This suggests that Miss MacIntosh tried to instill a sense of conventionality in Vera, despite Vera’s inherent imaginative tendencies (Miss MacIntosh “would have disapproved of, most ardently” of night-dreaming). Vera’s “escaped from my mother’s house” further indicates a strained relationship with her mother, who is portrayed as eccentric and prone to “frenzied dreaming.” Vera’s ongoing comparison of Mrs. Hogden to Miss MacIntosh, even after Miss MacIntosh’s death, shows her lingering attachment to these formative figures and her effort to understand her own identity through their contrasting influences.
  • Despite her imaginative immersion, Vera demonstrates a keen observational and analytical mind, though often applied to her internal world. She meticulously describes Mrs. Hogden’s outlandish appearance and actions, down to the “smashed hat with a wilted ostrich feather” and the “row of Javanese temple bells.” She also notices the “evidence left by Mrs. Hogden” in the playroom, such as “derelict whiskey bottles, empty coat hangers, withered apple cores, broken egg shells, dead flies,” distinguishing even an “artificial eyelash.” Her philosophical reflections, such as her belief in a “small, warped flower growing... outside the consciousness of God,” reveal a mind that questions universal truths and finds significance in anomalies.
  • Vera also conveys a sense of loneliness and a search for meaning or connection. She explicitly states, “The life I required was the life which required, like his, a lost companion —not merely this self-centered affliction of the right hand loving the left.” Her observation that “Everybody is lonely” suggests a deep, shared human experience she identifies with. Mrs. Hogden, as an imagined companion, could be seen as Vera’s attempt to fill this void, even while acknowledging the illusion.
  • Vera’s identity appears somewhat fluid and permeable. Mrs. Hogden mistakenly supposes Vera to be Lisa Lunde, and Vera herself often blurs the lines between her own experiences, her mother’s dreams, and the fabricated reality of Mrs. Hogden. This fluidity contributes to the dreamlike atmosphere of the chapter and underscores Vera’s role as a narrator whose self is not rigidly defined, but rather an evolving construct shaped by memory, imagination, and the enigmatic characters she encounters, both real and imagined.

Catherine Cartwheel

  • Catherine, Vera’s mother, is a profoundly eccentric, wealthy invalid whose grasp on reality is tenuous and fragmented. Her character is defined by a series of contradictions, delusions, and a self-centered, often manipulative, approach to those around her.
  • Initially, Vera establishes Catherine as an unconventional figure, noting that Miss MacIntosh, Vera’s former nursemaid, raised Vera to understand that her “mother’s way of life was far from average and was not to be imitated by a good woman.” Catherine is also revealed as the “true subject of [Vera’s] dreams,” indicating her deep, almost obsessive, significance to Vera.
  • Catherine’s disconnection from reality is a dominant trait. She “dreamed that she was poor,” contrasting with Lisa Lunde’s mother who “dreamed that she was rich.” Her physical and mental state is described as one of profound invalidism, often sinking into “deeper and deeper coma” and experiencing hallucinations, such as “a huge toad was eating my mother’s face” or “the balloon fish floating over my mother’s head,” and “shadowy figures stepped out of the walls. There were moths with human faces.” She herself admits to having “lost my mind several times” and declares, “I am not responsible for my actions.” Her identity is fluid; she believes she has “killed her former self and that my mother’s former self was walking through the house while my mother lay with her hair streaked out like twilight on a pillow, for my mother was dead—added, subtracted, multiplied, divided.”
  • Despite her profound mental disarray, Catherine exhibits moments of surprising shrewdness and practicality, particularly concerning financial matters. She can display a “shrewd gleam in her eyes, the absolute efficiency with which she inquired into her financial concerns, past and present,” even seeking to collect “Old loans which she had forgotten making” from dead parties. Her instruction to meticulously inspect the house for missing items after Mrs. Hogden’s departure further underscores this trait.
  • Catherine’s interactions with Mrs. Hogden, her newly arrived companion, highlight her manipulative and self-centered nature. Initially, she feigns “profound, glazed astonishment,” denying any knowledge of Mrs. Hogden or having sent an invitation, calling her an “impostor, a liar, a day-dreamer, a thief.” Despite Mrs. Hogden possessing intimate details of Catherine’s life like “the head of Alcibiades was in the drainpipe” or “a whale swimming into my mother’s bedroom” which only Catherine could have divulged, Catherine still refuses to see her. She employs elaborate excuses, claiming to be “gone to Boston to see a podiatrist” or “to the North Pole.” She blames Lisa Lunde for Mrs. Hogden’s presence and attempts to discourage Mrs. Hogden by assigning her an uncomfortable “deserted playroom” and a “hospital cot” that was “too short and too narrow.” Her ultimate directive to Mr. Spitzer to “push her off a high building or throw her into the ocean” to get rid of Mrs. Hogden reveals a cruel and extreme impulse.
  • Catherine’s desire for an arithmetic teacher who would teach “Two and two always making four...over and over again” suggests a yearning for simple, undeniable truths in a chaotic personal reality. However, she rejects Mrs. Hogden’s attempts to teach, deeming her “mad as a loon” for repeating the lesson. She views Mrs. Hogden as a “rival invalid” and a “gross, stinking parody” of her own “distended world of shimmering, loosely woven textures,” indicating a fierce protectiveness over her unique inner world.
  • Catherine’s memory and awareness fluctuate. After Mrs. Hogden’s departure, she is “disappointed now that she had not been taught arithmetic and blamed Lisa Lunde,” even considering hiring another “lonely old woman” to teach her. This cyclical nature underscores her persistent detachment from the consequences of her actions and her inability to learn from past experiences.

Lisa Lunde

  • Lisa Lunde is introduced as Vera’s mother’s “young companion who took my place when, carrying a large suitcase, I escaped from my mother’s house.” This immediately establishes her as a replacement for Vera and suggests a practical, perhaps less privileged, background. Indeed, she is described as a “would-be physicist working for her living in order to help support her mother who was poor and mad and dreamed that she was rich,” highlighting her sense of responsibility and pragmatic drive, contrasting with the opulence and delusion of Vera’s mother’s household.
  • Lisa Lunde is characterized by her analytical, skeptical, and often blunt nature. She “prided herself on seeing immediately through Mrs. Hogden’s pretensions” and quickly assesses Mrs. Hogden as a “very dubious character, a shoplifter or something equally vicious.” Her scientific inclination is evident in her desire to “shatter all illusions at one blow,” as when she cruelly declares “Res Tacamah... is nothing but a drug bottle.” She struggles with Mrs. Hogden’s evasiveness, conceding “she was perhaps not a shoplifter... but she did not look like a school-teacher either—there was something so evasive about her, something that evaded definition.” This reflects her need for clear categories and her frustration with ambiguity.
  • Despite her initial skepticism and clear-sightedness, Mrs. Hogden’s unwavering self-assurance eventually sways Lisa Lunde, until she “began to believe that she had come because she was invited.” This suggests a slight vulnerability to the pervasive illusions of the house, or perhaps a weariness from constantly battling them.
  • Her role in the conflict between Vera’s mother and Mrs. Hogden is notable. When Vera’s mother blames her for Mrs. Hogden’s presence and demands she “get rid of her,” Lisa Lunde reveals a more complex, almost vindictive side. “Lisa Lunde, revengeful, began to enjoy the embarrassing situation. She ran back and forth with meaningless messages between the two, and she would do nothing to interpret either character to the other.” This shows a passive-aggressive streak, where she derives satisfaction from the escalating absurdity, perhaps as a form of rebellion against her demanding employer or simply a release from her own constrained life.
  • Lisa Lunde also acts as a voice of conventional sanity, remarking that talking to oneself is fine, “but when you get to answering your own questions—then you are crazy as a loon.” This pragmatic, somewhat judgmental perspective contrasts sharply with the eccentricities of Mrs. Hogden and the delusions of Vera’s mother.
  • Lisa Lunde serves as a grounded, albeit sometimes resentful, observer and participant in the surreal events of the household. She is a foil to the dreamers and the deluded, attempting to apply reason and practicality in a world that largely defies both. Vera’s mother, however, consistently blames Lisa Lunde, even for “keeping Mrs. Hogden out of sight,” underscoring Lisa Lunde’s thankless position as the companion trying to manage an unmanageable reality.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

  • Mrs. Hogden herself is a complex symbol. She represents a kind of spectral presence, both corporeal and ghost-like, who invades the household and disturbs the established order. She is described as a “fat, corporeal, moon-eyed” woman who is not beautiful and who waddled “in and out of my dreams.” She embodies the intrusion of chaos and unwanted reality into a fragile, dreamlike world maintained by Vera’s mother and the memory of Miss MacIntosh.
  • Mrs. Hogden is also symbolic of decay and disorder, her physicality is often described in grotesque and unsettling terms, such as “rolling of loose, misshapen flesh,” “wrinkled breasts hanging like bags of whey,” and “a huge mold of greenish, bluish jelly.” She contrasts sharply with the idealized past represented by Miss MacIntosh and the house itself.
  • Miss MacIntosh and Mrs. Hogden, in their stark contrast, explore different facets of the novel’s central themes. Miss MacIntosh, the champion of stark reality, ultimately reveals a hidden life of profound vulnerability and disillusionment, making her a complex figure of “imperfection” that Vera comes to love. Mrs. Hogden, on the other hand, is a figure of superficial optimism and self-deception, a “grotesque parody” who inadvertently reveals the emptiness and shared nature of the very illusions she seems to embody. Through these two women, Young delves into the intricate relationship between outward appearance and inner truth, the nature of human suffering, and the pervasive power of imagination in shaping individual realities. Vera’s journey of understanding and acceptance, particularly regarding Miss MacIntosh, becomes a quest to reconcile the perceived and the concealed, the dream and the truth.
  • The playroom is a central symbolic space. It is “closed for so many years” and filled with “useless or forgotten objects,” old trunks, umbrellas, and boxes. This room represents a liminal space where the past and present collide, and where forgotten memories and decayed hopes reside. Mrs. Hogden inhabiting this space symbolizes the intrusion of decay and madness into the sanctuary of childhood memories and order. The moonlight that “lit her up” and the “silvery walls” that “shone around her like curled water” give the playroom an eerie, otherworldly quality, blurring the line between reality and dream.
  • Mrs. Hogden’s teaching of arithmetic is symbolized by the apples she brings, some of which are wormy, and later by buttons as substitutes. The apples and buttons stand for the attempt to impose order and reason in a chaotic, irrational world. The wormy apples suggest decay and imperfection beneath the surface of seemingly simple truths. Yet Mrs. Hogden’s faith that “two and two still making four” is a “triumph of reason” symbolizes a desperate clinging to logic and clarity amidst confusion.
  • The radio, described as an old cracked instrument producing static, multiple voices, and unintelligible noises, symbolizes the fragmented and unreliable nature of communication and reality in the house. Mrs. Hogden argues with announcers and imagines conversations among them, reflecting her fractured mental state and the fractured reality in which she dwells.
  • Mr. Res Tacamah, revealed to be a drug bottle, symbolizes the presence of medication, altered consciousness, and perhaps addiction or dependency, linking to the theme of mental instability.
  • The Arctic tern that visits Mrs. Hogden annually at her Dutch windmill home symbolizes a kind of haunting persistence and the natural cycle that contrasts with human loneliness and decay. The tern’s repeated visits, and Mrs. Hogden’s perception of being singled out by it, underline themes of isolation and the inexplicability of fate or nature.
  • Mrs. Hogden sitting “under a frieze of gargoyles” and the idea that “if Mrs. Hogden sat under the frieze of gargoyles long enough, she would become a gargoyle herself” symbolizes the transformation between human and stone, animate and inanimate, reality and myth. The statues and gargoyles represent permanence, desolation, and grotesque beauty, mirroring Mrs. Hogden’s own state and the house’s atmosphere.
  • The ivory-handed back-scratcher which Mrs. Hogden steals, symbolizes a small but tangible connection to the past and the household, a personal token in the midst of disorder. It also symbolizes Mrs. Hogden’s claim to the house and her attempt to assert identity and control.
  • Imagery of Mrs. Hogden wearing “a mask of beauty clay, cracking as it dried, only her eyes exposed, her eyes winking” and her multiple coats, diverse objects, and elaborate, sometimes disheveled wardrobe symbolize the layering of identity, the performance of self, and the blurring of appearance and reality. Her “blondish hair,” “bleached face,” and “plastered curls” depict a constructed and fragile persona.
  • Mrs. Hogden playing chess alone with an atomizer substituting for a bishop symbolizes her solitary struggle to impose order and control. The chessboard and the immovable Black King represent strategies, inevitability, and perhaps the futility of control over one’s fate.
  • Throughout the chapter, there is a recurring motif of dreaming: Mrs. Hogden’s presence in dreams, Vera’s mother dreaming, and the confusion between reality and imagination. Dreams symbolize the fragile boundary between perception and reality, memory and forgetting, sanity and madness.

Physical decay is a pervasive image

  • wormy apples, rotted fish-like faces, dirty and torn clothes, dust, dead flies, and foul smells. This imagery creates a sense of entropy and decline, reflecting the mental and physical deterioration of characters and the household.
  • The repeated emphasis on arithmetic, especially “two and two always making four,” functions as a symbol of order, logic, and certainty. Yet it is juxtaposed against the madness and chaos that Mrs. Hogden and the household embody, highlighting the tension between reason and irrationality.
  • Catherine and Mrs. Hogden, though seemingly antithetical, are deeply intertwined through their relationship with illusion and Vera’s perception.
  • Catherine creates illusions to escape a painful reality, while Mrs. Hogden accepts the house’s illusions as her own reality, finding profound happiness within them. Catherine is the “genuine unbeliever” in reality, while Mrs. Hogden is the “least critical human being.”
  • Catherine is a fading, beautiful ghost, a “dead beauty,” while Mrs. Hogden is an overwhelmingly corporeal, even grotesque, presence. Mrs. Hogden’s physical reality is a “stinking parody” of the house’s dreams.
  • Mrs. Hogden acts as a “substitute” for Miss MacIntosh in Vera’s mind and directly mirrors Catherine’s dreams, often speaking them aloud. Catherine even mistakes Mrs. Hogden for her dead Cousin Hannah, highlighting the fluidity of identity within the house’s dreamscape.
  • Mrs. Hogden’s character serves to “universalize” and “vulgarize” the “rare and individual and hidden” aspects of the house’s dreams, making them commonplace through her uncritical embrace.
  • Both characters, in their distinct ways, explore the novel’s central themes of reality, illusion, memory, and identity, offering different facets of a world where the boundaries between the living and the dead, the real and the imagined, are constantly dissolving.

Research: Resources

  • “there was a faceless woman, a Mrs. Hogden”: Mrs. Hogden is a complex, multifaceted figure who embodies disorder, decay, misplaced optimism, and the collapse of old certainties. She is a grotesque presence in the household after Miss MacIntosh’s departure, representing a stark contrast to the grounded, practical, and clear-eyed Miss MacIntosh. Mrs. Hogden is described as corpulent, overburdened with possessions, dressed in layers of ill-fitting clothes, and surrounded by chaos, both physical and mental. She clings to illusions of social status, authority, and control, despite her evident disarray and marginalization. She is simultaneously comic and tragic, a figure who inhabits a liminal space between reality and dream, presence and absence, sanity and madness. Symbolically, Mrs. Hogden could be seen as representing aspects of American society and history, especially those tied to the decline of certain old social orders and the ambiguous transition to modernity. Mrs. Hogden’s sale of her Dutch windmill, her carrying all her worldly goods in a two-door Ford, and her clinging to relics such as the beaded bag, the birdcage, and the Javanese temple bells evoke the image of a once-privileged class now reduced to decay and disarray. She could symbolize the fading of old American aristocracy or the genteel upper-middle class, who struggled to maintain dignity and status in a changing social landscape. Her presence in the house, with its priceless chairs and frozen grandeur, underscores the tension between fading grandeur and the disorder of decline. Mrs. Hogden’s peripatetic arrival, her confusion about places and people (mixing up Mr. Res Tacamah, a drug bottle, with a gentleman visitor), and her life of loneliness parallel the broader social dislocations in America, including the effects of the Great Depression, the migration from rural to urban areas, and the alienation of individuals in modern capitalist society. Her sense of being unwanted yet persisting reflects the experience of many who were left behind by rapid social and economic change. As a middle-aged woman burdened by social pretensions and personal illusions, Mrs. Hogden represents the constrained roles available to women before the feminist advances of the 1960s. Her role as a supposed “teacher of arithmetic” who never fulfills this role and instead devolves into a caricature of herself suggests the limitations and frustrations imposed on women whose identities were often tied to domestic service or caretaking roles, without autonomy or recognition. Mrs. Hogden’s refusal to acknowledge reality fully: her constant smiling despite ruin, her endless dressing and undressing, her imagining social events and parties in an empty house, and her inability to accept her own decline can be read as a metaphor for American society’s denial of its underlying problems. This denial includes racial segregation, economic inequality, and social injustice that persisted before the civil rights movements and social reforms of the 1960s. Mrs. Hogden may symbolize the marginalized individuals who existed on the peripheries of American prosperity, those who were invisible or ignored by mainstream narratives of progress. Her ambiguous identity (Mrs. Hogden, Logden, or Fogden), her lack of family or friends, and the servants’ refusal to acknowledge her expectations all point to a persona that is both part of and apart from society. Mrs. Hogden symbolically represents the decaying remnants of a bygone American social order, burdened by illusions of grandeur and authority, and caught in the liminal space between past certainties and an uncertain modern future.** She embodies the contradictions of persistence and decline, presence and invisibility, sanity and madness, reflecting the complex historical and social transformations experienced in the United States, including the decline of old aristocratic values, the dislocations of modernity, the constrained roles of women, and the societal denial of persistent inequalities.   • “my basting stitches to hold together surf and shore”: Basting stitches are long, temporary stitches used in sewing to temporarily hold fabric layers together, secure pleats, create gathers, or mark pattern lines before permanent stitching, allowing for easy removal and adjustments without damaging the fabric, often done with a machine’s longest stitch or by hand with a loose running stitch. They are crucial for fitting, easing fabric, and managing slippery materials, replacing pins to avoid bulk or shifting.
  • “It was her great desire that I should be brought up as a kindly extrovert”: The line refers to Miss MacIntosh’s strong wish for Vera to grow up as an outgoing, socially engaged person who interacts kindly and openly with others. Miss MacIntosh wants Vera to be a clear-eyed, active participant in life, focusing on reality and kindness toward others rather than retreating into introspection or fantasy, which she sees as less helpful or even harmful.
    • Extroverts gain energy from social interaction, thrive in groups, are often talkative and assertive, and process thoughts externally (thinking out loud). Introverts expend energy in social situations, need solitude to recharge, prefer deep dives into fewer topics, process internally, and are often described as reserved or introspective, though not necessarily shy. Both types exist on a spectrum, with most people being ambiverts, falling somewhere in the middle.
  • “according to Lisa Lunde, my mother’s young companion”: Lisa Lunde is presented as a sharp, young, aspiring physicist who works to support her poor and mentally ill mother. She occupies a role of both practical caretaker and critical observer within the household, particularly when dealing with Mrs. Hogden, whom she suspects of being a dubious character, possibly a shoplifter or deceiver. Lisa Lunde is skeptical, pragmatic, and often harsh in her judgments, especially toward Mrs. Hogden’s self-assured but chaotic presence. She acts as a foil to Mrs. Hogden’s delusional optimism and to the dreamlike, unstable atmosphere surrounding Vera’s mother, Catherine. Symbolically, Lisa Lunde could represent the emerging rational, scientifically minded, and socially aware American woman or intellectual figure in the early to mid-20th century. Lisa is a “would-be physicist,” embodying the growing presence of women entering scientific and intellectual fields in the US, especially during the early 20th century when women increasingly challenged traditional gender roles. Before 1965, women in science were rare and often marginalized, but they symbolized progress, rationality, and a break from the purely domestic roles prescribed to women in earlier eras. Lisa’s role as a breadwinner and caretaker for her mentally ill mother also reflects the increasing visibility of women as both laborers and family anchors in a changing social landscape. Lisa supports her poor and “mad” mother, who dreams of wealth despite poverty, highlighting the contradictions and struggles of many working-class American families during the early to mid-20th century. Many immigrants or second-generation Americans faced such hardships, balancing ambition and survival. Lisa’s pragmatic, no-nonsense attitude may symbolize the ethos of self-reliance and skepticism toward illusions that characterized many striving Americans. Lisa exposes Mrs. Hogden as potentially a shoplifter and ridicules her pretensions, thus acting as a guardian of social order and truth in the face of delusion and fantasy. This can be read as a broader symbolic role of the rational, modern American citizen who resisted charlatanism and false pretenses in a rapidly modernizing society, especially during the post-Depression and post-war years when social realism and pragmatic attitudes gained prominence. Lisa’s critical view of Mrs. Hogden and the fanciful world around Vera’s mother points to the tension between dream and reality, illusion and fact, an important theme in American culture, especially as the country grappled with modernity, the aftermath of the Great Depression, war, and social change. Lisa embodies the scientific, empirical mindset that confronts myth and deception. Lisa’s skepticism toward the older generation’s illusions (including those of Vera’s mother and Mrs. Hogden) may symbolize the younger generation’s disillusionment with the promises of the American Dream, especially in the years before the social upheavals of the 1960s. Her practical, sometimes harsh demeanor reflects a cultural shift toward realism and away from romantic or fanciful ideals that had dominated earlier eras. Symbolically, Lisa Lunde represents the scientifically educated, pragmatic, socially aware young American woman before 1965 who challenges illusions and embodies the tensions of modernity, class struggle, and generational change in the United States. She is a figure of rationality and skepticism standing in contrast to the dreamlike, unstable figures around her, reflecting broader cultural and historical shifts in American society during the early to mid-20th century. A device that sprays liquid as a fine mist.
Balloon fish
A pufferfish, which swallows water to swell its body into a spiny ball when threatened.
Basting
Long, loose temporary stitches.
Bellrope
A rope pulled to ring a bell, such as one that summons a servant.
Bemused
Puzzled and lost in thought.
Capacious
Roomy; able to hold much.
Chandelier
A branched hanging light.
Cherubim
Winged angelic beings. In art they are often shown as chubby winged children. The singular is cherub.
Chiffon
A sheer, lightweight fabric of silk or nylon.
Corporeal
Having a body; physical.
Cortege
A funeral procession.
Deprivation
The lack or loss of something needed.
Derangement
Disorder, especially of the mind.
Derelict
A person or thing abandoned; a homeless outcast.
Déshabillé
The state of being partly dressed.
Diffused
Spread out widely.
Drove
A herd or flock driven or moving together.
Dubious
Doubtful.
Effigy
A sculpture or model of a person, especially one lying on a tomb.
Errant
Wandering; straying from the proper course.
Ethereal
Light, airy, and delicate, as if not of this world; heavenly.
Extrovert
An outgoing person who draws energy from others.
Fallacy
A mistaken belief, or a flaw in reasoning that makes an argument unsound.
Falsetto
An artificially high voice.
Feigned
Pretended.
Fitful
Starting and stopping; irregular.
Fluted
Marked with long rounded grooves, like a column.
Gargoyle
A carved grotesque figure on a building, often serving as a waterspout.
Grudgingly
Unwillingly.
Hallucinatory
Like a hallucination; seeming real but not there.
Igloo
An Inuit house built of snow blocks.
Immoderate
Excessive; beyond reasonable limits.
Imploring
Begging earnestly.
Impostor
One who pretends to be someone else.
Incorrigible
Beyond correction.
Indisposed
Slightly ill; unwell.
Innumerable
Too many to count.
Interloper
An intruder.
Introversion
A turning inward of the mind.
Iridescence
A rainbow shimmer of colors that change with the angle of view.
Jab
A quick, short punch.
Kaleidoscope
A tube of mirrors and colored glass making changing patterns.
Livid
Bluish-gray, the color of a bruise. Also furiously angry.
Maharajah
A great king or prince in India. From the Sanskrit for “great king.”
Maudlin
Tearfully sentimental, as a drunk can be.
Melodiously
Tunefully.
Minuet
A slow, stately dance in triple time for couples, and the music written for it.
Officious
Pushy in offering help or giving orders.
Omniscient
All-knowing.
Parasitically
Like a parasite, living off another.
Pendulous
Hanging loosely.
Petrified
Turned to stone; also, paralyzed with fear.
Podiatrist
A foot doctor.
Porte-cochere
A covered entrance where a carriage stops to let passengers out.
Precarious
Unstable and likely to fall or fail.
Protracted
Drawn out.
Rhapsodic
Full of rapturous feeling.
Rosette
A rose-shaped decoration of ribbon or lace.
Rotund
Round and plump.
Seraphic
Angelic.
Slumberous
Sleepy; inducing sleep.
Speculative
Thoughtful and wondering; based on guesswork.
Taciturn
Habitually silent; saying little.
Ten cent store
A five-and-ten, a store selling cheap goods, such as Woolworth’s.
Tenement
A crowded, run-down apartment building.
Translucence
The quality of letting light pass through without being clear.
Turret
A small tower on a building or castle.
Urn
A tall vase; also a large metal container with a tap, for serving coffee or tea.
Vapid
Dull and empty of life or interest.
Waterproof
An old word for a raincoat, as in Miss MacIntosh’s “plaid, faded waterproof.”
