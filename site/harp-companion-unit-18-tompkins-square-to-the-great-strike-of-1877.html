Harp Song for a Radical

Unit 18, pp. 473-512: Tompkins Square to the Great Strike of 1877

← Unit 17 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 19 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

The unit opens with the 1874 police riot against the unemployed in New York and builds to July 1877, when a wage cut on the Baltimore and Ohio set off the first national strike in American history. Young follows it from Martinsburg to Baltimore, Pittsburgh, Reading, Chicago, and St. Louis. Along the way she looks ahead to Haymarket, whose martyr Albert Parsons first spoke to strikers in 1877.

Major entries

The Tompkins Square Riot (pp. 476-483)

  • What happened: On January 13, 1874, in the depth of the depression, thousands of unemployed workers gathered in Tompkins Square on the Lower East Side (p. 478) to demand public works jobs and relief. Police had revoked the meeting's permit at the last minute without telling the crowd. Mounted and foot police charged and clubbed men, women, and children.

  • Samuel Gompers: The young cigar maker escaped a police club by leaping into a cellarway. In his autobiography, Seventy Years of Life and Labor (1925), he said the day taught him that radical rhetoric invited repression (p. 481). The lesson shaped the cautious, craft-based American Federation of Labor he later led.

  • Why it matters: The riot made clear that the authorities would treat the unemployed as a threat to order, not a responsibility.

  • Further reading: Herbert G. Gutman, "The Tompkins Square 'Riot' in New York City on January 13, 1874," Labor History (1965).

The Great Railroad Strike of 1877 (pp. 482-512)

  • Martinsburg: On July 16, 1877, firemen and brakemen of the Baltimore and Ohio at Martinsburg, West Virginia, stopped trains after a second ten percent pay cut. The B&O's president, John W. Garrett, was staying at New York's Brevoort House (p. 482). West Virginia's governor, Henry M. Mathews, found his militia sympathetic to the strikers (p. 485), and President Rutherford Hayes sent federal troops.

  • Baltimore and Pittsburgh: In Baltimore on July 20, militia fired on a crowd and killed about ten people. In Pittsburgh on July 21, militia brought from Philadelphia fired into a crowd and killed about twenty. The crowd then burned the Pennsylvania Railroad's yards, destroying more than a hundred locomotives and over a thousand cars (pp. 489-493).

  • Reading and Chicago: At Reading, Gowen's town, militia killed about ten more (p. 503). In Chicago, police and troops fought crowds at the Halsted Street viaduct on July 25-26, and some thirty people died (p. 508). General Philip Sheridan brought regulars back from the Sioux campaigns (p. 503).

  • St. Louis: The Workingmen's Party led a general strike that briefly ran the city, sometimes called the St. Louis Commune.

  • Why it matters: About a hundred people died nationwide. Federal troops broke a strike on a national scale for the first time, and states built armories and expanded the National Guard for the next one. Pennsylvania Railroad president Tom Scott called in the North American Review for federal troops to be used without waiting for governors to ask (p. 504).

  • Debs's position: Debs, then a young officer of the firemen, did not support the strike. He told the firemen's 1877 convention that strikes hurt workers, a view he reversed completely by 1894.

  • Further reading: Robert V. Bruce, 1877: Year of Violence (1959).

Albert Parsons and Haymarket (pp. 476, 500, 509-510)

  • Who he was: Albert R. Parsons (1848-1887), a Confederate veteran from Texas who became a Radical Republican, married Lucy Gonzalez, a woman of Black, Mexican, and Native ancestry, and moved to Chicago. In 1877 he addressed the strikers, lost his printing job, and was warned by Mayor Monroe Heath to leave town (p. 509).

  • Haymarket: On May 4, 1886, during the national eight-hour movement, anarchists held a rally at Chicago's Haymarket Square. As police moved to break it up, someone threw a bomb. Seven police and at least four civilians died, many from police fire. Eight anarchists were convicted with no evidence tying them to the bomb. Parsons, August Spies, Adolph Fischer, and George Engel were hanged on November 11, 1887. Louis Lingg killed himself in his cell.

  • Where the record differs: Young calls him "Alfred Parsons" (p. 509).

  • Further reading: James Green, Death in the Haymarket (2006).

John Hay, Amasa Stone, and *The Bread-Winners (pp. 495-500)

  • Who they were: Hay, Lincoln's former private secretary and later secretary of state, married the daughter of the Cleveland industrialist Amasa Stone. During the 1877 strike Hay managed Stone's business in Cleveland (p. 495) and wrote frightened letters about the mobs.

  • The novel: Hay published The Bread-Winners anonymously in 1883, an anti-union novel in which a strike leader is a scheming villain. Young sets it beside his earlier folk poems, Pike County Ballads (1871), with "Jim Bludso" and "Little Breeches" (pp. 498-499).

  • Amasa Stone: Stone designed the Ashtabula River bridge in Ohio, which collapsed under a passenger train in December 1876, killing about ninety-two people. He died by suicide in 1883.

  • Further reading: John Taliaferro, All the Great Prizes: The Life of John Hay (2013).

Shorter entries

  • The eight-hour movement of 1872 (p. 477): About a hundred thousand New York workers struck for the eight-hour day in the spring of 1872. Employers organized to defeat them.

  • Peter J. McGuire (p. 479): Founder of the United Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners in 1881 and often called the father of Labor Day. Young ties him to "the first Labor Day, May 1, 1886," but the first Labor Day parade was held in New York in September 1882. May 1, 1886, was the national eight-hour strike.

  • Lucien Sanial (p. 479): A French-born leader of the Socialist Labor Party and an editor of its paper, The People.

  • Henry George in San Francisco (p. 482): George wrote Progress and Poverty in San Francisco in 1877-1879.

  • James Ford Rhodes (p. 483): The historian, son of an Ohio coal operator, who recalled the strike as "a thunderbolt out of a clear sky."

  • The Brotherhood of Railroad Brakemen (p. 488): Founded in 1883, later the Brotherhood of Railroad Trainmen. Debs helped organize it.

  • Louise Michel (p. 499): A heroine of the 1871 Paris Commune. Hay mentions her in a letter about an 1883 Paris bread riot.

  • Frederick Dent Grant (p. 503): President Grant's son, an army officer who helped restore order in Chicago.

  • Mr. Dooley (p. 509): Finley Peter Dunne's fictional Irish saloonkeeper on Chicago's Archey Road, whose comic monologues in the Chicago Evening Post from 1893 skewered the powerful.

  • The Chicago Board of Trade (pp. 510-511): The grain exchange whose members, with Pullman and Marshall Field, backed armed citizen patrols in 1877.

  • The Louisville strike (p. 502): Black sewer workers in Louisville struck in 1877 for \$1.50 a day. Young's report of a leader called "Buffalo Bill" is unverified.

  • Herman Melville's Clarel (p. 512): His long 1876 poem of a pilgrimage to the Holy Land, which Young quotes on the injustices of the decade.

Allusions

  • Vesuvius (p. 479): Tompkins Square as a volcano about to erupt.

  • Gethsemane (p. 490): The garden of Christ's agony before his arrest.

  • Shays's Rebellion (p. 510): The 1786-1787 uprising of indebted Massachusetts farmers.

  • The Paris Commune (p. 492): The workers' government of Paris in 1871, crushed with some twenty thousand killed, the specter behind every American strike of the 1870s.

  • Mark Twain's "Jumping Frog" (p. 483): His 1865 tall tale of Calaveras County.

  • Moby-Dick (p. 499): Melville's 1851 novel, for obsession.
