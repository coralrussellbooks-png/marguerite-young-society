Chapter 6. Born on a Grocery Scale

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 5 · The Unfinished Harp: Reading Harp Song for a Radical · Chapter 7 →

Daniel and Marguerite Debs, the Alsatian inheritance, and a town that was not Nowhere

Part Two of Harp Song for a Radical opens on page 179 under the book's own title, "Harp Song for a Radical." After nearly two hundred pages of German tailors, Paris poets, and prairie utopias, Young finally turns to the Debs family. The first thing she tells about them is a burial.

In 1849, Daniel and Marguerite Debs, newly married in New York, lose their first child, a daughter who "could not breathe and had died almost immediately" (181). The baby is buried in the Greenwood graveyard in Brooklyn, "where one day the socialist tailor Weitling would be buried" (182). Part One ended with Weitling lowered into that same ground. Part Two begins with a Debs child placed in it first.

Young makes the connection deliberately, and then adds a detail from the future. Years later, when Eugene Debs was a presidential candidate beloved in the New York tenements, he went looking for his lost sister's grave at Greenwood. Young writes that had he known Weitling lay there too, he would have left flowers, since "his constant habit was to visit the graves of dead children and poets and socialists, he being that kind of permanently elegiac fellow" (182).

That sentence tells a reader what kind of hero this book will have. He is a man who goes looking for graves.

The mill girl and the book lover

Young sketched the parents' story briefly back in Part One (pages 18 to 22), and it is worth recapping it here. Daniel Debs grew up in a tall old house in the shadow of the cathedral in Colmar, in Alsace, the son of a prosperous mill owner. He was sent to school in Paris, where he read French romantics, skeptics, and revolutionaries. Marguerite Bettrich was a poor girl who beat flax and spun thread in his father's mills. His father forbade the match. Daniel would not give her up.

Young's comment on the romance is one of her quietest and most important lines: "Perhaps revolution was always love, love of common humanity, love of the poor" (19). She does not make Daniel a theorist. His radicalism starts with a refusal to accept that a mill owner's son and a mill girl belong to different kinds of people.

Marguerite, Young notes, hesitated for a sensible reason. She "had feared that in America there might be the injustices that there were in France" (19). She was right. Daniel sailed ahead of her in 1848, the year of failed revolutions. On the voyage and in New York, a confidence man talked him out of most of his inheritance, "the French equivalent of six thousand dollars" (20). He met his bride at the dock with roses in his hand and very little money.

The long way to Terre Haute

What follows is a story of repeated loss. After the first baby died, the couple decided to leave the city for Cincinnati. Daniel shipped their linens, silver, and china from Colmar down the Ohio River by flatboat. The flatboat broke loose in a flood and drifted away. "It was never reported as found among the lost" (182). The last physical pieces of their old life simply float out of the story.

They ended up instead in Terre Haute, Indiana, a Wabash River town where Daniel had heard there were many French speakers. What they found was mud and hogs. Hogs on their way to the slaughterhouse were "sometimes so thick in the unpaved clay streets that they were like a heavy tide" (182), and the river itself was sometimes clogged with slaughtered animals.

Daniel had the hands of a scholar, hands that "had not handled an ax but rather the rare leathers of old books" (183). He took work laying railroad ties. After two fourteen-hour days, "the palms of his hands so blistered and bleeding, that he could not hold the handle of the pick" (183), the foreman sent him home. He moved from job to job. A second daughter was born and died before she could be named. A third, Marie Marguerite, survived. The family went back east, where Louise was born, and then west again to Terre Haute.

With "the forty dollars that were left of their earthly capital" (184), they opened a grocery in the front room of a shack in the factory district. Daniel, who had learned from his father how to butcher a hog, worked the chopping block. Eugene Victor Debs was born on November 5, 1855, in the back room of that store, "which had a scale upon which to weigh him" (184).

It is a perfect image for this book. The future labor leader was born among the goods, and weighed on the same scale as the hams and flour. Young refuses to see destiny in it. The future, she writes, was then as always "a permanent enigma wrapped around with mysteries that might or might not unwind themselves" (184).

A town that was somewhere

Young pauses over the history of Terre Haute, and the pause is instructive. The site had once attracted French Jesuits who dreamed of a wilderness paradise of priests and converts. Later, General William Henry Harrison built Fort Harrison nearby and made war on the Native nations of the region, driving them west. The town itself was laid out in 1816, a short time after Father Rapp founded Harmony farther down the same river.

Then Young adds a dry, telling sentence: "This county was not that which should have been called Nowhere" (188).

The word "utopia" means "no place." Part One was a tour of Nowheres, Icaria and Communia and Zion and Harmony, each built as a model of the perfect society and each falling apart. Terre Haute is the opposite. It was built by speculators and settlers, with no angel's footprints and no founding vision. Its county lines were surveyed and recorded. Its streets were full of hogs.

This is The Sacred Is Here in its plainest form. After two hundred pages of paradises, the book's hero is born in a real place, messy and unplanned, among ordinary people trying to make a living. Young still sees the layers beneath it. Under the grocery lie the Jesuits' dream, the Native dead, Harrison's fort, and the Rappite mortgage from Part One. The Past Doesn't Go Away never stops. But this time the top layer is somewhere.

Books on the shelves

The Debs grocery was not a prosperous business, and Young makes clear why. In hard years, when customers had no money, Daniel gave credit or simply handed out a loaf of bread. The shelves "were sometimes better stocked with Daniel's books than with the provisions" (186). Between customers, he read French poetry aloud while Marguerite rocked the cradle at her feet.

He named his son for the two writers he had loved since his student days in Paris, Eugène Sue and Victor Hugo. Young describes what those writers meant to him. They believed that "the lowliest beings of the dust, were a species of divinity" (186), that the poorest people carried within them the living spark of redemption. The name itself was an inheritance, a philosophy handed to a baby.

Then Young records something unexpected. When the old Debs was asked where his revolutionary convictions came from, he did not credit his father's library, with its Rousseau, Voltaire, Saint-Simon, Sue, Hugo, and Heine. "He said quite simply that his mother had taught him to be a revolutionary when he was a little boy at her knee" (186).

Young agrees with him. Marguerite Debs, she says, "was and always had been an instinctive socialist" (186). She was the woman who put up preserves for the store and did not mind her husband wrapping hog jowls in paper printed with revolutionary poems. This is Not One Self at the root of Debs's character. He had two inheritances, his father's books and his mother's practice, and he claimed the second. The theory came from Daniel. The habit of care came from Marguerite.

It may be worth noting that the writer who tells this story was also named Marguerite. Young makes nothing of it. Readers may.

Blaming God instead of a rod

Young sets Debs's birth against the national scene, and one story from the White House stands out. In January 1853, before his inauguration, President-elect Franklin Pierce lost his only surviving son, Benjamin, in a railroad accident. Young notes that "the railroad powers would rather have blamed God than a rod" (185). According to Young, Pierce's grieving wife found comfort in believing the accident was an act of Providence meant to free her husband for the nation's work, and Pierce had his lawyer defend the railroad against the other victims' suits.

This connects back to the opening chapter, where Tom Mooney's father dies of coal-dust lungs, a death the mine owners preferred to call one of the "acts of God." It is Reality Is Constructed again. Calling a disaster an act of God is a way of hiding the people who could have prevented it. A broken rod has an owner. God does not answer lawsuits.

Hog cars full of boys

Debs was five when the Civil War began and ten when it ended, and Young tries to see it through his eyes. Terre Haute, she admits, was not a town especially moved by the suffering of enslaved people. Many of its families came up from the South. Daniel hated slavery, but he also saw something his neighbors missed. The deepest division in the country, he believed, "was not the division between North and South but the division into the rich and the poor" (191). He predicted, correctly in Young's view, that the war would widen that division.

When young Eugene asked why the war was called civil when it was so cruel, he got the answer other Hoosier fathers gave: it was not civil, and not polite. Children of the time, Young notes, pictured the Mason-Dixon Line as a chalk line drawn across the country (191). A boundary that decided who could be owned was, to a child, something anyone could draw.

What stayed with the boy was a single image. "What had mainly impressed him as to the uncivil Civil War were the hog cars packed not with hogs but with boys on their way to the South" (191). Many of them were poor Butternuts, as Southern-born Hoosiers were called. The draft let a prosperous father pay a poor boy to go in his son's place. Young's verdict is blunt: "The most expendable of all the products of the North were men" (192).

This is The Margins Reveal the Truth seen by a child. In a town that shipped hogs to slaughter by rail, the boy watched the same cars carry boys away.

Young balances this with gentler memories. Daniel took his son into the meadows and taught him the names of birds and bees. The boy "had learned to recognize all the wild birds and their songs, which he could accurately imitate throughout his life" (191). Daniel was known around town as the bloody mushroom hunter, because he knew exactly which mushrooms were safe to eat. The war was on the railroad. The father was in the woods, teaching the difference between what nourishes and what kills.

The doctor and his gun

The last chapters of this stretch belong to Dr. Richard Jordan Gatling, an Indianapolis physician who invented the rapid-fire gun that bears his name. Young describes his public reasoning, that a weapon terrible enough would make war unthinkable, as "the humanitarian mask of Dr. Richard Jordan Gatling's idea" (193).

She stages his demonstration in an abandoned Indianapolis graveyard, where one turn of the crank shot every quail out of the long grass. Then she reaches back to Part One. Gatling's patent made him rich, while Weitling, whose buttonhole machine was designed to "punch holes for buttons and not holes in men" (196), died in poverty. The country rewarded the invention that killed and ignored the one that would have spared poor women's eyes.

Young then carries the gun forward through time. Gatlings were turned against strikers at the McCormick works, at Homestead, and in mining towns from Appalachia to Coeur d'Alene and Cripple Creek. And she gives the older Debs, looking back on the Pullman strike of 1894, a bitter line: "the rapid-fire injunction would seem to him a great improvement over the great Gatling gun" (197).

That quip is the Crossing Point of this section. Debs was born into the age of the machine gun, and he would come of age in the age of the court injunction, when a judge's order could break a strike more efficiently than bullets. The weapon changed from a gun to a piece of paper. The people it was aimed at stayed the same.

What the lens shows

These pages turn the book from the dream to the ground. Grief Doesn't End runs through them in the two dead daughters, the lost china, and a father's bleeding hands, none of it resolved or redeemed. The Sacred Is Here is in the grocery scale, the shelves of books, and the mushrooms. Reality Is Constructed shows in the act-of-God excuse and the draft that sold one boy's life for another's. Part One is pressed underneath, from Colmar and 1848 to the Shakespeare Hotel and the Weitling grave, and it has finally surfaced as a boy in a back room in Indiana.

Checking the record

Young's family history agrees with the standard accounts. Eugene Victor Debs was born in Terre Haute on November 5, 1855. His parents had ten children, six of whom survived, and the family's Terre Haute grocery is well documented. Richard Gatling did live in Indianapolis, patented his gun in 1862, and saw it adopted by the Army in 1866. The Chicago Tribune did suggest strychnine as a remedy for tramps during the upheavals of 1877, as Young reports.

Several points need correcting or flagging:

  • Tecumseh at Tippecanoe. Young writes of Harrison's "cowardly attack upon the utopian philosopher Tecumseh and his sleeping red men when they had already surrendered" (187). At the Battle of Tippecanoe in November 1811, Tecumseh was away in the South. The fighting began with a pre-dawn attack on Harrison's camp by warriors following Tecumseh's brother, Tenskwatawa, known as the Prophet. Young's sympathies are clear, but the facts run the other way.

  • Lincoln's repeating gun. Young writes that Lincoln ordered ten "Union Repeating Guns" in October 1861, and she places the order in the middle of her Gatling chapter (194). The Union Repeating Gun, sometimes called the coffee mill gun, was a different weapon, sold by J. D. Mills, and it predated Gatling's invention. Lincoln's order was real. It was simply not an order for Gatlings.

  • The death of Benjamin Pierce. Young says the boy was killed "about a month after the election" (185). The accident happened on January 6, 1853, about two months after Pierce's election.
