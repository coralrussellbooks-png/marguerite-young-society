Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 23

← Chapter 22 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 24 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The Questions

Miss MacIntosh doubts that Caesar crossed the Rubicon, that there was a Caesar at all. Is that the same woman who calls marriage a natural order?

She hates electric light for leaving “no dark corner.” What does the fully lit world lose that the dark corner kept?

If “falsehoods…may be the face of another truth,” has Miss MacIntosh’s own blueprint cracked open?

What is she defending when she defends the darkness?

Miss MacIntosh here is more open than anywhere else, and the openness is startling in her. “Who knows that he crossed the Rubicon or that there was a Caesar? What man alive ever saw him?” she asks; “History lies, and geography is uncertain, unstable as water or a sick lady’s dreams.” This is the plainest woman in the book doubting the plainest facts, seeing received history and settled geography as constructions no one can stand on. She even grants that there are “falsehoods which, though false, may be the face of another truth.” For a moment the blueprint is not sealed at all; she is keeping the construction open, and doing it with more nerve than Vera has yet managed.

But the openness runs one direction only, as it did with Columbus. She doubts Caesar and romanticizes bayberry candles; she unsettles history and enshrines the “middle brow, the middle way, the average life.” Toward the far construction, radical skepticism; toward her own near values, nostalgia and certainty. She is the same divided figure the book has been tracking, open where doubt costs nothing and sealed where she lives, and this chapter shows both halves at their fullest in the same breath.

Her hatred of electric light is the chapter’s surprise, because in it she is partly right by the book’s own lights. She mourns that there is “no dark corner” left, that the lit world has ruined privacy and mystery, exposed the “blushing bride” and flattened the sacred dark into total illumination. This reads as mere nostalgia until one hears what she is defending: the unlit, the not-fully-known, the corner that stays open because no light has finished it. The electric light is the false totality, the claim that everything can be shown and settled and seen. Miss MacIntosh, of all people, is defending the unfinished against it.

So the chapter complicates the sequence that came before. The woman whose plainness was unsealed as a construction turns out to carry, inside that construction, a real instinct for mystery and the open dark. She is not simply the cautionary blueprint. She doubts the received world, she defends the unfinished corner, and she does both while remaining sealed about her middle way. The book will not let her be one thing, which is the point it has been making about her, and everyone, all along.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that flattens Miss MacIntosh into pure cautionary blueprint after the baldness, a sealed figure with nothing open in her, and the reading that takes her nostalgia at face value as simple reaction. Neither the flat warning nor the mere reactionary is the whole of her.

The footing is that Miss MacIntosh is open toward the far construction, doubting Caesar and history with real nerve, and sealed toward her own near values, the same division the book has tracked since Columbus. And her defense of the dark corner against electric light is, against expectation, a defense of the unfinished and the unknown against a false totality, so that even the sealed blueprint carries a genuine instinct for the open. She is open where doubt is cheap and sealed where she lives, and she defends mystery even so. The chapter lets her be both, doubter and nostalgic, without resolving her into one.

For Reflection

Where is one radically skeptical because doubt costs nothing, and certain because the belief is where one lives?

What did the dark corner keep that total illumination loses?

Can a sealed frame still carry, somewhere inside it, a true instinct for the open?
