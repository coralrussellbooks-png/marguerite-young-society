Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 71

← Chapter 70 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 72 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Justice O’Leary · Tabitha · Vera Cartwheel

The doctor's office fills with births that never happen in this chapter, and the interrupted birth and invisibility carry it. Dr. O'Leary keeps instruments for delivery, a wooden arm, and a practice of imaginary pregnant patients, tending deliveries that produce nothing in a room where a "pregnant man" is rumored, and the phantom obstetrics is the empty pregnancy practice the book has run since Chapter 5, birth that will not complete presided over by a madman. The scattered, mateless women's shoes from many eras and places are the women who passed through this barren country and left only one shoe each, invisibility and erasure worked into a floor, and the wooden arm among the instruments is the body as the place the self comes apart set out among the tools of bringing a body into being.

Vera wears Catherine's pearls, the "real pearls" that cannot be lost or stolen, a lost estate carried on the body, and the pearls are women's dispossession and the failure of the record to hold a woman's inheritance folded into an ornament, the mother's fortune reduced to what a daughter can wear around her neck. Catherine's dream, even here, outfits a thief in aristocratic costume and promises to finance a revolution from her drugged bed, the woman's latent, unrealized power flickering under the stupor the world keeps her in.

O'Leary argues on with Sarah, the crawling silent woman he calls dead, and her toy drum and her muteness hold the boundary between life and death open around a dead child who will not leave, the same open threshold the book keeps for everyone it cannot let the world erase. The woman in metallic curlers who accuses Vera of being a "tramp girl," who curses the couples and denounces Jews and Communists and racial minorities, is a widow whose loneliness has curdled into the policing of other women, a female character both marginalized and reproducing the order that marginalizes her, and her half-dressed rage exposes the respectability she is failing to keep.

The landlord's wife, large and goitered, speaks her fragmented dialect of endurance, and Tabitha, the mute chambermaid who cares for the doctor, kneels washing the stone flaggings with her black curly head bowed, her whispered "Good-morning" a fragile assertion of presence. Tabitha is the unacknowledged female labor and the severed voice the book keeps honoring, invisibility and erasure carried by a woman who keeps the place running and is barely seen doing it, one more silent figure kept at the threshold where the book gathers the erased.
