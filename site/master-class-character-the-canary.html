The Canary: The Small Life the World Rested On

← Mr. Chandelier · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Cousin Hannah Freemount-Snowden →

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

After the murder plot and the pearls and the invented chauffeur are all set aside, the book names the thing the mother's life actually hung on, and it is not a person. "It had not been her chauffeur upon whom her life depended, however." It was "a plain little bird," "a yellow canary with a glassy eye and ruffled wing," "sleeping in a gilded cage at her elbow," and on this, her life and everything else depended.

The rule is absolute, and she believes it completely. "So long as this bird lived, she could not die." The canary is "all that had ever stood between her and tides of death," and if it ever went she must go too, "searching through all the realms of death with an empty bird cage." So she tends it like a shrine, a silken bed, a marble bath, "three gold-edged mirrors for the phantom birds," and she guards its sleep, because if it stays calm and unaged then she will stay "as preserved as she had been when she was young and beautiful." "It never sang." The whole delusion of her deathlessness perches on one drugged bird that makes no sound.

Ask the wrong question and the canary is a pathetic detail, an addict's pet. Ask the right one and it is Radical Immanence's load-bearing case, the small real thing at the dead center of a constructed world. Miss MacIntosh could not admit her wig was a wig because the admission was load-bearing, and the mother cannot look closely at her canary for the same reason, because the one glance that told the truth would bring the whole cosmology down.

And the truth is that there is no one bird. The canary "had died already many times without her knowledge," "as changeable as her own dead selves," and it was Mr. Spitzer who arranged "this substitution of the living for the dead," "another canary always in the house, a spare." He warns the servants she must never see "the canary's funeral in the wave-washed garden," or "the whole universe might topple." So the faithful companion on which everything depends is a rotation of interchangeable birds presented as one, "an unbroken continuity of one small life," though "there had been many little yellow canaries in this cage." The mother gives the single bird many names, Juniper and Cellini and Methuselah and Marco Polo, and among them Solomon, the same name Vera hung on her turtle, as if wisdom kept getting handed to the small dumb creatures. She thinks the names are moods of one bird. They are closer to headstones.

The canary is Spitzer's keeping-faith-with-the-dead performed as mercy. He pays a dead man's debts out of his own pocket, and he keeps a dead bird's post filled out of the same refusal to let the dead be gone, and both are done so the survivor need not know. It is the gentlest lie in the book and the most total, a man burying a bird by starlight so a woman can go on believing she cannot die. And when the deceit finally slips, the horror lands the wrong way up. She sees "Marco Polo flying out through an open transom" into a storm, a real bird escaped at last, and instead of terror she is "quite calm, resigned hopelessly" to the fact that now she "must certainly live, of course, forever," because "who could locate one lost canary in a hurricane." The rule she built to keep death away has trapped her in a life she can no longer end. The small life the world rested on has flown, and the world does not topple. It simply goes on, which is worse.

The turn for a writer is where to set the thing everything depends on. A tidy story would rest the mother's survival on her grand invention, the chauffeur with the loaded gun, the drama she authored. Young rests it instead on a bird the mother barely sees, drugged and silent in a cage at her elbow, and then reveals that even the bird is not one bird but a quiet succession no one is allowed to notice. The load-bearing thing is never the vivid thing. It is the small, overlooked, endlessly replaced thing at the edge of the frame, the canary and not the chauffeur, and a writer who wants to know what a character truly cannot lose should look away from the drama she performs and toward the plain creature she keeps without attention. The mother spent her invention on a murderer at the wheel. Her life hung on a bird that never sang, that she never really looked at, that Mr. Spitzer buried again and again in the dark so she would not have to learn what everyone learns, that the thing you cannot live without was mortal all along, and small, and already gone several times over while she slept.
