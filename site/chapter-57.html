To All My Darlings

Chapter 57

On Chapter 57 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 10, 2026

← Chapter 56 · Home · Chapter 58 →

Content note: This chapter mentions the suicide of Mr. Spitzer’s twin brother, Peron.

Summary

A polite, threatening bill keeps arriving from Peron’s tailor for the black funeral suit Mr. Spitzer is sure he paid for in cash, without receipts. In a recurring dream he journeys to pay it twice and finds no tailor, only empty lofts, and wakes from seeing himself in Peron’s brown derby. Awake, he searches Boston for the tailor’s shop, turning left for right because dreams go by opposites. At the Public Library he combs city directories and old newspapers and finds Peron’s death notice and his own name in the society columns, but no tailor and no street. Beside him a man argues with a dead man about the soul, and a young man who cannot be older than twenty swears he took a bullet at Appomattox, where the South won. Outside, at twilight, Mr. Spitzer watches the clouds become a skull, a hand, a bird, and a rose, and wishes for a rose named for both brothers, red and white on a single bush.

Key passages

From the research

  • The recurring motif of the great tailor, possibly mad or non-existent, who supposedly tailored the black funeral suit and sends an incessant bill to Mr. Spitzer, symbolizes Mr. Spitzer’s unresolved debts, guilt, and identity crisis. Despite having paid the funeral expenses, Mr. Spitzer is haunted by the tailor’s persistent demand, which can be interpreted as a metaphor for the burdens of the past that Mr. Spitzer cannot escape. The fact that the suit is associated with his dead brother (Peron) who wore it at the funeral adds layers of grief and identity confusion, as Mr. Spitzer wonders if he himself might be the dead man or the other way around. This passage reveals the theme of blurred boundaries between life and death, self and other, reality and dream.
  • Mr. Spitzer repeatedly dreams of searching for the tailor’s shop, traveling through confusing, shifting streets and cities that resemble a storm-tossed sea or a surreal landscape. This endless, futile search reflects his psychological torment and the elusive nature of truth and memory. The tailor’s shop is described as empty or nonexistent, reinforcing the idea that what Mr. Spitzer pursues may be an illusion or a part of himself lost in time and space. The dream imagery (revolving doors, escalators, pigeons, and contradictory directions) emphasizes disorientation and the fractured nature of his existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer consciously allows for distortions, reversals, and opposites in his search, knowing that dreams operate by opposites: dark is fair, life is death, man is woman. This recognition of duality and the unreliability of sensory impressions underscores the philosophical meditation on reality’s instability and the fluidity between contradictory states. This theme is crucial for understanding Mr. Spitzer’s predicament; he is caught between worlds, identities, and states of being.
  • The scenes in the library reading room, where Mr. Spitzer consults city directories and old newspapers, highlighting his attempt to anchor himself in reality through facts and records, but his efforts are futile, no tailor or street exists. The presence of eccentric characters debating immortality and reconstructing alternate histories (such as the Confederate soldier who claims the South won the Civil War) adds to the surreal atmosphere and reflects Mr. Spitzer’s own existential doubts and confusion about time, history, and death.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s inner desire for absolute certainty and immortality contrasts with his awareness of doubt and confusion. His longing to interrupt the argument about the soul’s existence but refraining out of fear of rejection reveals his loneliness and the human desire for definitive answers about life after death, which remain elusive. This passage deepens the existential questioning central to the chapter.
  • Toward the end of the chapter, Mr. Spitzer’s observation of the twilight sky filled with colorful clouds and shapes (skulls, hands, birds) culminates in the image of a rose that changes from red to white. This rose symbolizes constancy, memory, and perhaps reconciliation of the living and the dead (the two brothers). Mr. Spitzer’s wish for a rose named after him and his brother encapsulates his yearning for remembrance, identity, and peace amid the instability of his existence.
  • Throughout the chapter, Mr. Spitzer struggles to distinguish his dreams from his waking life. He encounters impossible events (dropping a handkerchief in Pisa despite never having been there) and perceives his reflection as a shadow or a pigeon’s murmur. This ambiguity highlights the chapter’s central concern with the fluid boundary between life and death, memory and forgetting, reality and illusion.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “The great tailor was dead, or he was obviously mad.” Who is the great tailor?
  • Mr. Spitzer goes by the rule “that dreams go by opposites.” How does the rule lead him astray?
  • “There had been greater mysteries then than now, Mr. Spitzer believed.” Why does he miss the world before the telegraph?

Characters

  • Mr. Spitzer fears “that he had clothed the wrong man.” Which brother did he bury?
  • A young man insists that “The South had won over the North.” Why does Mr. Spitzer, “a Boston Abolitionist,” only nod?
  • Mr. Spitzer learned that “most people do not want the argument to be settled.” Why does he want certainty when others prefer doubt?

Connections

  • The old librarian “always looked through the wrong pair” of spectacles. Where else in the novel do people see through the wrong lens?
  • Mr. Spitzer dreams of “a red rose and a white rose growing on a single bush.” How does this rose answer the red and white roses of Chapter 56?
  • “So many things had happened to Mr. Spitzer which could not possibly have happened to him.” How does the novel treat memories that cannot be true?

Reflection

  • Mr. Spitzer hopes that “if he waited long enough, there might be no problem.” Do debts to the dead ever expire?
  • He “had yearned for absolute certainty and had wished to be immortal.” Are the two wishes the same?
  • “For how should he be remembered?” What makes a fitting memorial?

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer is grappling with grief, guilt, and confusion after his brother’s death. He is introspective, haunted by recurrent dreams, and obsessed with settling an ambiguous tailor’s bill related to his brother’s funeral suit. He represents the struggle between reality and illusion, life and death.

Peron

  • Peron, Mr. Spitzer’s deceased brother, is described as a gambler and a man of dignity in death. Peron’s identity is intertwined with mystery and symbolic imagery (black suit, red rose, bright red necktie), representing a life ended but still profoundly present in Mr. Spitzer’s mind.

The Great Tailor

  • The Great Tailor may have made the black funeral suit for Peron. He symbolizes unresolved debts, both financial and psychological, and the elusive nature of truth and memory.

Strangers in the Library

  • Various strangers and characters Mr. Spitzer encounters, including a Confederate soldier who claims contradictory historical facts, a librarian, and others who contribute to the dreamlike, surreal atmosphere.

Research: Themes

Identity and the Double

See: Identity and the Self · Twins and Doubles

  • Mr. Spitzer’s identity is fluid and fragmented. He sometimes confuses himself with his brother, dreams of being the tailor, or living a double existence. His struggle to reconcile these selves reflects the complexity of human identity, especially after trauma or death.

Debt and the Past

See: Memory and the Past

  • The persistent tailor’s bill symbolizes the unpayable debts of the past and the burden of memory. Mr. Spitzer’s attempts to pay or deny the debt represent his inability to move beyond grief and the past.

Dream and Waking

See: Dreams and Imagination · Illusion and Reality

  • The narrative blurs the line between waking life and dream, with Mr. Spitzer experiencing continuous, overlapping realities. The chapter questions what is real and what is illusion, emphasizing the instability of perception.

The Soul and Immortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • Discussions on the soul, the afterlife, and the persistence of the dead in memory permeate the text. Mr. Spitzer’s desire for certainty about immortality contrasts with others’ doubts, highlighting existential uncertainty.

Time and History

See: Time

  • The chapter reflects on the passage of time, both personal and historical, with references to changing cities, lost histories, and the impossibility of retracing steps exactly. Time is portrayed as fluid and unstable.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

The Black Funeral Suit and Tailor’s Bill

See: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts · Weddings and Funerals

  • Symbolizes unresolved debts, both literal and metaphorical. The suit represents Peron’s dignity and the past that Mr. Spitzer attempts to honor and settle.

The Penny with Heads on Both Sides

See: Cards, Games, and Chance

  • A talisman of luck and uncertainty, symbolizing Mr. Spitzer’s gamble with fate and his desire for control in an uncontrollable world.

The Rose (Red and White)

See: Roses and Flowers

  • Represents memory, fidelity, and duality. Mr. Spitzer imagines a rose named after himself and his brother, red and white together, symbolizing their intertwined fates and memories. The symbolism of red and white flowers together generally represents unity, harmony, and the balance of opposing concepts like passion and purity. This meaning is most common in Western traditions and varies significantly depending on the cultural context and the specific flowers chosen.

Dream Imagery

  • Recurring images of clouds, city streets, pigeons, skull-shaped clouds, giant hands, and birds evoke the ephemeral and mutable nature of existence.

Timepieces (Two Wrist Watches)

See: Clocks and Watches · Twins and Doubles

  • Represent duality and the conflicting measures of time, cosmic and earthly, highlighting Mr. Spitzer’s fractured perception of time.

Research: Resources

  • “a psychic armor no one now investigates”: The psychoanalyst Wilhelm Reich called the rigid habits of posture and manner that people build against feeling their “character armor” (Character Analysis, 1933).
  • “an insistently polite, threatening note from his brother’s tailor”: See The Great Tailor and the tailor retailored in the Chapter 55 resources. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “for the table was always set for the wanderer”: Compare the old custom of setting an extra place at the table for the unexpected guest or wanderer, kept in Poland on Christmas Eve, and the cup poured for the prophet Elijah at the Passover seder. See Judaism.
  • “alert interest and baited breath”: Young’s spelling puns on the usual phrase, from Shylock: “With bated breath and whispering humbleness” (William Shakespeare, The Merchant of Venice, 1.3). Bated means held back, abated. See Shakespeare.
  • “those old Floradora girls”: Floradora girls may refer to the musical Florodora which ran in London and Broadway in the early 1900s. It was famous for its chorus line of Florodora girls. Bella donna means beautiful woman in Italian. The belladonna plant is poisonous but drops were used by women to dilate their pupils which made them look attractive. The plant’s name, beautiful lady, comes from that use.
  • “horse-hair wigs”: Horsehair has a long history of use in wigs, particularly in 17th-century Europe, with King Louis XIII and Charles II popularizing them as a status symbol and to conceal hair loss or skin conditions. The wigs were initially made of black horse and goat hair but were problematic due to their coarse texture and tendency to attract lice, leading to the development of white horsehair wigs in the late 18th century as a more manageable alternative. Horse-hair wigs will play a role in Miss MacIntosh’s story in the following chapters as we learn more about her background.
  • “The debt, even if there had been a debt which he had forgotten to pay, would be outlawed by time”: A debt no one sues on within a set number of years is barred by the statute of limitations; in Massachusetts the limit for a contract is six years.
  • “to bet with a penny which had heads upon both sides”: The two-headed coin is the oldest of gamblers’ tricks: whoever calls heads cannot lose. See Gambling and Playing Cards.
  • “the same Grand Central Station”: New York’s Grand Central Depot opened in 1871, became Grand Central Station in 1900, and was rebuilt as Grand Central Terminal in 1913.
  • “and he only dreamed that he was dreaming”: “All that we see or seem / Is but a dream within a dream” (Edgar Allan Poe, “A Dream Within a Dream,” 1849). See American Writers.
  • “like his being caught in a revolving door”: Theophilus Van Kannel of Philadelphia patented the revolving door in 1888.
  • “passing himself on an escalator going up as he was going down”: Jesse Reno built a moving inclined stairway at Coney Island in 1896, and the Otis Elevator Company showed its first “escalator” at the Paris Exposition of 1900.
  • “the music of the hurdy-gurdy”: The true hurdy-gurdy is a stringed instrument sounded by a cranked wheel; in America the name went to the barrel organs of street musicians. See Music and Composers.
  • “or perhaps he was the measuring worm”: The measuring worm, or inchworm, is the caterpillar of the geometer moths, whose name means earth-measurers; it moves by looping, as if measuring the ground. See Butterflies and Moths.
  • “Ah, how many gargoyles under the eaves!”: A gargoyle is a carved waterspout, from the French gargouille, throat; the water runs out through its mouth.
  • “well knowing that dreams go by opposites”: The folk saying that dreams go by contraries is old; Freud gave it a theory in The Interpretation of Dreams (1900), where a dream may turn a thing into its opposite. See On dream logic and the logic of the opposite. See Dreams and Imagination.
  • “the three golden apples hanging in the air”: The three golden balls of the pawnbroker, which Young calls golden apples, come from the arms of the Medici and the Lombard bankers and are also said to stand for the three bags of gold Saint Nicholas gave as dowries to three poor sisters.
    • In Greek myth the golden apples grew in the garden of the Hesperides, and Eris’s golden apple, inscribed “to the fairest,” began the Trojan War. See Greek Mythology.
  • “Jews’ harps”: Jews’ harps is a small musical instrument, also known as a jaw harp or mouth harp, that produces sound by the vibration of a flexible metal or bamboo tongue attached to a frame. The player places the frame against their teeth or lips and plucks the tongue, while changing the shape of their mouth to create different pitches and melodies. Despite the name, the instrument has no connection to Jewish people or Judaism and most likely originated in Asia thousands of years ago.
  • “corsets”: Are structured garments designed to alter the body’s silhouette, evolved from Minoan-era forms to the rigid 16th-century stays of French courts and later the Victorian era’s extreme hourglass shape. Used by both men and women for centuries, corsets became associated primarily with women, shaping the waist and supporting the bust. Materials progressed from boning to whalebone, steel, and later synthetic options, while the garment’s role shifted from an undergarment to a symbol of social constraints and, in modern times, a symbol of empowerment or rebellion.
  • “bird-cages”: Date to antiquity, first appearing in Mesopotamia, Egypt, and China for symbolic, religious, and wealth display purposes, with the term “bird cage” emerging in late 15th century Europe. During the 16th and 17th centuries, exotic birds became status symbols, leading to ornate, expensive cages in Europe, a trend that peaked in the Victorian era with elaborate designs. The use of birds in cages has also served practical functions, such as navigation by sailors and safety checks in mines, though their primary role shifted from food source to domestic pets and decorative objects.
  • “knowing, also, that dark is fair, that fair is dark”: “Fair is foul, and foul is fair” (William Shakespeare, Macbeth, 1.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “the great blimp like a silver whale”: Goodyear flew its first passenger blimp, the Pilgrim, in 1925, and its blimps became a fixture over American cities.
  • “Chinese kites with their long ribbons streaming in the seventh heaven”: In Jewish and Islamic tradition there are seven heavens, and the seventh is the highest, nearest God.
  • “bridges of blackbirds”: In the Chinese legend of the Cowherd and the Weaver Girl, magpies form a bridge across the Milky Way once a year, on the seventh day of the seventh month, so the lovers can meet.
  • “the ferris wheels reflected on a horizontal sea of clouds”: George Ferris built the first Ferris wheel, 264 feet high, for the World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893, as America’s answer to the Eiffel Tower.
  • “how many funeral gondolas there were”: Venetian gondolas are black by a law of 1562 against display, and funeral gondolas carried the dead across the lagoon to the cemetery island of San Michele.
  • “how many black swans following the white midnight”: Europeans long used the black swan as a byword for the impossible, after the Roman poet Juvenal’s “rara avis in terris nigroque simillima cygno,” a bird as rare on earth as a black swan (Satires 6.165), until the Dutch explorer Willem de Vlamingh found black swans in western Australia in 1697. See Birds.
  • “mare clausum”: The sea that is under the jurisdiction of a particular country The phrase comes from John Selden’s Mare Clausum (1635), written against Hugo Grotius’s Mare Liberum (1609), the free sea.
  • “He had dropped his handkerchief in Pisa”: Galileo’s pupil Vincenzo Viviani told the story that Galileo dropped balls of different weights from the Leaning Tower of Pisa to show that they fall together.
  • “He had gone, after several days, to the Public Library reading room”: The history of American public libraries began with early private collections and subscription libraries like Benjamin Franklin’s in 1731. The first entirely tax-supported public library was established in Peterborough, New Hampshire, in 1833, followed by the large Boston Public Library in 1854. The late 19th century marked a boom with Andrew Carnegie’s philanthropy, which funded the construction of over 1,600 libraries, and the establishment of the American Library Association (ALA) in 1876 to advocate for their services. Public libraries have since evolved to offer a wide array of services beyond book lending, including educational programs and internet access, becoming a cornerstone of American civic life. Public libraries in the US are open to all and are known as one of the last open places where the people can come in and stay as long as they do not cause a distraction. This has led to vulnerable populations taking shelter there. See On the Boston Public Library as sanctuary.
  • “consulted the city directory”: Boston’s city directories, published from 1789, listed residents and businesses by name and street year by year.
  • “bustles, corsets, shrouds and stays and canvas sails and masts”: Young puns on rigging: shrouds are the ropes that brace a mast from the sides and stays the ropes that brace it fore and aft, so the shrouds and stays of the dead and of the corset sit beside the sails and masts of the ships.
  • “landaus, barouches, phaetons like great sea shells”: A landau is a four-wheel luxury carriage with a roof that can be let down. See Horses and Carriages.
    • Barouches are large, luxurious, four-wheeled carriages.
    • Phaetons were a sporty open carriage. See Phaethon in the Chapter 56 resources.
  • “the one-horse shay”: One-horse shay or one-hoss shay is a light, covered, two-wheeled carriage. It was made famous by American writer Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. He memorialized the shay in his satirical poem “The Deacon’s Masterpiece or The Wonderful One-Hoss Shay.” In the poem, a fictional deacon crafts the titular wonderful one-hoss shay in such a logical way that it could not break down. The shay is constructed from the very best of materials so that each part is as strong as every other part. In Holmes’ humorous, yet “logical” twist, the shay endures for a hundred years and then “went to pieces all at once,— / All at once, and nothing first,— / Just as bubbles do when they burst” (Oliver Wendell Holmes, “The Deacon’s Masterpiece,” in The Autocrat of the Breakfast-Table, 1858). It was built in such a “logical way” that it ran for exactly one hundred years to the day. See American Writers.
  • “rumors of ships which had gone down far from any human eye and might rise silently, ghost ships”: The brigantine Mary Celeste was found in 1872 sailing the Atlantic with no one aboard, her cargo intact and her crew never seen again; the Flying Dutchman is the legend’s ghost ship, doomed to sail forever.
  • “a string of spectacles attached to her bosom, one above the other, one for every kind of distance”: Benjamin Franklin, tired of changing spectacles, had two pairs cut in half and joined, the first bifocals, which he described in a letter of 1785. See Benjamin Franklin and Poor Richard’s Almanack.
  • “it might have been Bedlam”: Is a word referring to a scene of uproar, chaos, and confusion, or an asylum for the mentally ill, derived from the name of London’s Bethlem Royal Hospital, which was a famous psychiatric institution. The hospital’s historical association with treating the mentally ill led its name to become a common term for a madhouse and, by extension, any place of extreme disorder and madness.
  • “the reading room was an Automat”: Is a restaurant where food is purchased from a wall of individual, coin-operated dispensers and retrieved through small, glass-windowed compartments. The concept originated in Europe with the Quisisana restaurant in Berlin and was brought to America by Joseph Horn and Frank Hardart. Automats were popular in cities like New York and Philadelphia in the early to mid-20th century, symbolizing efficiency and speed. They became less popular with the rise of fast food and changing dining preferences but are now remembered with nostalgia. See On the Automat.
  • “whether it is an elixir, a substance, a speck, a dot, a little man”: Philosophers and doctors tried them all: Descartes placed the soul’s seat in the pineal gland, and in 1907 the Massachusetts doctor Duncan MacDougall weighed six dying patients and claimed the soul weighs twenty-one grams.
  • “the musical stars sang”: “When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy” (Job 38:7, KJV). See The Bible.
  • “reconstructing the Civil War—the Battle of Appomattox”: The Battle of Appomattox Court House, fought in Appomattox County, Virginia, on the morning of April 9, 1865, was one of the last battles of the American Civil War. It was the final engagement of Confederate General in Chief, Robert E. Lee, and his Army of Northern Virginia before they surrendered to the Union Army of the Potomac under the Commanding General of the United States Army, Ulysses S. Grant. (Wikipedia) Lee did not surrender in a tent but in the parlor of Wilmer McLean’s house at Appomattox Court House. See On the Confederate soldier and alternative history. See The Civil War.
  • “the flags of the Confederacy”: Refers to several flags used by the Confederate States of America during the Civil War (1861-65), with the most recognizable being the Battle Flag of the Army of Northern Virginia. It was created for battlefield use to distinguish it from the Union flag and later became a symbol for the Lost Cause narrative of Southern heritage, though its use also became tied to white supremacy and racist groups. The flag’s controversial meaning, representing both Southern heritage and a symbol of racism and slavery, has led to ongoing public debates and the removal of official displays. See The Civil War.
  • “the flags of the Grand Union Army”: Young’s Grand Union Army is the Union army of the Civil War, whose veterans formed the Grand Army of the Republic in 1866. The Grand Union Flag, by contrast, was the first flag of the Continental Army, flown from 1775 until the Stars and Stripes was adopted in 1777. See The Civil War.
  • “would always be a Boston Abolitionist”: Boston abolitionists, active from the 1830s to the Civil War, included prominent figures like William Lloyd Garrison, Wendell Phillips, and Lewis Hayden, who formed societies like the New England Anti-Slavery Society and published the anti-slavery newspaper The Liberator. These activists, both Black and white, organized and rallied against slavery, often facing violent opposition from anti-abolitionist mobs in the city. They also supported the Underground Railroad, assisted fugitive slaves, and pushed for social justice within their communities, eventually contributing to the eventual end of slavery. See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “opposed even to the slavery of the moth to the star”: “The desire of the moth for the star, / Of the night for the morrow” (Percy Bysshe Shelley, “One word is too often profaned,” 1821). See Romantic Poets.
  • “these were clouds without water”: “clouds they are without water, carried about of winds” (Jude 1:12, KJV). See The Bible and Clouds and Storms.
  • “Delicate as mountain laurel”: Is a flowering plant native to the eastern United States. Its range stretches from southern Maine to northern Florida, and west to Indiana and Louisiana. Mountain laurel is the state flower of Connecticut and Pennsylvania.
  • “a Sphinx had settled on his coat sleeve”: This sphinx is a sphinx moth, or hawk moth, named for the caterpillar’s habit of rearing up its head like the Great Sphinx. See The Life of the Sphinx Moth in the Chapter 55 resources. See Butterflies and Moths and Ancient Egypt.
  • “He had seen a cloud like a great skull”: Compare Hamlet teasing Polonius: “Do you see yonder cloud that’s almost in shape of a camel?” (William Shakespeare, Hamlet, 3.2). See Shakespeare and Clouds and Storms.
  • “there should be a red rose and a white rose growing on a single bush”: Young’s hybrid exists. The York and Lancaster rose, a damask rose grown since the sixteenth century, bears pink and white flowers on the same bush and is named for the red rose of Lancaster and the white rose of York, whose Wars of the Roses ended when Henry VII joined the two in the Tudor rose. See On the red rose and white rose. See Roses and Flowers.
