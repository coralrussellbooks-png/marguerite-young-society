Chapter 57

On Chapter 57 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 56 · Home · Chapter 58 →

Summary

A bill keeps arriving from the tailor who made Peron's funeral suit, though the tailor and his shop cannot be found. Mr. Spitzer follows directions from his dreams and searches city directories in the library. There he meets a Confederate soldier who believes the South won, and readers debating immortality. He imagines a rose named for both brothers, red and white on a single bush.

Key passages

From the research

  • The recurring motif of the great tailor, possibly mad or non-existent, who supposedly tailored the black funeral suit and sends an incessant bill to Mr. Spitzer, symbolizes Mr. Spitzer’s unresolved debts, guilt, and identity crisis. Despite having paid the funeral expenses, Mr. Spitzer is haunted by the tailor’s persistent demand, which can be interpreted as a metaphor for the burdens of the past that Mr. Spitzer cannot escape. The fact that the suit is associated with his dead brother (Peron) who wore it at the funeral adds layers of grief and identity confusion, as Mr. Spitzer wonders if he himself might be the dead man or the other way around. This passage reveals the theme of blurred boundaries between life and death, self and other, reality and dream.
  • Mr. Spitzer repeatedly dreams of searching for the tailor’s shop, traveling through confusing, shifting streets and cities that resemble a storm-tossed sea or a surreal landscape. This endless, futile search reflects his psychological torment and the elusive nature of truth and memory. The tailor's shop is described as empty or nonexistent, reinforcing the idea that what Mr. Spitzer pursues may be an illusion or a part of himself lost in time and space. The dream imagery (revolving doors, escalators, pigeons, and contradictory directions) emphasizes disorientation and the fractured nature of his existence.
  • Mr. Spitzer consciously allows for distortions, reversals, and opposites in his search, knowing that dreams operate by opposites: dark is fair, life is death, man is woman. This recognition of duality and the unreliability of sensory impressions underscores the philosophical meditation on reality’s instability and the fluidity between contradictory states. This theme is crucial for understanding Mr. Spitzer’s predicament; he is caught between worlds, identities, and states of being.
  • The scenes in the library reading room, where Mr. Spitzer consults city directories and old newspapers, highlighting his attempt to anchor himself in reality through facts and records, but his efforts are futile, no tailor or street exists. The presence of eccentric characters debating immortality and reconstructing alternate histories (such as the Confederate soldier who claims the South won the Civil War) adds to the surreal atmosphere and reflects Mr. Spitzer’s own existential doubts and confusion about time, history, and death.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s inner desire for absolute certainty and immortality contrasts with his awareness of doubt and confusion. His longing to interrupt the argument about the soul’s existence but refraining out of fear of rejection reveals his loneliness and the human desire for definitive answers about life after death, which remain elusive. This passage deepens the existential questioning central to the chapter.
  • Toward the end of the chapter, Mr. Spitzer’s observation of the twilight sky filled with colorful clouds and shapes (skulls, hands, birds) culminates in the image of a rose that changes from red to white. This rose symbolizes constancy, memory, and perhaps reconciliation of the living and the dead (the two brothers). Mr. Spitzer’s wish for a rose named after him and his brother encapsulates his yearning for remembrance, identity, and peace amid the instability of his existence.
  • Throughout the chapter, Mr. Spitzer struggles to distinguish his dreams from his waking life. He encounters impossible events (dropping a handkerchief in Pisa despite never having been there) and perceives his reflection as a shadow or a pigeon’s murmur. This ambiguity highlights the chapter’s central concern with the fluid boundary between life and death, memory and forgetting, reality and illusion.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer is grappling with grief, guilt, and confusion after his brother’s death. He is introspective, haunted by recurrent dreams, and obsessed with settling an ambiguous tailor’s bill related to his brother’s funeral suit. He represents the struggle between reality and illusion, life and death.

Peron

  • Peron, Mr. Spitzer’s deceased brother, is described as a gambler and a man of dignity in death. Peron’s identity is intertwined with mystery and symbolic imagery (black suit, red rose, bright red necktie), representing a life ended but still profoundly present in Mr. Spitzer’s mind.
  • The Great Tailor may have made the black funeral suit for Peron. He symbolizes unresolved debts, both financial and psychological, and the elusive nature of truth and memory.
  • Various strangers and characters Mr. Spitzer encounters, including a Confederate soldier who claims contradictory historical facts, a librarian, and others who contribute to the dreamlike, surreal atmosphere.

Research: Themes

  • Mr. Spitzer’s identity is fluid and fragmented. He sometimes confuses himself with his brother, dreams of being the tailor, or living a double existence. His struggle to reconcile these selves reflects the complexity of human identity, especially after trauma or death.
  • The persistent tailor’s bill symbolizes the unpayable debts of the past and the burden of memory. Mr. Spitzer’s attempts to pay or deny the debt represent his inability to move beyond grief and the past.
  • The narrative blurs the line between waking life and dream, with Mr. Spitzer experiencing continuous, overlapping realities. The chapter questions what is real and what is illusion, emphasizing the instability of perception.
  • Discussions on the soul, the afterlife, and the persistence of the dead in memory permeate the text. Mr. Spitzer’s desire for certainty about immortality contrasts with others’ doubts, highlighting existential uncertainty.
  • The chapter reflects on the passage of time, both personal and historical, with references to changing cities, lost histories, and the impossibility of retracing steps exactly. Time is portrayed as fluid and unstable.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

The Black Funeral Suit and Tailor’s Bill

See: Clothing, Hats, and Skirts · Weddings and Funerals

  • Symbolizes unresolved debts, both literal and metaphorical. The suit represents Peron’s dignity and the past that Mr. Spitzer attempts to honor and settle.

The Penny with Heads on Both Sides

See: Cards, Games, and Chance

  • A talisman of luck and uncertainty, symbolizing Mr. Spitzer’s gamble with fate and his desire for control in an uncontrollable world.

The Rose (Red and White)

See: Roses and Flowers

  • Represents memory, fidelity, and duality. Mr. Spitzer imagines a rose named after himself and his brother, red and white together, symbolizing their intertwined fates and memories. The symbolism of red and white flowers together generally represents unity, harmony, and the balance of opposing concepts like passion and purity. This meaning is most common in Western traditions and varies significantly depending on the cultural context and the specific flowers chosen.

Dream Imagery

  • Recurring images of clouds, city streets, pigeons, skull-shaped clouds, giant hands, and birds evoke the ephemeral and mutable nature of existence.

Timepieces (Two Wrist Watches)

See: Clocks and Watches · Twins and Doubles

  • Represent duality and the conflicting measures of time, cosmic and earthly, highlighting Mr. Spitzer’s fractured perception of time.

Research: Resources

  • Floradora girls may refer to the musical Florodora which ran in London and Broadway in the early 1900s. It was famous for its chorus line of Florodora girls. Bella donna means beautiful woman in Italian. The belladonna plant is poisonous but drops were used by women to dilate their pupils which made them look attractive.
  • Three golden apples - It metaphorically signifies the precious, idealized dreams or hopes from a golden age or future that the narrator contemplates amidst the themes of loss, searching, and the tension between dream and reality.
  • It is used in many folklore tales and legends. In one Greek legend the golden apple can be seen as a metaphor for a practical joke meant to cause cognitive dissonance in the target. It may also refer to balls as in the traditional, universal symbol for a pawn shop, with origins in the story of Saint Nicholas and possibly linked to the crest of the Medici family or Lombard merchants. The symbol represents gold coins or bags of money, often said to be the gold Nicholas gave to a poor man's daughters or the gold coins used in medieval banking.
  • Jews’ harps is a small musical instrument, also known as a jaw harp or mouth harp, that produces sound by the vibration of a flexible metal or bamboo tongue attached to a frame. The player places the frame against their teeth or lips and plucks the tongue, while changing the shape of their mouth to create different pitches and melodies. Despite the name, the instrument has no connection to Jewish people or Judaism and most likely originated in Asia thousands of years ago.
  • “horse-hair wigs” Horsehair has a long history of use in wigs, particularly in 17th-century Europe, with King Louis XIII and Charles II popularizing them as a status symbol and to conceal hair loss or skin conditions. The wigs were initially made of black horse and goat hair but were problematic due to their coarse texture and tendency to attract lice, leading to the development of white horsehair wigs in the late 18th century as a more manageable alternative. Horse-hair wigs will play a role in Miss MacIntosh’s story in the following chapters as we learn more about her background.
  • “corsets” are structured garments designed to alter the body's silhouette, evolved from Minoan-era forms to the rigid 16th-century stays of French courts and later the Victorian era's extreme hourglass shape. Used by both men and women for centuries, corsets became associated primarily with women, shaping the waist and supporting the bust. Materials progressed from boning to whalebone, steel, and later synthetic options, while the garment's role shifted from an undergarment to a symbol of social constraints and, in modern times, a symbol of empowerment or rebellion.
  • “bird-cages” date to antiquity, first appearing in Mesopotamia, Egypt, and China for symbolic, religious, and wealth display purposes, with the term "bird cage" emerging in late 15th century Europe. During the 16th and 17th centuries, exotic birds became status symbols, leading to ornate, expensive cages in Europe, a trend that peaked in the Victorian era with elaborate designs. The use of birds in cages has also served practical functions, such as navigation by sailors and safety checks in mines, though their primary role shifted from food source to domestic pets and decorative objects.
  • A landaus is a four-wheel luxury carriage with a roof that can be let down.
  • Barouches are large, luxurious, four-wheeled carriages.
  • Phaetons were a sporty open carriage.
  • One-horse shay or one-hoss shay is a light, covered, two-wheeled carriage. It was made famous by American writer Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. He memorialized the shay in his satirical poem "The Deacon's Masterpiece or The Wonderful One-Hoss Shay". In the poem, a fictional deacon crafts the titular wonderful one-hoss shay in such a logical way that it could not break down. The shay is constructed from the very best of materials so that each part is as strong as every other part. In Holmes' humorous, yet "logical" twist, the shay endures for a hundred years then it "went to pieces all at once, and nothing first, — just as bubbles do when they burst". It was built in such a "logical way" that it ran for exactly one hundred years to the day.
  • The history of American public libraries began with early private collections and subscription libraries like Benjamin Franklin's in 1731. The first entirely tax-supported public library was established in Peterborough, New Hampshire, in 1833, followed by the large Boston Public Library in 1854. The late 19th century marked a boom with Andrew Carnegie's philanthropy, which funded the construction of over 1,600 libraries, and the establishment of the American Library Association (ALA) in 1876 to advocate for their services. Public libraries have since evolved to offer a wide array of services beyond book lending, including educational programs and internet access, becoming a cornerstone of American civic life. Public libraries in the US are open to all and are known as one of the last open places where the people can come in and stay as long as they do not cause a distraction. This has led to vulnerable populations taking shelter there.
  • The Battle of Appomattox Court House, fought in Appomattox County, Virginia, on the morning of April 9, 1865, was one of the last battles of the American Civil War. It was the final engagement of Confederate General in Chief, Robert E. Lee, and his Army of Northern Virginia before they surrendered to the Union Army of the Potomac under the Commanding General of the United States Army, Ulysses S. Grant.
  • Boston abolitionists, active from the 1830s to the Civil War, included prominent figures like William Lloyd Garrison, Wendell Phillips, and Lewis Hayden, who formed societies like the New England Anti-Slavery Society and published the anti-slavery newspaper The Liberator. These activists, both Black and white, organized and rallied against slavery, often facing violent opposition from anti-abolitionist mobs in the city. They also supported the Underground Railroad, assisted fugitive slaves, and pushed for social justice within their communities, eventually contributing to the eventual end of slavery.
  • "for the table was always set for the wanderer, set with plates of silver and plates of gold and oyster forks and goblets" is a reference to a passage from the 1872 book The Poet at the Breakfast Table by Oliver Wendell Holmes. The phrase metaphorically represents the dreamlike suspension of time and social reality where the rituals of life continue in a symbolic fashion despite physical withdrawal from the living world.
  • “mare clausum” the sea that is under the jurisdiction of a particular country
  • "Bedlam" is a word referring to a scene of uproar, chaos, and confusion, or an asylum for the mentally ill, derived from the name of London's Bethlem Royal Hospital, which was a famous psychiatric institution. The hospital's historical association with treating the mentally ill led its name to become a common term for a madhouse and, by extension, any place of extreme disorder and madness.
  • “Automat” is a restaurant where food is purchased from a wall of individual, coin-operated dispensers and retrieved through small, glass-windowed compartments. The concept originated in Europe with the Quisisana restaurant in Berlin and was brought to America by Joseph Horn and Frank Hardart. Automats were popular in cities like New York and Philadelphia in the early to mid-20th century, symbolizing efficiency and speed. They became less popular with the rise of fast food and changing dining preferences but are now remembered with nostalgia.
  • "flags of the Confederacy" refers to several flags used by the Confederate States of America during the Civil War (1861-65), with the most recognizable being the Battle Flag of the Army of Northern Virginia. It was created for battlefield use to distinguish it from the Union flag and later became a symbol for the Lost Cause narrative of Southern heritage, though its use also became tied to white supremacy and racist groups. The flag's controversial meaning, representing both Southern heritage and a symbol of racism and slavery, has led to ongoing public debates and the removal of official displays.
  • ‘flags of the Grand Union Army” also known as the Continental Colors, was the first national flag of the United States, featuring 13 alternating red and white stripes with the British Union Jack in the canton, symbolizing the colonies' transition from British subjects to independent states. It was first flown in late 1775 and used by the Continental Army and Navy until the Stars and Stripes was adopted in 1777.
  • “mountain laurel’ is a flowering plant native to the eastern United States. Its range stretches from southern Maine to northern Florida, and west to Indiana and Louisiana. Mountain laurel is the state flower of Connecticut and Pennsylvania.
  • Sphinx began in ancient Egypt, where sphinxes were depicted as mythical guardians, typically with a lion's body and a human head, often wearing a pharaoh's headdress. The Great Sphinx of Giza, a monumental statue carved from the bedrock, is the most famous example, though its exact construction date and builder are still debated, with most theories placing it between 2600 and 2500 BCE. While the Great Sphinx primarily served as a spiritual guardian for the pyramids and monuments of Giza, the concept of the sphinx also appeared in Greek mythology, where it was a more monstrous and treacherous figure.
