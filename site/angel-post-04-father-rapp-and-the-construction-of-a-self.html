Chapter 4. Father Rapp and the Construction of a Self

Part of a reading of Angel in the Forest (1945), Marguerite Young’s history of the two failed utopias at New Harmony, Indiana.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 3 · Angel in the Forest: A Reading in Fourteen Chapters · Chapter 5 →

Marguerite Young / Angel in the Forest, Chapter 4

If you wanted to design the most extreme possible attempt to make many people into one person, you could hardly do better than George Rapp's community. Everything this study has been circling, the plural self, the constructed reality, the suppression of difference, has a limit case, and Young found it in the celibate order that built the first Harmony. The Rappites did not merely encourage conformity. They set out to erase the individual so completely that the members would become identical, sexless, and interchangeable, a single angelic type repeated seven hundred times. The interest of Young's account is that she shows exactly what that project costs, and exactly why it cannot hold.

The theology and the machine

Rapp's people believed they were becoming angels. Not metaphorically. They held that the true human state was asexual, that Adam's fall was sexual reproduction itself, and that to climb back toward the angelic condition a person had to give up the body's continuation entirely. From this followed total celibacy, enforced across the whole community, and from that, in Young's flat phrasing, the theology afforded an excuse for the most drastic measures, even emasculation. She does not look away from this and she does not soften it. She lets you see the full brutality of a belief followed all the way to its end.

What she notices, and what makes the chapter more than a horror story, is that the theology and the account book are the same machine. She points out that before the edict of celibacy, Rappite babies had arrived at regular intervals but never at harvest time, and draws the dry conclusion that Rapp's harmony was a form of economy. The suppression of sex was also the suppression of any drain on the corporation. The producer, she writes, is forced to lop off his own branch of the tree of life so that the books may show a steady increase in earnings. Rapp had learned from the new preachers who talked about birth control and soil preservation, and he harnessed the new science to the old religion. From complete birth control, Young writes, it was a short road to the complete death of the individual will and the simplified angelic state, its weakest echo. The angel and the ledger want the same thing: a member who takes up no room of his own, who does not reproduce, who does not want, who sinks, in her exact words, to the level of the general desire. That sentence sums up the entire logic of the community. If the community is to be preserved, the individual must be destroyed. Once you accept that premise, everything that follows, the celibacy, the emasculation, the nameless graves, proceeds with a horrible internal consistency. Young's achievement is to show you that consistency from the inside, so that you understand it even as it appalls you.

This is the political point the philosophy names when it says the demand for a single fixed identity is not natural but a tool. Systems that need to sort people need simple people. Rapp needed future angels, maximally identical, maximally sortable, all facing the same direction. The seven hundred actual human beings underneath that demand were the raw material the machine existed to simplify.

How Young stays in the room

The easy thing would be to file Rapp as a monster or to rescue him as a misunderstood visionary. Young does neither, and her refusal is itself a method. Her Rapp is grotesque and, in his own way, great, a tyrant and a mountebank and also unforgettable, a man she will forgive even his monstrous theories of sex without ever pretending they were anything but monstrous. She holds the two judgments at once and lets neither win.

The clearest case is the angel. Rapp said he had seen an angel descend in the old Harmony in Pennsylvania, and that it left its footprints pressed into a slab of stone. Was the angel real? Was Rapp a madman, or a liar, or a man who genuinely saw something? Young allows the question to float and never brings it down. She does not debunk the angel and she does not endorse it. She keeps it suspended as a permanent condition of the book, because the honest answer is that we cannot know, and pretending otherwise, in either direction, would be a lie about the limits of what a history can settle. Staying in the room with an unresolved thing, refusing to force it into a verdict, is the discipline this philosophy calls the witness, and the angel is where Young practices it most purely.

Frederick, the seam in the leadership

The proof that Rapp's system could not hold its own members is standing right next to Rapp the entire time. Frederick, his adopted son and the community's lord temporal, the number two man in the whole order, was a pantheist and a lover of nature living in the heart of a community organized around denying nature. He believed that God is all, that God is everywhere, that the whole body of the universe was the body of God, a huge animal without eyes, because there is nothing outside God for God to look on, not even one small, wet violet.

Read what that belief actually reveres. Frederick's God exists permanently and inseparably as an essential, natural and intrinsic part of everything, from the catkins of the willow and the buds of the ash, to the mayfly that lives a day and the whale that lives four hundred years, to the flower distended for the bee and the sexual juices and the endless business of procreation. He worshipped exactly the fecundity his community existed to renounce. The harmony he loved was, in Young's phrase, larger than Harmony, the whole teeming reproductive world that Rapp had declared to be the fall. And this man was not an outsider or a dissident. He was the co-ruler. The seam does not show at the edge of the community, among some marginal heretic. It shows at the very top, in the second most powerful person in the order. A schema that cannot contain its own lord temporal without contradiction is a schema that was never holding anything. It was only pressing down, and the pressure was visible in the leadership itself.

The Coming of One

The deepest illustration of all this is the chapter Young calls "The Coming of One," and it deserves to be told in full, because it is the perfect experiment: what happens to a community organized entirely around awaiting a single redemptive figure when one actually arrives.

The Rappites had spent decades waiting for the One, the promised figure who would confirm everything. In July of 1829 a magnificently embossed letter reached them from Frankfurt, announcing Count Maximilian de Leon, the Great Ambassador and Anointed One of God, descendant of the Stem of Judah and the Root of David, child of Abraham and Isaac and Solomon, enemy of Cain and Herod and Pontius Pilate, came to America as the last hope of salvation. Rapp, flattered, invited him. It took two years for the Count to cross. When he finally rolled into Economy on a fine autumn day in 1831, the whole community lined the streets with offerings of late flowers while the band played from the church tower, and the herdsmen blew their horns three times as if to welcome a heavenly host.

Out of the curtained carriage stepped the long-expected One: a corpulent, fat gentleman, nothing ethereal, in purple and green broadcloth, blond curls draped over his collar, a golden book in his green-gloved hands. Three chins. Kid gloves. Peacock struttings. His real name was Bernard Mueller. He was a traveling salesman with no means but the borrowed clothes on his back and, in Young's devastating phrase, the idea of happiness.

The arrival of the promised One did not seal the single-self project. It detonated it. The Count did not preach uniformity. He went through Economy person by person, learning each member's private history, and then he climbed into the pulpit and preached the exact opposite of everything Rapp had built. Every man, he said, was a world shut inside himself, onion skin within onion skin, mystery within mystery, which only God could peel. He asked the questions the individual asks when it reasserts itself against the organization that has been imposed: why were the graves nameless, where were the unborn children, who would carry on the family names, why had no one ever been shown the corporation's books. And he offered what Rapp had spent his life abolishing: marriage, the body, individual desire, restored not at the end of time but here and now.

A third of the community left with him. Of seven hundred members, two hundred fifty deserted, husband and wife retiring to separate camps, brother and sister taking opposite sides. It happened, Young writes, quietly, with no argument, only a division of the odd from the even, the fat from the lean. And in that division something is revealed that Rapp had spent decades hiding: the community was never one self. It was seven hundred plural people held under pressure, and the instant a figure arrived who named them as separate mysteries, a third of them came apart from the whole. The Count was, in Young's pun, a judgment on economy, on the town and on the entire economy of selfhood-suppression that built it. He was the return of everything Rapp had repressed, and he arrived, devastatingly, wearing the costume the system itself had taught everyone to wait for. The machine of uniformity contained the seed of its own shattering, because the raw material was never uniform to begin with.

Neither one works

Young does not turn the Count into a hero, which is the final proof of her honesty. The restoration of the flesh he offered was its own fiasco. He was a fraud and a bachelor and a gambler, interested in no one but himself and possibly his secretary. He blew through the settlement money in six months, his followers turned into a band of scarecrows squatting on bare ground, and when the Leonites tried to raid Economy for its hams they were driven off by the Rappite women with hot water and brooms and kicks. The Count absconded down the Red River and died of cholera. His community of honeymooners failed as completely as Rapp's community of angels.

That is the shape of Young's witness. She watches the machine that destroys the individual, and she watches the counter-figure who promises to liberate the individual, and she lets both of them fail without choosing a winner. She does not resolve Rapp into a villain or the Count into a savior. She keeps them both suspended, exactly as she keeps the angel suspended, because the truth is that neither the suppression of the plural self nor its cheap liberation actually delivered what it promised.

What Rapp's case leaves us with is the first and hardest lesson of the book. You cannot make many people into one self. You can demand it, enforce it, build a theology and an account book and a celibate order around it, press seven hundred people down toward a single angelic type for decades. And the plurality you suppress does not go away. It waits underneath, and it comes back, sometimes in the very form your own longing prepared for it, and when it comes back it takes a third of everything with it.
