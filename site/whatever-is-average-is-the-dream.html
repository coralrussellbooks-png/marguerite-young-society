Movement Six: What the Novel Teaches

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · The Stone Deaf Man · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Dreams and Imagination

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Plato and Platonism

Allusions: Deafness and Sign Language · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · The History of Opium

Definition from research

The novel's last philosophical statement is delivered in two sentences: "Whatever is average is the dream. Whatever is hidden is the revealed." Vera speaks this near the end, reflecting on Miss MacIntosh, on the stone deaf man she has chosen to love, on the entire journey the novel has been on. They are not a resolution. They are the arrival at a paradox that has been building since page one, the paradox that is the AND at the center of Young's entire philosophical project: the ordinary and the extraordinary are not opposites. They are each other's deepest form.

Miss MacIntosh was the average. She was from What Cheer, Iowa, which she described as very ordinary, a small town like any other, where all but a few atheists went to church and they were very kind people, where the winters were cold and the children's ears were frozen and one broke through the frosty ground with a hoe. She was plain, practical, sensible, unremarkable in every way she insisted on being unremarkable. She was "the sensible and average and right." She was the dream. Vera did not understand this until the novel's end because the dream of a grounded ordinary life was what Vera most needed and least recognized, hidden in plain sight behind its own ordinariness. "Had I been less wrapped up in myself, I might always have known her, the other soul, its power, its enchantment."

The plural self pillar arrives at this paradox through characterization. Miss MacIntosh is not one person. She is the practical nursemaid and also the woman with a mastectomy and also the figure who walked into the sea and also the face of every face and the heart of every heart. But none of these other dimensions are available until you have accepted the plain surface first. Young spends the novel's opening chapters insisting on Miss MacIntosh's ordinariness with a kind of aggressive repetition that trains the reader to see her as she presents herself: common sense, flat feet, broken nose, the path of virtue rugged and hard. Only after that training has taken hold does the depth beneath the surface begin to emerge. The average conceals the dream. The ordinary is the door into everything Young is building, but it is a door that only opens if you approach it without impatience, without the desire to skip to the revelation.

Illusion as primary is where the reversal becomes most visible architecturally. Catherine's opium world is elaborate, populated, spectacular, inexhaustible in its invention. It is full of Platonists and drowned sailors and centaurs with lilies in their mouths. It is the opposite of average. And yet it is not the dream. It is the escape from dreaming, the place where the dreamer goes to avoid the dream the ordinary world contains. Catherine cannot bear the ordinary, which means she cannot bear the dream. She lives in a world of extraordinary illusions precisely because she has refused the average, and the refusal is what keeps her from the thing she most needs. What is hidden is the revealed: the truth about Miss MacIntosh, who she actually was, was hidden in her plainness all along. Catherine's spectacular phantoms are visible but opaque. Miss MacIntosh's plain fact is invisible but transparent, once you know how to look.

The grief as permanent weather pillar arrives at the paradox through the stone deaf man. He lost his hearing at fourteen in a sleep so near to death it might almost have been his death. He woke into silence. The human voice went first, then everything else, until there was only the memory of sound and a world of blurred shapes. He became a painter. He heard what others heard not, the fishes whispering at the bottoms of rivers. A butterfly made a loud noise wheeling around his head. He had traveled all over America and never heard a traffic horn, which is to say he had been spared everything ordinary about the noisy world and had been given instead a world of pure imagination in which silence was his permanent weather and everything he heard was internal. He is the novel's most extreme image of the hidden revealed: his deafness is the condition that reveals what hearing conceals, that the world is more beautiful and more strange than the traffic horn allows.

Vera's choice of the stone deaf man is not resignation and it is not settling. It is the application of the novel's final lesson. She has spent the entire book searching for Miss MacIntosh, for the ordinary world that Miss MacIntosh represented, the world of plain common sense where the dream was supposed to be absent. She arrives instead at the understanding that the ordinary world she sought was always already the dream, that Miss MacIntosh's plainness was itself the extraordinary thing, and that the man she loves is extraordinary precisely through his ordinariness transformed by loss into something else. He is the impossible artist who became a painter when sound died. He is the average, the stone deaf man swaying in a corridor at dawn, and he is also the dream.

The witness pillar closes the novel in the figure of the old Black woman who tells the newly deaf boy to dream on. Her speech is the only moment in the novel where Young gives a character the direct authority to name what the novel has been arguing: "life is imaginary, and this be an imaginary world." This is not nihilism. It is the statement that imagination is not the opposite of reality but its deepest form, that the world as it is and the world as it might become are made of the same substance, that the witness who attends fully to what is ordinary will find that what is ordinary is always also everything else. The Black woman in a racist America, speaking to a white boy whose deafness has placed him temporarily outside the protection his skin would normally provide, knows this from the inside of her own condition. Her suffering is different from his. The community they share is made of the fact that both are suffering what is not their fault, and in that shared condition she sees not tragedy but the beginning of imagination, which is the beginning of the world as it might become.

The recursive construction pillar is the device by which this final paradox lands with the weight it has accumulated across the entire novel. Young has spent 1,198 pages building toward "whatever is average is the dream" by showing us, again and again, the dream hidden in the ordinary: the honey in the skull, the bees that make the dead productive, the salt crystals bearding Miss MacIntosh's cheeks on the ordinary shore, the mute swan who does not sing even when it dies, the violinist who touched the strings only once. Each of these is a version of the same claim, deposited one after another into the reader's understanding until the claim has the weight of proof. The novel arrives at its final paradox not as a surprise but as a recognition, the sensation of having always known this without knowing that you knew it. That sensation is what Young spent sixteen years building. It is the deepest form of the ordinary. It is the dream.
