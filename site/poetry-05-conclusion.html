Exploring Young's Poetry

Conclusion

← The Same Ideas Across The Four Books · Exploring Young's Poetry

A girl in 1920s Indiana, writing her first poem at nine, was already reaching for the idea that would organize everything she made. Not a mood, not a set of images, but a philosophy: the conviction that meaning is here, in the actual world underfoot, and nowhere else. She spent the next six decades working it out in whatever form was in front of her. She put it in poems, first the early ones and then the two collections. She put it in history, in the account of two failed utopias sunk into the ground at New Harmony. She put it in biography, in the life of Eugene Debs and the price a cause charges one generation for the next. And she put it in the novel, the 1,198 page prose poem that most people know her name from and almost no one finishes.

The astonishing part is not that the vision is present in all four. It is that it is the same vision, held with the same eleven ideas, from the child's poem to the last work. Most writers spend a life circling a subject and finding it. Young seems to have started with hers and never let it go, and the poems are the proof, because they carry the whole philosophy in miniature before the longer books stretch it out.

Kurt Vonnegut, who studied with her, called her a genius, and the word does not feel loose here. It is the right size for a woman who built a complete and coherent system of thought and then dissolved it into poetry, history, and prose so dense and strange that the system almost disappears into the beauty of the thing she made. That she did it largely unread, and kept doing it anyway, provisional and unfinished and saying that it was enjoyable to do so, is not a footnote to the accomplishment. It is the accomplishment. The ground her work rests on was there from the beginning, and it held.
