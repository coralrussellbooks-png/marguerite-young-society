Young's Interviews Analyzed

The Duncan MacDonald Interview, "Observation Point," September 1965

← Previous · Young's Interviews Analyzed · Next →

A reading for the Miss MacIntosh project.

The interview at a glance

This is a radio conversation on a program called "Observation Point," hosted by Duncan MacDonald, recorded around the publication day of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling by Charles Scribner's Sons. The host dates it himself. He had just watched Arthur Ashe beat Roy Emerson at Forest Hills, and he cites William Goyen's Sunday Times review calling the novel "one of the most arresting literary achievements of the last 20 years in America." Those cues place the talk in September 1965, which makes this the earliest of the interviews and the one closest to the book's birth. Where the 1983 recording is a long retrospective, this one catches Young at the moment of release, still inside the Scribner's whirl, telling the story of the book before decades of retelling had smoothed it. It runs short, but the density is high, and several claims here are worth setting directly beside the later account.

The shape she meant and the shape it took

Young says she began with "rather humble feelings" and wanted the shortest novel she could publish. Told that the shortest was "about 200 pages," she aimed for that and "added a thousand." By the end of the second year she knew it would be long and was happy about it, because it kept growing "like Topsy, but under control." Two claims anchor her whole account of the book: "I always knew where it was going," and "I knew the last scene the day I started." She illustrates the second with the ending. About ten years before finishing, a painter friend suggested that Esther Longtree hang an "out to lunch" sign in her restaurant window, and Young did exactly that. The book closes on that sign, "out to lunch, be back in a while," with every word misspelled because "she could not spell either." Young insists she "had it in mind the day I started. Every major character and thing." The painter's prompt and the day-one claim sit slightly against each other, but her point holds: she worked toward an end she had fixed from the start.

The six-year-old poem and a self that does not change

The interviewer presses a natural question: over seventeen years a person changes, so how did the novel not change with her? Young answers that she "became more myself all the time," calls the writing "a wonderful experience in self-discovery," and argues that "we don't change any more than we change the color of our eyes about the deepest things of our lives." As proof she offers a poem she wrote at six, which she says "contains in it all the themes of my future work." She recites it: a bird sings of what it did when young, of its wife and baby birds, and the little birds fly away and the nest is "put into a museum... unless someone has taken it away." She reads it as memory, broken human relationships, and a vanished place preserved as a museum, and she traces the same figure forward. New Harmony in "Angel in the Forest" is literally a museum today, and the great domed house on the New England coast, swept away by the sea at the end of the novel, is another such place where dreams come to life. The poem is her evidence that the book was latent in her from childhood.

The grandmother's book

The grandmother, central in every telling, gets her fullest early portrait here. She was "very poetic and inspired," and from the time Young could hold a pencil she said "this child will be a writer," so that Young "became what she believed and dreamed." She died when Young was fifteen, and Young still feels her presence at the desk. Two anecdotes make the point. When a friend, Ella Tate, asked who looked over her shoulder as she wrote and guessed at T.S. Eliot and others, Young answered, "it's my grandmother." And Leon Edel, having read a third of the manuscript, asked who the influence of her life was; when she said her grandmother, he replied that he "just knew this was a grandmother's book," and Young links herself to Proust and his grandmother. She reflects that a grandmother near the end of life "has no competition with the child, only dreams of the child," so "the child becomes a vehicle for the grandmother."

The opium lady as searchlight

The opium lady appears here unnamed, "a friend of ours in Chicago." What stands out is how Young frames the drug. Opium in the book is "a way of lighting the mind, not of disassociating a person from reality, but of showing what reality is." It is "like a searchlight," and the dreamer is "a critic of reality," a professional dreamer who sits very close to the real. Young repeats that she has "never taken a drug in my life" and never would, because "I know I would stop dreaming instantly," adding that she is already "in a drug state when I'm undrug." She sets the dreamer beside Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Coleridge, and De Quincey. The Thornton Wilder anecdote recurs, though the wording differs from the later version. Told she was writing about the opium lady, Wilder here asks whether it is "a way of eliminating the unconscious," and Young agrees, pleased that he "knew instantly what the book would be."

One character at a time, and not a word rewritten

Asked how magazine excerpts could run in Mademoiselle and Harper's Bazaar before the book was done, Young explains the form. Miss MacIntosh is a psychological novel built on interior monologue, where "one character at a time dominates" and the book "isn't made of confrontation" but of each character taking over in turn. Any section can be lifted whole, as an episode of Proust or Joyce's "Ulysses" can, a trait she later found described as inherent to the psychological novel in "a very great book" on the subject. She makes a strong claim about method: she never rewrites, "never, never," and "not one word has been rewritten." She works so slowly that she has spent "as much as two weeks on a single sentence," not from any struggle with language, since her style is "as the air I breathe," but to keep faith with the personality of each character. Following Dickens and William James, who "said, man is body, soul, and clothing," she wanted to externalize the inner life and give every figure "flesh, blood, and clothing," even the walk-on. She refuses the mere butler who enters and leaves; a minor character must move "in an orbit of his own dream." She delights that a newspaper cartoonist drew the minor character Mrs. Hodgen with her birdseed and empty cage and "left the door of the birdcage open," doing, she jokes, "a little better than I have."

Miss MacIntosh: Boston, the nursemaid, and the sea

Young set the novel in Boston, though she had barely spent time in New England, "Boston three days" for an "Angel in the Forest" event. She chose it for transcendentalism and "my beloved Emerson," with Hawthorne and Henry James behind him. The city she knew intimately was Chicago, but as she puts it, "It had to be Boston." Working from a map, she made only one small street error, caught by a Boston-born Scribner's editor. Against the dreaming opium lady she set Miss MacIntosh as the seeming realist, a "flat-footed old nursemaid" from What Cheer, Iowa, in her mackintosh raincoat and black umbrella carried "indoors and out," "avidly normal." Young likens her to Dr. Johnson kicking a stone to refute Berkeley: "my foot feels this, it's not a dream." Yet by page six the nursemaid is revealed to have lived her own fantasy. "Her whole life is an impersonation," and "it's just her verbalism that makes her seem sensible." She commits suicide on page six, and the book still circles her. Born bald and buried bald, she reads through a biblical lens, and her missing hair becomes "the symbol of the dream and of the feminine in life" she was never given. She walks into the sea at dawn "when the moon and sun are both shining," wig off, wearing only black gloves, setting her umbrella at sea level before going down into the deep, "symbolic of eternity and the unconscious." John Hall Wheelock, reading the manuscript, kept insisting "she is normal... I don't care if she did commit suicide, she is normal." Young resists the Virginia Woolf comparison but admits she wrote the sea-walk with Woolf's drowning in mind, and was struck when an editor who had known Woolf told her Woolf "had left her walking stick at the tide's end," close to Miss MacIntosh and her umbrella.

Scribner's and the publication moment

A good deal of the interview is gratitude to Scribner's. Young stresses the risk they took on a very long first novel priced at \$10.95, "six novels almost," and calls their commitment the "most wonderful thing of all," "the greatest groundswell in their history before publication." They printed the "Hangman's Tale" section for the booksellers' convention in Washington. She names her editor Burroughs Mitchell and a Mr. White in charge of publicity, and admits she "hate[s] to see this book come out," not from reluctance to finish but because she will miss hearing from them every day. She and everyone around her, she says, always knew it would be finished.

Set against the 1983 interview

For the compilation, the two recordings agree on the load-bearing story and diverge in revealing ways. Both make the literary grandmother the origin, both root the book in the opium lady and repeat the Wilder anecdote, both insist she knew the last scene on day one, both hold that every character and event comes from life, and both describe the many-character, one-at-a-time method. The differences are worth marking. Here the opium lady is unnamed; in 1983 she is Mette Weissenbach. Here Wilder asks about "eliminating the unconscious"; in 1983 the same line becomes "revealing the imagery of the unconscious." Here Young says flatly that "not one word has been rewritten"; in 1983 she describes going back to rewrite Mr. Spitzer's uncertain identity over two or three years and letting characters "run away," which reads as rewriting of a structural kind even if not of language. The span drifts too, "17 years" here against "eighteen years" later. And this early telling carries material the retrospective drops or downplays: the six-year-old bird poem as the seed of everything, the suicide on page six, the Woolf walking-stick parallel, and the whole texture of the Scribner's publication. Read together, 1965 shows the story at its freshest and most formal, fixed on craft and the making of the book, while 1983 has folded in the political reading, the Debs work, and the named people behind the characters.
