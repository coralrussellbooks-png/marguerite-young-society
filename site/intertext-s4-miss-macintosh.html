Definition from research

Miss MacIntosh carries reference books, not stories. Her shelf holds Pilgrim's Progress, the genealogies of the Moody Bible, Franklin's almanac maxims, the Farmer's Almanac, and the plain wisdom of Ecclesiastes and Exodus. She prefers Bunyan to Scripture itself, because "the Word of God was that which should be taken with a grain of salt" (chapter 3). Her teaching aims at use: "she would have abjured all learning but what pertained to fact" (chapter 3). So Vera memorizes the sons of Levi and Judah (chapter 9), the Hebrew months, and the stations of the Exodus from Rameses to Elim (chapter 20). She learns "everything that old Benjamin Franklin had ever said about early to bed and early to rise" (chapter 15).

Miss MacIntosh treats the great men of history as personal opponents she would scold to their faces. "Vain man, Benjamin Franklin!" she cries (chapter 23), and she would tell Thomas Edison what she thought of electric lights. She never forgives the Emperor Constantine for changing the day of rest. Nearly every rebuke she delivers turns on time and light, on who decided when people should sleep, wake, and rest.

Her questions about the great are rarely curious. "Who was Martin Luther? Not that she cared for the answers" (chapter 66). Yet her cluster ends in something close to tenderness. At the edge of her own death, the scorn becomes pity: "Oh, my poor Canute who could not stay the tide" (chapter 63). Her guidebook turns into its own negation: "ours is a Pilgrim's Progress from nowhere to nowhere" (chapter 63). The woman who carried the most practical books in the novel ends by admitting, "The Bible exaggerates" (chapter 63).
