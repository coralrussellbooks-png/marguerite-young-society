Movement Two: The Five Pillars in the Traditions

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Mr. Weed

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Plato and Platonism

Allusions: Exploration and the Poles · Napoleon and Wellington · The History of Opium

Definition from research

Young said she did not want the novel to be seen as all doom and gloom, that there was a great deal of comedy in it. The statement has been largely ignored. The critical tradition around Miss MacIntosh, My Darling treats it as a cathedral of grief and philosophical weight, which it is, but this treatment misses something structurally essential: the comedy is not relief from the serious work. It is the serious work, operating through a different register. Young understood, as the best comic writers always have, that comedy is the form that takes the most seriously the gap between what things are and what they are supposed to be, and that this gap is precisely the territory the five pillars are built to inhabit. Grief as permanent weather requires comedy because without it the grief becomes sentimental, which is the one thing Young never permits. The witness requires comedy because the only way to sustain full attention to the world's contradictions without flinching is to find them funny as well as devastating. Illusion as primary requires comedy because the mechanism by which illusion operates, the straight face that insists on the impossible, is the mechanism of the joke.

The novel announces its comic method in one of its earliest and most sustained exchanges. The mother asks Mr. Spitzer: "Tell me, is there or is there not an invisible elephant in the corridor leading to this bedroom?" Mr. Spitzer, "smoothing his grey velvet cuffs, his mouth yawning, his eyes dimmed," maintains that "the burden of proof...rested not on him but on my mother who had seen the invisible." The mother's counter: why should an elephant rest on her, such a huge elephant, "a composition of sultry light beams, and with such a long memory, it could remember everything." And then the clincher: "Besides, if there was no elephant, then what was an elephant doing in the house?" This is not absurdism deployed to signal philosophical depth. It is a genuine comic argument about epistemology, about who bears the burden of proof for the invisible, structured as a perfect straight-man routine in which Mr. Spitzer is the straight man and the invisible elephant is the premise neither party will abandon. The exchange is funny because it is logically rigorous. The mother's final question, "what was an elephant doing in the house," is unanswerable within the terms of any epistemological framework Mr. Spitzer could bring to bear. She has won the argument by the rules of the argument.

The servants of the household have developed, through long proximity to the mother's opium world, a parallel universe of institutional comedy. One thinks he is waiting on Wellington because they have discussed Wellington so much. He is called Wellington by the other servants, and the mother asks him how Wellington is, what Wellington's appetites are, as though the conversation has always been about a third party who happens not to be present. Another servant thinks he lives in a forest of cuckoo clocks. One imagines he is Hannibal driving the elephants over the Alps. The second gardener thinks he is "a cosmic praying mantis reaching through miles of starlight." The servants dispute among themselves whether it is Roman in the morning or Greek in the afternoon. The blue room is already occupied by Plato's boat, et cetera. This is Young at her most fully comic, the "et cetera" doing exquisite work: having established that the household's rooms are assigned to historical and philosophical figures without regard for century, the prose waves away the rest of the list because to continue it would reduce the comedy by over-explaining it. The et cetera is the punchline's perfect punctuation.

Moses Hunnecker the bus driver is the novel's most comprehensively comic character, and his comedy is political satire delivered in a vernacular monologue that never stops, never quiets, and never for a moment loses the internal logic that makes it devastating. He will not cut his hair "until Roosevelt got out of the White House and all those Brain Trusters were busted with their W.P.A. projects and their public parks and their marble bird baths and their rotten roads and their free skating rinks that nobody wants when the river is frozen over and their free kindergartens that nobody wants." The list escalates from large to deliberately small, the marble bird baths arriving with the same indignation as the rotten roads, the free skating rinks positioned as culturally equivalent to governmental overreach, all of it delivered by a man driving a bus while drinking whiskey and invoking "God and all ye angelic choirs" in the same breath. Moses Hunnecker is a comic character in the Dickensian tradition: grotesque, exaggerated, completely himself, and in his very excess a precise portrait of a particular American type and a particular American political psychology. His hair, growing uncut as Roosevelt's terms multiply, is the novel's funniest physical gag and simultaneously a completely serious image of the body as political protest, of the personal as the political before that phrase existed.

The Christian hangman Mr. Weed and the landlord at the hotel provide the novel's most formally constructed comic exchange, a duet of professional non-sequiturs in which each man's area of expertise renders him unable to hear the other's. The hangman ties knots that end lives. The landlord ties knots that join lives. They discuss knots for several pages without either noticing they are talking about incompatible things. The hangman worries he nearly killed a dog. The landlord worries about ships that sink. The hangman wears his Sunday clothes to hangings and to church because they are the same occasion. The landlord notes that the hangman's bow-tie is loose: "Someone else will have to tie it. This is something a wife should do." The comedy is structural: two men in a late-night hotel room, each carrying the weight of his own mortality in the professional form it has taken for him, talking past each other with perfect comic timing across the gap that the plural self pillar names but cannot bridge. Their exchange is funny in the way that mortality is funny when you look at it directly enough.

The mute with the frog in his mouth is the novel's purest image of comedy as a philosophical method. The mute has trained a frog, through long practice and "labial and laryngeal and dental manipulations," to serve as his voice box, and in this capacity the frog has performed the Lord's Prayer in a thieves' café, advised on betting, and cautioned against pickpockets. When the frog dies, Mr. Spitzer attends its funeral in his "flowing black Englishman's bowtie and mourning cloak," because there is no one else to share the mute's grief. The comedy is completely earnest: Young is not laughing at the frog's funeral. She is presenting it with the same full dignity she extends to every form of grief and loss in the novel, which is itself the comedic method. The frog has served as a voice for someone who had none. That this is funny does not make it less true. The recursive construction pillar builds here as it always does: the absurd image contains within it a perfectly serious philosophical claim about what voices are, what they cost, and what it means to lose them.

Miss MacIntosh is funny in the way that total consistency is funny when it meets a world that refuses to be consistent. Her responses to the world's philosophical confusions are practical to the point of comedy: she dismisses chess as a game for kings and focuses on navigation and cooking, she prefers Pilgrim's Progress to the Word of God because Bunyan is "fairly safe even though not altogether trustworthy," she laughs wildly when a man at the Stock Exchange invites her to the North Pole with nothing but ear muffs, "her mouth wide and cavernous, a red ridge on her broken nose, her bald head sloping like a snowy cape." Miss MacIntosh laughing wildly on the Stock Exchange, bald, being invited to both poles simultaneously, is the novel's definitive image of what the witness pillar looks like when it is fully inhabited: clear-eyed, practical, absolutely itself, and completely capable of finding the situation hilarious without losing its grip on what the situation actually is.

Young knew that a novel about grief as permanent weather, about illusion as the primary mode of experience, about the plural self and the failure of identity and the impossibility of verification, had to be funny. Not to leaven its seriousness but because seriousness without comedy is sentimentality, and sentimentality is the one form of illusion Young refuses. The comedy is proof that Young was watching the world with both eyes open: one eye for what is devastating about it, and one eye for what is genuinely, structurally, unarguably absurd.
