Chapter 44

On Chapter 44 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of The Wheel and the Spokes.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 43 · Wheel and Spokes Home · Chapter 45 →

Research notes: Chapter 44 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 44

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Cousin Hannah is perhaps the chapter's most vivid demonstration of the plural self. She is simultaneously:

  • The great suffrage captain, the public hero who fought lords and kings and patriarchs across deserts and mountain ranges
  • The dying lover, crying for a lost love, calling for skirts, asking for snowdrops
  • The accuser and the accused, splitting herself in her dying speech: "Was I

false, and were you true? Was I true, and were you false?"

  • The absent presence, already forgotten long before she dies: "She had

already passed into oblivion"

These selves do not reconcile. The suffragette who spent her life urging women to remove their skirts dies crying "Let us put on our whispering skirts". The hero who never wore the white feather of cowardice accuses herself of cowardice at the last. The woman who fought against love's domestications dies entirely inside love's grammar.

Mr. Spitzer is equally plural. He is the lawyer and the musician. He is the witness and the participant. He is the one who brought the snowdrops that may have hastened her death, "It was almost as if his own magic had done this", and the one who must now interpret what he witnessed. He cannot separate these roles. His understanding arrives after it could be useful, which is itself a structural feature of his selfhood, not an accident.

The chapter refuses to privilege any single version of either person. This is not ambiguity as literary decoration. It is the ontological condition the text insists upon.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter is saturated with false lights. Hannah herself names them in her dying delirium:

"There was a false moon, and there was a false sun, and the planets were false. They were false as paper boats."

But the text does not position Hannah's vision as deluded and some other, truer reality as available. Mr. Spitzer himself operates entirely within constructed realities; he hears "race-track music long after Peron's last horse race upon this star" he reads papers by moonlight that may or may not say what he thinks they say, he interprets a dying woman's words through the fog of his own consciousness.

The suffrage movement itself is reframed as potentially illusory: "Mr. Spitzer was almost inclined to believe that she had never been a suffragette at all, that suffrage was her mask". This is not debunking. It is the recognition that all public identities are constructions, masks worn so long they become indistinguishable from the face. The cave is not escaped by removing the mask. There is only the next mask, the next construction.

Even the documents Mr. Spitzer reads, the archives of Hannah's great campaigns, are described in terms that undermine their reliability as records of truth: letters "crumbling into ashes and dust", written "in language of the shrouded dead, unintelligible", in "foreign tongues which he could not read and never would have translated, for they were those languages which could never be translated". The archive, the supposed repository of historical truth, is itself a ruin. Reality, here, is not found. It is assembled from fragments, and the assembly is always incomplete.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This pillar may be the most thoroughly embodied principle in the entire chapter. Hannah's grief is not a response to a recent loss. It is decades old, geological in its depth, and it has shaped the entire architecture of her life:

"dead love which had prompted her suffrage movement and battles against overwhelming odds. She, too, had loved and lost."

Her grief did not resolve into her activism. It became her activism. The suffrage campaigns, the desert marches, the mountain climbs: all of it was grief moving through the world in the only forms available to it. And at the end, the grief returns to its original shape, stripped of all its public clothing. She is not a suffragette dying. She is a lover dying, still inside the original loss.

Mr. Spitzer's grief is equally accumulative. He carries "adventitious moments, trivial attitudes he had dismissed before, irrelevant intuitions which had never quite escaped from the crevices of his memory". These do not fade. They add up: to mountains, to residue, to that which "time could not kill, for it had never existed in time".

The text explicitly refuses the consolation of resolution: "actual death could add scarcely a note to that which had already occurred". Death is not the end of grief. It is simply another layer added to what was already accumulating.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Mr. Spitzer is, structurally, the chapter's witness. He is the only one present at Hannah's death. He is the one who goes through her papers. He is the one who carries her words forward into the after-years. And crucially, he is a bad witness in all the conventional senses (he misunderstands in the moment, his vision is myopic, his comprehension arrives too late) and yet he is the only witness, which makes his imperfect attention the only attention there is.

The text is explicit about his limitations:

"Much that he might have heard was then perhaps unintelligible to a man who, like himself, was guided by his reason even when reason failed."

But he stays. He does not leave the room. He does not simplify what he hears into a manageable narrative. He carries the confusion forward intact, and it is precisely this refusal to resolve that allows the truth to eventually (partially, provisionally) emerge:

"slowly putting together these fragments, hints, suggestions until the shadow of the truth emerged if but for a fleeting moment."

Note the precision of that phrase: the shadow of the truth, for a fleeting moment. This is witnessing without the violence of conclusion. The ethical act is not understanding. It is continuing to look.

His myopia is also philosophically important: "he seeing best with his eyes closed". The witness who sees best with eyes closed is the witness who has learned that direct sight is itself a form of distortion, that attention must be oblique, patient, willing to receive rather than to grasp.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The chapter's entire temporal structure is recursive. Mr. Spitzer is not narrating in sequence. He is returning, repeatedly, to the scene of Hannah's death, each time with slightly different understanding, each time finding something he missed:

"slowly putting together these fragments, hints, suggestions"

"it took a long time for such remarks to sink into his consciousness as through miles of clouds"

"long after things were over, must go on with them"

He builds his understanding of Hannah's life the way one builds anything under Radical Immanence: provisionally, recursively, never arriving at a final version. The papers he reads are themselves recursive objects: archives of campaigns that were themselves recursive responses to an original loss. Hannah built a movement. The movement was built on grief. The grief was built on love. The love was built on: what? The text does not say, and the refusal to say is itself the point.

The image of Mr. Spitzer as a musician whose baton has escaped him, continuing "to dance, conducting music of clouds, waters, winds, avalanches", is a perfect figure for recursive construction. The music continues beyond his control, beyond his intention, beyond his death. He set it in motion. It now builds itself.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter is formally incomplete. It does not resolve Hannah's story. It does not tell us who her lost love was. It does not explain what happened on the mountain. It does not conclude Mr. Spitzer's meditation. The letters in the archive cannot be translated. The mystery is the key: "there was no key but the mystery".

This formal incompletion is not a failure of craft. It is the chapter's deepest ethical commitment. To complete the story would be to do violence to what the story is actually about: the irreducible opacity of other lives, the impossibility of fully knowing even those we witness most closely.

Hannah herself embodies the ethics of incompletion. Her life's work, suffrage, was never finished. Her love was never resolved. Her identity was never singular. And the text insists this is not tragedy in the conventional sense. It is simply the honest shape of a life:

"The unfinished is not failure": the chapter demonstrates this by being, itself, unfinished.

The most striking formulation of this principle comes in the observation about marriage:

"he knew most of marriage who never married, was never committed to one road which had its ending"

Completion, the road with its ending, is here figured as a kind of epistemic closure that forecloses understanding. The incomplete life, the uncommitted road, paradoxically knows more because it has not foreclosed the question.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Hannah is marginal in multiple, overlapping ways. She is a woman who lived as a hero in a world that reserved heroism for men. She is a public figure who died in private obscurity. She is a suffragette who was secretly a lover. She is a woman who fought against femininity and died calling for her skirts. She is famous and forgotten simultaneously.

Mr. Spitzer names her marginality explicitly:

"She was a man without a country. She was that poor mollusk who had no sea shell, no mother-of-pearl mantle."

The mollusk without its shell is the creature stripped of its defining category, and this is precisely where Hannah becomes most visible as evidence of how categories work. The suffragette identity was a shell, a construction, a mask. Underneath it was not a truer self but simply more complexity, more irresolution, more of what the category was designed to manage and contain.

Mr. Spitzer is equally marginal: the lawyer who is really a musician, the witness who arrives too late, the man who "had been ignored" who gives honor not to the great captains but to "those who had always been ignored, those to whom had been extended no accolade". He identifies with the marginal because he is the marginal. And from this position he sees what the center cannot: that the great hero is secretly the coward, that the suffragette is secretly the lover, that the public identity is always a construction covering a more complicated truth.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This is perhaps the pillar most explicitly thematized in the chapter's language and imagery. The past is not past. Hannah is dying inside a moment that happened decades ago on a mountain. Mr. Spitzer is hearing race-track music from a race already run. The letters in the archive are geological strata: layers of campaigns, each built on the layer of loss beneath it.

The text offers its most direct statement of sedimentary time here:

"Adventitious moments, trivial attitudes he had dismissed before, irrelevant intuitions which had never quite escaped from the crevices of his memory, all had somehow added up to mountains of the moon and that residue of mystery or love time could not kill, for it had never existed in time."

The phrase "residue of mystery or love time could not kill, for it had never existed in time" is philosophically precise. The deepest layers of the sediment are not in time at all. They are structural, ontological. They press up through every subsequent layer.

The image of the sea shell is the chapter's central figure for sedimentary time:

"the dead heart was like a sea shell carrying its own roaring"

The shell carries the roar of a sea it no longer touches. The past is not behind it. It is inside it, still sounding. Hannah's dying words are this roaring: the original loss, preserved intact beneath decades of public life, still pressing up through the ground of her consciousness as she dies.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely self-conscious about the limitations of language. The letters in the archive are written in tongues "which could never be translated by the greatest translator, languages for which there were no words". Hannah's dying speech is not coherent testimony. It is language at its limits: fragmentary, contradictory, addressed to someone not present, mixing accusation and longing in ways that cannot be cleanly parsed.

Mr. Spitzer's own language is marked by its location. He speaks always in qualifications, hesitations, recursive self-corrections: "perhaps", "it seemed", "he had supposed", "he was not sure". This is not weakness. It is honest language naming its own uncertainty, refusing the violence of false confidence.

The text also reflects on how language constructs identity. Hannah was "immortalized" by language, by the public record of her deeds, and then forgotten when that language faded. The archive that should preserve her is "mildewed", crumbling, unintelligible. Language that once made her real has become the evidence of her unreality.

Most strikingly, the chapter reflects on the language of categories (suffragette, hero, coward, lover) as constructions that both reveal and conceal. To call Hannah a suffragette is to use language that "was her mask". To call her a coward is to ignore the question "Had not the greatest heroes been prompted by secret cowardice". Language here is not description. It is negotiation with complexity that language cannot fully hold.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter's transcendence is entirely immanent. There is no heaven Hannah is moving toward, no resolution in a beyond. The sacred is located in the most ordinary and most broken things: a handful of snowdrops the rattle in a throat "like pebbles washed by tides" a sea shell on a beach, a snail's writing in sand, a leaf falling.

Mr. Spitzer's meditations consistently locate meaning inside the immanent world, not beyond it:

"He had always thought that the burning moth was more heroic than the burning star, for the star burned because of its own nature, and the moth burned because of its desire."

This is immanent transcendence precisely formulated. The moth's heroism is not supernatural. It is the ordinary creature's reaching toward what will destroy it: desire as the sacred impulse, located entirely within the mortal world.

The chapter's most beautiful formulation of immanent transcendence may be this:

"Perhaps a leaf had fallen in the wind a thousand years ago, or a star had fallen through a cloud. Perhaps a door had slammed in a distant city. Perhaps a snail had crept out of its shell in the sand."

These are not metaphors for transcendence. They are the transcendence. The sacred is the leaf, the door, the snail. Death "happened in many ways, more ways than love, some which were quite subtle, some which were bold", and all of those ways are here, in this world, in these ordinary events that carry the full weight of the ultimate.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This final pillar illuminates the chapter's deepest structural logic. Mr. Spitzer is not simply a man observing Hannah's death. He is the crossing point where multiple times intersect: Hannah's past, his own past, the after-years of his reflection, the future readers who will never exist because no one will write this biography. He is constituted by all of these simultaneously.

The chapter's most explicit statement of this principle:

"all other lives were increased by this great mystery of death, that all who lived had already died"

To have already died is to be the crossing point: to be constituted by what came before in a way that is structural, not chosen. Hannah was constituted by her lost love before Mr. Spitzer ever knew her. Mr. Spitzer is constituted by Hannah's death before he fully understands it. The understanding that comes after is not separate from the experience. It is the experience completing itself through him, using him as the medium through which the past continues to press forward.

The closing meditation on marriage and knowledge is the chapter's most explicit articulation of the crossing point's obligation:

"he knew most of marriage who never married, was never committed to one road which had its ending. For sometimes the happiness of married couples was so secretive, so remote that it was as if they had withdrawn from life."

The one who carries the knowledge is not the one who lived the experience most fully in the conventional sense. It is the one who remained at the crossing point: uncommitted to a single road, available to the accumulation, still open to what the sediment presses up. Mr. Spitzer is this figure. His carrying continues precisely because he has never arrived.
