Research notes: Chapter 21 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 21

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The narrator exists in at least three simultaneous temporal registers throughout this passage: the night of witness, the imagined future morning, and the actual morning. These are not sequential. They overlap and contradict.

"Easy it would be to concentrate tomorrow, however..."

The conditional future tense ("would be," "should accept," "should remember") does not describe what happens; it describes what the narrator intends to feel, which is already different from what they do feel. The self that plans pity and the self that experiences it are not the same self. And Miss MacIntosh herself embodies plural selfhood structurally: she is simultaneously the red-haired practical woman, the bald sleeper, the toothless open-mouthed figure, and the woman standing at the bedpost. These are not masks over a true self. They are all equally real and irresolvable.

"She had not changed at all since the evening before."

This is the narrator's willed simplification, and the text knows it is a lie.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The entire chapter is structured around the maintenance of illusion as an act of love. The narrator's "good intention" is explicitly to deceive Miss MacIntosh into believing herself ordinary:

"It was my good intention that she should always believe herself to have been the first to have awakened, that she should be completely deceived by my apparent ignorance of her."

This is not cynical. It is tender. But the philosophy cuts deeper: the narrator also deceives themselves, falling back asleep and waking to find Miss MacIntosh "fully dressed," accepting the reconstruction of her public self without "the slightest astonishment." The cave here is the bedroom in morning light, and both figures consent to its construction. The wig is not merely a prop; it is ontological infrastructure. Reality is restored not by truth but by the wig's return to the head.

Miss MacIntosh's own philosophy, quoted at length, reinforces this:

"The Devil does not always wear horns... he may be quite charming, a very handsome fellow."

Reality cannot be trusted to announce itself. Appearance is the only available surface, and it is always potentially false.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

The grief in this chapter does not build toward catharsis. It accumulates in the prose itself, in the famous triple repetitions:

"the pity, pity, pity of that head where nothing grew"

"the pity, pity, pity of nothing, of something"

This is not rhetorical emphasis. It is the formal enactment of grief that cannot move forward. The repetition is the weather. The narrator cannot say it once and be done. Grief here is specifically the grief of seeing someone you love stripped of their constructed self (bald, toothless, mastectomized, hairless) and understanding that this is also them, permanently, beneath everything. The morning does not resolve this. Miss MacIntosh stands at the bedpost in her wig, and the grief simply goes underground, where it will remain.

"It was only the beginning of pity. It was not the end."

This sentence is a direct statement of Pillar 3. Grief has no end. The honest text says so plainly.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

This is perhaps the chapter's central philosophical act. The narrator witnesses Miss MacIntosh in her full, unmediated reality, and the ethical question the chapter poses is: what does a witness owe?

The narrator catalogs with extraordinary precision:

"the stalk of a breast which had been cut away so early, the scar, the tissues like a baby's fist... no eyebrows, no eyelashes, no single hair or feather, the hairless body shining..."

This is not voyeurism. It is the practice of full attention: the refusal to look away, to soften, to substitute metaphor for reality (though metaphor arrives anyway, because language cannot help itself). The narrator does not rush toward resolution or meaning. They stay in the room. They see. The seeing is the act.

And yet the chapter also interrogates the witness's limits. The narrator ultimately chooses not to let Miss MacIntosh know she has been seen:

"I would not be the one to open a final door."

The witness here makes an ethical choice to withhold the witness: to protect the witnessed from the knowledge of having been seen. This is a profound complication of Pillar 4: sometimes the most ethical witnessing is invisible.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The prose itself performs recursive construction. The narrator imagines the future morning, then lives it, then finds it has already been constructed by Miss MacIntosh before the narrator woke. The building of the day (the wig, the sweater, the green ribbon, the silver-dollar watch) is Miss MacIntosh's own recursive act:

"She had not changed at all since the evening before."

She rebuilds herself every morning. This is not vanity. It is the ongoing construction of a livable self: provisional, known to be provisional (the narrator knows, and perhaps Miss MacIntosh knows the narrator knows), and built anyway. The party dress, meanwhile, cannot be mended:

We shall "only have to tear it further. We shall have to throw it away."

Some constructions cannot be recursively repaired. They must be discarded. But the self, unlike the dress, is rebuilt daily regardless.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The prose style is itself the argument. Young's sentences do not conclude; they extend, qualify, loop back, accumulate subordinate clauses that never fully subordinate:

"this great baldness as of the moon sunken beyond a watery horizon of iron waves, though yet I should also remember this bald head baring my heart of all deceit, making me know what lay just beyond the surface, that the strongest heart was also the very heart of phantoms and of chaos."

The sentence refuses to end cleanly because the experience refuses to end cleanly. Unfinished Is Honest is built into the grammar. Miss MacIntosh herself is described as:

"this unfinished thing, tentative and unbelievable"

She is not unfinished as a failure of development. She is unfinished as the honest condition of a person (mastectomized, bald, old, gnarled) who exists in the world without the completeness that social presentation demands. The demand that she be finished (wigged, dressed, robust) is described as a kindness, but the text also names it as a kind of violence against what she actually is.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Miss MacIntosh is the marginal figure made structurally central. She is:

  • A woman who has lost a breast to mastectomy
  • Bald (alopecia, or illness: the text does not specify, which is itself significant)
  • Old, gnarled, toothless in sleep
  • A servant (nurse/governess)
  • Irish
  • A woman whose body does not conform to the categories of femininity she publicly performs

"the hairless body shining... no eyebrows, no eyelashes, no single hair or feather"

She is the evidence that the category "woman" (as constructed by wig, brassiere with its "wad of cloth," green ribbon, pin-striped shirtwaist) is exactly that: constructed. Her body makes the construction visible. The marginal body here is not the exception to femininity. It is the proof of femininity's constructed nature.

The brassiere with its "wad of cloth" is particularly devastating:

"a brassiere with a wad of cloth which had been Concealed, that there had been this member lost above the heart"

The capital C in "Concealed" (if intentional in the original) marks this as ontological concealment, not merely personal modesty. What is hidden is not shameful. It is the evidence.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

The past does not recede in this chapter. It presses upward through Miss MacIntosh's body. The mastectomy scar is old, "cut away so early", but it is present in the morning light as fully as it was on the day of the surgery. Her gnarled hands carry:

"veins knotted by old labors like the veins upon her forehead"

Every labor she has ever performed is sedimented in her body. Her body is not a present-tense object. It is a stratigraphic record. The barnacles on her feet extend this image; she is being slowly covered by the sea, by time, by accumulation.

Miss MacIntosh's own philosophy of time is sedimentary:

"the road to Hell... is paved with bones and good intentions, and life is this valley of dry bones."

The bones are the past. You walk on them. They do not disappear. They become the road.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely self-aware about language's constructive power. Miss MacIntosh's own quoted speech is full of inherited formulations (proverbs, biblical cadences, folk wisdom) that she deploys with authority:

"The King James version of the Bible was not to be trusted because made for the vanity of earthly kings and for the unification of the British Empire."

This is a remarkable moment. Miss MacIntosh identifies a specific language construction (the KJV) as politically located: as speaking from somewhere in particular while pretending to speak from nowhere. She is, without using the vocabulary, making Pillar 9's argument explicitly.

The narrator's own language is equally self-aware. The prose reaches for metaphor constantly (the bald head as moon, as Alpine slopes, as glacial dome) and each metaphor partially fails, replaced by another. Language cannot hold Miss MacIntosh's reality. The accumulation of failed metaphors is itself the honest acknowledgment of language's limits.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The sacred in this chapter lives entirely in the ordinary and the bodily. There is no appeal to a beyond. The transcendence is located precisely in:

"this great head which was the secret pearl"

The pearl is not a symbol of heaven. It is the bald head itself: ordinary, biological, strange, present. The sacred is the thing seen in the morning light, not a meaning extracted from it.

The light itself is immanent; it comes from the sea, or seems to, and it illuminates without explaining:

"the sweeping sea light which seemed to come from no source but the sea"

The mystery is not resolved into transcendence. It stays inside the world, inside the room, inside the body. Even Miss MacIntosh's philosophy, for all its biblical references, locates meaning in earthly practice: guard your country manners, improve yourself, be not deceived. The sacred is vigilance in the immanent world, not hope for a world beyond it.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

The narrator is explicitly positioned as a crossing point between Miss MacIntosh's world and the future. The chapter is written in a conditional past-future tense that places the narrator at the intersection of what was and what will be:

"For that love is love which is love forevermore and unchanging, which does not alter with the changing circumstance and does not ebb, does not wane"

This is not a description of a feeling. It is the narrator constituting the ground on which future love will stand: declaring, from the crossing point, what love is, so that it will have been so.

Miss MacIntosh herself is a crossing point in a different sense. She carries the past (Irish, working-class, bodily marked by labor and illness and surgery) and she transmits it forward through her care of the narrator. What she deposits is not wisdom exactly, but a way of being in the world: practical, unsentimental, capable of both ribald laughter and grim truth-telling.

"the highway to Hell is paved with more good intentions than shining cobblestones"

She says this the morning after the narrator has seen her stripped of every construction. The proverb is the same. She is the same. And yet everything has changed, because the narrator now carries the knowledge of what lies beneath the proverb's speaker. This is the crossing point: the narrator is now constituted by what they witnessed, and they will carry it forward into a future Miss MacIntosh will not see.
