Chapter 65: Mr. Bonebreaker's Umbrella

Research notes: Chapter 65

Characters: John Knox MacIntosh · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Mr. Titus Bonebreaker · Richard MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Symbols: Eyes and Blindness · Pregnancy, Birth, and Children · Snow, Ice, and Winter · The Sea and Water · The Umbrella

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Ancient Egypt · Don Quixote · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Revelation and the Apocalypse · The Bible

Miss MacIntosh had a romance once. This is the chapter where she tells Vera about it.

His name was Mr. Titus Bonebreaker, and he was a Gospel salesman from Missouri, and he preached the end of the world on street corners, and he could not, in the end, accept the truth of her body. He left his umbrella behind when he fled. She kept it.

What actually happens

Miss MacIntosh tells Vera the story of Mr. Bonebreaker: how they met, their courtship, their engagement, and the night she showed him who she really was (bald, scarred, missing a breast) and he ran. She tells it plainly, with the dark humor she brings to everything. After he leaves, she works for a time among the blind in Chicago, where her physical differences are less visible. Then she drifts eastward, carrying his umbrella and a luminous watch he gave her, and eventually, she keeps going until she walks into the ocean.

This chapter is the romance of Miss MacIntosh's life, told in the past tense, because it is already over.

Mr. Bonebreaker

He is one of Young's great comic-tragic figures. A Gospel salesman, meaning he sold religious literature door to door, from the Missouri Ozarks, gaunt and stoop-shouldered with a beaked nose and bright eyes, perpetually preaching the Last Day. He ate no meat because cattle inhabited the earth, fowl the heaven, and fish the sea and rivers, and he would not consume any of them. His bones, he was fond of saying, quoting Psalm 22, were out of joint. He carried a long black umbrella. He believed Chicago would be destroyed, Babylon sunk into the sea, the Egyptians spoiled, the sinners called to account.

He is, in other words, a man who has organized his entire existence around the end of everything. And yet he falls in love with Miss MacIntosh.

This is not accidental on Young's part. The man who preaches annihilation falls for the woman who stands for endurance. The man who carries his umbrella as a symbol of prophetic shelter falls for the woman who has carried her own umbrella all her life as practical protection. They are, briefly, a perfect pair: two people with umbrellas, facing the weather together.

And then he sees her without her wig.

"He had put away childish things"

Mr. Bonebreaker is fond of quoting 1 Corinthians 13: the great chapter on love that concludes with the line about seeing through a glass darkly, about knowing as we are known. He often said “he had put away childish things.” He believed this about himself: that he had transcended his past, his sins, his attachments, and become a man of pure spiritual purpose.

Miss MacIntosh observes, with characteristic sharpness, that there was still a child inside him, as there is inside everyone, and that the childlike parts of him were precisely the ones he refused to see. His dream of a heavenly crown. His rage at those who contradicted him. His brushes with the police. His inability to face her physical reality.

He preached that love is not blind, that true love sees clearly. He was wrong about himself.

The night he fled

Miss MacIntosh showed him everything. The bald head. The missing breast. The scars of a body that had wandered, broken its nose, walked into traffic, kept going anyway. She showed him the truth she had been carrying under her wig and her mackintosh.

He ran. He left his umbrella. He left his Bible literature. He did not come back.

Young gives this moment without melodrama. Miss MacIntosh tells it the way she tells everything: flatly, with a dark joke at the edge of it. She had known, perhaps, that this would happen. She had lived long enough with her own body to know how people responded to it. And still she showed him. That is not naivety. That is courage of a particular, unglamorous kind: the courage of giving someone the chance to stay, knowing they will probably go.

He called himself a man who could love truly. He could not. She let him discover this about himself.

Chicago and the blind

After he leaves, Miss MacIntosh works with the blind in Chicago. This is one of the chapter's quietest, most telling details: among people who cannot see, her baldness and her scars disappear. She is only herself, as she sounds and moves and speaks. For a time, she is simply a woman among people who meet her without the visible weight of her difference.

She does not stay. She drifts. She had been chasing Dutch windmills, she tells Vera: a phrase she uses for the illusions she had allowed herself to pursue: connection, romance, being seen clearly and loved anyway. Young is in conversation here with Don Quixote, as she is throughout the novel. Miss MacIntosh, who of all people should know better than to tilt at windmills, tilted at one. She is not ashamed of it. She is just done.

Niagara Falls, alone

She goes to Niagara Falls. She stands there alone, with her umbrella, among the honeymooners: "Shuffle Off to Buffalo," the song of people going to the falls for their first nights together, playing somewhere in the background of Young's imagination. The noise and joy of other people's new beginnings. Miss MacIntosh, with no bridegroom, no escort, only the black umbrella Mr. Bonebreaker left behind.

This image is almost unbearably concentrated. Niagara Falls is where people go to begin. She is there to mark an ending. And the falls keep falling, indifferent to the difference.

"She walked into the roaring ocean on a cold September morning"

Your notes confirm what the text implies: Miss MacIntosh's death, or disappearance, happens in September. She walks into the ocean. We are not told exactly when (in the novel's chronology, it is still coming) but Chapter 65 places it clearly in view. The woman who stood at Niagara Falls alone will eventually walk into the sea. The woman who kept the rain out will go, finally, into the water.

The black umbrella will not help her there.

Mr. Bonebreaker's umbrella

She kept it. This is important. The woman whose own umbrella has been her signature (her shield, her symbol, her practical shelter) kept the umbrella of the man who could not love her truly. She carries both now. It is not clear whether she carries his as an act of mourning, or as a kind of dark joke, or simply because she needed an extra umbrella and there it was.

Young leaves this open. Miss MacIntosh would probably say she kept it because it was a perfectly good umbrella and she saw no reason to waste it.

What to hold onto

Miss MacIntosh standing at Niagara Falls, alone, holding her umbrella, or maybe his, while the water falls and falls, and somewhere behind her the honeymooners are beginning their lives. She is a woman whose heart was broken not by cruelty but by someone's failure of courage. She accepted this. She moved on. She kept the umbrella.
