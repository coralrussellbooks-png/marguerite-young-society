Chapter 9

On Chapter 9 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Woman in Young's Magnum Opus.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

Characters: Miss MacIntosh · Solomon the Turtle · Vera Cartwheel

The chapter's first question is who, or what, killed Miss MacIntosh, and the book's refusal to answer is the reading. Murder, suicide, accident, God's hand, nobody's hand: Vera turns the possibilities over and settles none. This is the ambiguity of female autonomy and victimhood. It is the boundary between life and death held open, and the diffusion of agency the book stated in Chapter 1, the same logic as "all people killed cock robin": a death with no single author.

Then the long recollection of life with Miss MacIntosh, and the feminist catalog of the domestic training, the thrift, and the marriage lessons. The point is that after seven years Vera knew her as "the strangest being on earth" and never knew her at all. Miss MacIntosh is the person who cannot be reduced to one, plural selfhood in the character built to look most single. The embroidered "G" that is not her last name is a withheld identity; the ovaries kept, she says, "in the bottom drawer" are the body already in pieces, organs stored like objects, the body coming apart played for deadpan comedy. Solomon the turtle, carrying a geranium that smells of tobacco, is a small crossing tucked into the household.

Marriage returns as cynicism. "No wife ever knows her husband," Miss MacIntosh says, and then it is too late. It is a warning about the institution and it is also the book's deeper claim surfacing in her plain voice: no self is fully known to another, because no self is fully one. Against it, Vera dreams of a "great, dark romance" on "uncharted seas," which is the longing the whole book runs on, the pull toward the place where the fixed dissolves.
