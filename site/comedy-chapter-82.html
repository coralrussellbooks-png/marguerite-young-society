Definition from research

The final chapter distributes its comedy across three revelations and lets the tone soften toward tenderness at the end. First is the "celestial joke" of Mr. Spitzer, who confesses he is really Peron, a man who "knew not one note of music from another note" and "scarcely the difference between the plaintiff and the accused," yet has spent years impersonating a musician and a lawyer, preaching funerals and closing an estate. His whole life, he admits, has been "this joke, almost a celestial joke."

Second, and funniest, is the old clam-digger who compulsively buries himself in the sand, leaving only "an opening for his mouth and nose," so that migratory birds mistake him for the landscape and land on him, gulls descending on him with "loud mewings and cryings," a heron perching until "the rock had moved." Spitzer's fussy, fatherly warnings that the man will "dig one hole too many" and forget where it is turn the habit into an ongoing comic bit.

Third is the stone deaf man who ends the book, roaring at a silent lover through the wall. His opening lines, "Little boidies don't have no tits. Little boidies don't have no umbilical cords," and then "I am glad I have tits, ain't you," land as pure absurd non sequitur. His fumbling at the wrong door builds a small comic aria: "It's the wrong door. My God Who art my enchantment, we be in the wrong hotel. I be the wrong man. You be the wrong girl. It's the wrong key. It's the wrong world." The novel then closes on its last joke, Esther Longtree's restaurant sign, misspelled in full: "Owt to luntsch, bee bak in a whale," followed by the deadpan tag, "For she could not spell either." After eighty-two chapters of drowned worlds and dead children, the book signs off on a pun and a spelling error, which is Young's way of insisting that the comic and the elegiac were never separable in the first place.
