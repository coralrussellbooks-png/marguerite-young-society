To All My Darlings

Chapter 35

On Chapter 35 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 3, 2026

← Chapter 34 · Home · Chapter 36 →

Summary

Catherine imagines Cousin Hannah returning from the dead for a long winter of cards in the empty house, with Mr. Spitzer banished so that he cannot remind her that he outlived her. Mr. Spitzer, who cannot tell a heart from a spade, has always believed Hannah a card cheat and death “itself a great deceiver.” Catherine reviews Hannah’s suffrage campaigns through deserts, emirates, and harems, the bribes she refused, the stained glass window she declined, and her lack of time for courtly graces, small talk, or the dance. Hannah could never have been made to confess that “she had yearned for marriage, or she had loved a man,” for the suffrage movement was “her awesome husband.” Catherine admires in Hannah the “splendid unity of character” she lacks in her own fragmented selves, and wonders who brought the faded rose Hannah clutched when she died in bed. Hannah once told of a eunuch who became a father and was strangled for love. The chapter ends on Catherine’s distrust of anything predictable and her habit, as Mr. Spitzer says, of taking the opposite view.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, the narrative contrasts Cousin Hannah’s fiery spirit and audacity with Mr. Spitzer’s cautious, prideful, and somewhat pitiable nature. Mr. Spitzer is portrayed as ignorant of cards, jealous, embittered by failures and losses, and unable to compete or engage fully in life’s “games.” Cousin Hannah, in contrast, is described as a lively, dashing, and indomitable player who “had out-witted every mistaken strategy” of Catherine’s and had “played a mean game of cards,” which metaphorically represents her boldness in life and politics. This contrast highlights themes of courage versus timidity, engagement versus withdrawal, and vitality versus stagnation.
  • The chapter extensively praises Cousin Hannah’s life and character. She is portrayed as a “great pioneer” and “great captain” in the suffrage movement, a woman who had no time for trivialities or passive existence. Her life was devoted to overthrowing male domination and advocating for women’s rights on every front, domestic and foreign, and even in unknown territories. The metaphorical imagery of her riding horseback through vast deserts, confronting dragons and minotaurs, and facing physical dangers portrays her as an almost mythic figure whose courage and commitment transcended ordinary limitations.
  • Cousin Hannah’s rejection of typical feminine roles and sentiments, as Catherine sees it, is a key passage. She had no time for “feminine wiles,” “the halo of self-pity,” or a “moment of intimate and unexpected revelation.” Unlike the romantic or domestic aspirations often associated with women of her era, she was married to the suffrage movement itself. This passage emphasizes her total dedication, as well as the radical nature of her life and beliefs, setting her apart from both societal norms and from Catherine.
  • Catherine imagines Cousin Hannah returning as a ghost or spirit, invited to stay in the large, empty house with its many vacant rooms and servants. This passage is important as it reflects Catherine’s complex feelings of admiration, longing, and unresolved tensions. It also contrasts Cousin Hannah’s vivid, active presence with Mr. Spitzer’s “vacant face” and absence, underscoring the symbolic displacement of vitality by inertia and jealousy.
  • The chapter reflects on the idea that Cousin Hannah is “more vivid in death than she had been in life,” having “escaped this wide-meshed net of years.” This passage considers the nature of memory, legacy, and the afterlife, suggesting that Cousin Hannah’s spirit and influence endure powerfully even when forgotten by others. It contrasts the enduring flame of her character with the fading presence of others, including Mr. Spitzer.
  • Vera’s mother, Catherine, sees herself as fragmented with multiple partial selves and vague thoughts, compared to Cousin Hannah’s remarkable unity of character and steadfastness. This passage offers insight into the psychological complexity and self-reflection of Vera’s mother, emphasizing themes of identity, coherence, and internal conflict.
  • The chapter describes Cousin Hannah’s “hardy temper,” her unflinching courage in the face of threats, dangers, and opposition, and her refusal to compromise or surrender. Her willingness to face “greater dangers than most men know” and to cross forbidden boundaries symbolizes her heroic stature and her commitment to her cause.
  • Towards the end, Catherine is portrayed as “beautifully obtuse,” often taking contrary views, bewildered by familiar surroundings, and suffering from claustrophobia even when thinking of vast cosmic spaces. This reflects her emotional and mental state as conflicted and detached, yet proud of Cousin Hannah. The mention of their “day” being her “night” and their “winter” her “summer” encapsulates the theme of opposites and contrasts running through the chapter.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Mr. Spitzer believes that death “was itself a great deceiver.” Is he speaking of Hannah or of himself?
  • Hannah was “unforgettable even when she was forgotten.” How can both be true?
  • Catherine “would forget that it was she who might have been the dream visitor bearing the rose in her hand.” Why might she forget?

Characters

  • Hannah could never have been made to confess that “she had yearned for marriage, or she had loved a man.” What does Catherine need Hannah to be?
  • Mr. Spitzer was “an excellent dancer” and Catherine “the most marvelous dancer.” Why can Hannah not dance?
  • Catherine admires Hannah’s “splendid unity of character which she herself had never achieved.” Is Hannah as whole as Catherine believes?

Connections

  • In Chapter 34 Hannah called marriage “the death of a woman’s soul.” Here the suffrage movement is “her awesome husband.” Has she escaped marriage?
  • The old maid with “ivory-boned knitting needles” recalls Miss MacIntosh. Is Hannah her opposite or her double?
  • King Canute “could not command the waves to stand still.” How does he fit the sea that invades this house?

Reflection

  • “If a thing was predictable, my mother at that moment ceased to believe in it.” Do you trust what you can predict?
  • Can a cause become a marriage?
  • “The extremities of faith in anything might always beget… its opposite.” Have you seen this happen?

Research: Characters

Cousin Hannah

  • A formidable, spirited woman and a leader in the suffrage movement.
  • Described as a “great captain,” “old New England suffragette,” “stone horseman,” and a pioneer who had traveled extensively and fearlessly.
  • Known for her strength, courage, and refusal to conform to traditional feminine roles.
  • She is remembered as someone who never sought pity or protection and who had no time for courtly graces or domestic bliss.
  • Her life was devoted to overthrowing man’s dominance, both socially and domestically.
  • She represents emancipation, freedom, and unyielding resolve.
  • Even in death, she remains vivid and influential, symbolized by images of snowy mountains and celestial journeys.
  • Refused memorial tributes like stained glass windows, showing her disdain for conventional commemoration.
  • “She had believed in no image, neither the living nor the dead.”
  • She died in bed, “clutching in her withered hand like a claw a faded rose.”
  • She told Catherine “there was nothing so cold as the heart of a woman who had never been awakened.”
  • Domestic couples “had whispered their secrets to her—usually the reasons for their separations.”
  • She is “this proud cousin on the male side, not on the distaff side,” with “the glittering eye, the pointed silver beard, the lifted sword.”
  • She once told Catherine of “a eunuch who had become a father.”

Mr. Spitzer

  • Portrayed as cautious, timid, and jealous; he lacks the boldness and dynamism of Cousin Hannah.
  • He is ignorant of cards, suspicious of Cousin Hannah, and bears grudges linked to personal failures and family misfortunes.
  • His presence is seen as an obstacle to Cousin Hannah’s legacy and spirit.
  • Mr. Spitzer is described as “corporeal, fat, soft-jowled, many-valved, sleepy-eyed,” symbolizing inertia and lack of willpower.
  • He is contrasted sharply with the fiery and active Cousin Hannah.
  • He “had always believed that Cousin Hannah was a card cheat.”
  • “Death, he had no doubt, was itself a great deceiver.”
  • In his youth he had been “an excellent dancer, taking every step with mincing precision.”
  • Catherine believes Mr. Spitzer should no longer linger in the house or in memory, making room for Cousin Hannah’s continued influence. “From every sphere, Mr. Spitzer should be missing.”
  • Catherine thought him “embittered by his own sense of failure in music.”
  • He thought Hannah “quite gloomy with her project to organize all ladies to rebel.”

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • Catherine holds a complicated view of both Cousin Hannah and Mr. Spitzer.
  • She is reflective, sometimes “unreasonably proud” of Cousin Hannah and often alone, conversing with shadows and memories.
  • Her own life is described as fragmented, filled with multiple selves and illusions.
  • She contrasts with Cousin Hannah’s unity of character and decisiveness.
  • Catherine blends admiration, doubt, and longing.
  • Before she went to bed she had been “the most marvelous dancer.”
  • “She had always seemed to give her attention to those who were not there.”
  • “If a thing was predictable, my mother at that moment ceased to believe in it.”
  • She is “always in her swan boat,” talking to “grotesque shadows on a wall.”
  • She carries on “long conversations with triple-crowned, heron-footed King Canute.”
  • “She suffered from claustrophobia even when she thought of the moon, the sun, the stars, the pathless places.”

Peron Spitzer

  • Unlike Joachim, he “had always had an ace in the hole.”
  • His “astronomical gambling losses” kept Joachim “in a state of colossal bankruptcy for years.”
  • “Peron would never have lingered here.”

Research: Themes

Freedom and Emancipation

See: Freedom and Escape · Women, Gender, and Marriage · Women’s Suffrage

  • Cousin Hannah embodies the fight for women’s rights and freedom from traditional roles.
  • Her life is a metaphor for breaking boundaries: social, geographic, and existential.
  • The suffrage movement is portrayed as her husband, her master, and her true love.

Memory and Legacy

See: Memory and the Past

  • The chapter meditates on how people are remembered after death.
  • Cousin Hannah remains vivid in memory, more alive in death than in life.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s legacy is one of bitterness and inertia, suggesting that memory favors the bold.

Conflict and Opposition

See: Fear and Courage

  • The tension between Cousin Hannah and Mr. Spitzer symbolizes broader conflicts: between courage and timidity, action and passivity, truth and deception.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s jealousy and bitterness contrast with Cousin Hannah’s fearless spirit.

Isolation versus Connection

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Catherine’s isolation contrasts with Cousin Hannah’s active engagement with the world.
  • Despite physical distance and death, Cousin Hannah communicates and influences across time and space.

Death and Immortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • For Catherine, death is not an end but a transformation.
  • Cousin Hannah’s death elevates her to a symbolic, almost mythic status.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s survival past her death is seen as a burden.
  • Mr. Spitzer “looked for no improvement on the other side,” and Hannah “had had no faith… in any after-life.”

Unity and the Fragmented Self

See: Identity and the Self · Duality and Doubles

  • Catherine is “this fragmented ego, these fragmentations of partial selves, of splintered mirrors and partial images.”
  • Her selves include “the look-out in the crow’s-nest… the lady in the phaeton, the figurehead in the foam.”
  • Hannah seemed never to have “divided herself… into opposing factions, warring camps, the battle raging in her own breasts.”
  • “Yet the extremities of faith in anything might always beget, my mother knew, its opposite.”
  • Mr. Spitzer acknowledges “at all times, two truths, neither of which should be true.”

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Cards and Games

See: Cards, Games, and Chance · Gambling and Playing Cards

  • The metaphor of cards and poker reflects strategy, risk, and deception.
  • Cousin Hannah is a “mean game” player; Mr. Spitzer distrusts her, believing she cheats.
  • This imagery symbolizes life’s gambits and the characters’ differing approaches to fate and challenge.

Nature and the Elements

See: Snow, Ice, and Winter

  • Frequent references to snow, stars, deserts, mountains, and storms symbolize the harshness and grandeur of Cousin Hannah’s life.
  • Snow and ice often signify coldness, isolation, and purity.
  • The natural imagery links Cousin Hannah to timeless, elemental forces.

Music and Dance

See: Music and Instruments

  • Music is associated with Mr. Spitzer. It represents internal worlds and subtle emotional states.
  • His “quavering harp strings” reach “through clouds to catch the wind music where there was no wind.”
  • Cousin Hannah rejects courtly graces and formal dances, symbolizing her rejection of conventional femininity and societal constraints.
  • She “could never have taken her place in the pattern of a minuet,” and “she had escorted, it had seemed, no lady to the dance.”

Light and Darkness

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light · Light and Darkness · Moon and Stars

  • Light (stars, jewel reflections, candles) contrasts with darkness (shadows, fog, cosmic darkness).
  • Cousin Hannah is connected with shining stars and blazing eyes, symbolizing her enduring spirit.
  • Darkness often surrounds Mr. Spitzer and Catherine’s fragmented self.
  • “Like a shooting star which probably knows not its destination when it starts… she would have overshot her mark.”

Horses and Riders

See: Horses and Carriages

  • “She would have ridden naked through the gale. She would have ridden through the storm of years.”
  • She “had ridden horseback through many distant capitals already evacuated and was like a great stone horseman with a stone helmet.”
  • Mr. Spitzer thought “the fearful captain of the horse quite gloomy.”

Mirrors

See: Mirrors and Reflections

  • Catherine’s selves are “these many, beautiful, waxen ladies parading in a room of mirrors and phantoms, each but the partial portrait of the other.”
  • Hannah’s life “had seemingly included no such multiple mirror images and imaginary loves.”
  • Catherine asked “that the ice-breakers should go ahead of her and clear a way, break the mirrors, break the ice.”

Research: Resources

  • “unlike his brother, who had always had an ace in the hole”: In stud poker the hole card is dealt face down. An ace in the hole is a hidden winning card, and by the early 1900s the phrase meant any hidden advantage. See Cards, Games, and Chance and Peron Spitzer.
  • “he had not entered the lists”: The lists were the fenced field of a medieval tournament. To enter the lists is to accept a challenge.
  • “swifter in the race”: “The race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong… but time and chance happeneth to them all” (Ecclesiastes 9:11). See Ecclesiastes.
  • “even a British prime minister to quail”: Young’s source for Cousin Hannah, a suffragist “battle-axe” described by Charles Studin, a founder of the NAACP, “challenged a British prime minister to a duel and the prime minister refused to bear arms against the lady” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Cousin Hannah and Women’s Suffrage.
  • “room for one who had crossed dark waters”: Charon ferried the souls of the dead across the river Styx into Hades, for a coin placed with the corpse. Later Hannah is one who “would have ridden to the sable shores of Hades or even farther.” See Greek Mythology.
  • “with troops of singing stars”: “When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy” (Job 38:7). See The Book of Job.
  • “the blind moth fluttered with jewels upon its wings”: “The desire of the moth for the star, / Of the night for the morrow” (Percy Bysshe Shelley, “One word is too often profaned,” 1821). See Butterflies and Moths and Romantic Poets.
  • “and no Western Union messengers”: Western Union, an American company established in 1851, was the leader of the telegraphy industry.
    • Its uniformed messengers, many of them boys on bicycles, delivered telegrams by hand.
  • “She would have ridden naked through the gale”: By the legend in Roger of Wendover’s thirteenth-century chronicle, Lady Godiva rode naked through Coventry, covered only by her hair, so that her husband Leofric would lift a heavy tax.
  • “in no drugged moments outside of time such as the opium lady might indulge in”: The opium lady is Catherine herself. Young drew her from Minna K. Weissenbach, “the opium lady of Hyde Park” (David O. Dowling, “The Guru,” in A Delicate Aggression: Savagery and Survival in the Iowa Writers’ Workshop, Yale University Press, 2019). See the Chapter 2 resources and The History of Opium.
  • “that dark captain of the light-footed brigade”: The phrase may echo Tennyson’s “The Charge of the Light Brigade” (1854): “Into the valley of Death / Rode the six hundred.”
  • “the Crusader whose cap was covered with shells of mother-of-pearl”: The scallop shell was the badge of pilgrims to the shrine of St. James at Santiago de Compostela. Pilgrims home from Jerusalem carried palm fronds and were called palmers.
  • “the triumph of woman over man, even of beauty over strength”: Joan Smith’s Misogynies discusses the pervasive nature of hatred and discrimination against women in society, exploring cultural, social, and psychological roots of misogyny (Joan Smith, Misogynies: Reflections on Myths and Malice, Faber and Faber, 1989). The novel vividly portrays the suffering, marginalization, and complex interior lives of women like Catherine, Esther Longtree, and Mrs. Hogden, who each embody different facets of women’s oppression and resilience. The presence of misogyny is implicit in their isolation, the betrayals they endure, and their struggles to assert identity and selfhood. Miss MacIntosh’s pragmatic, no-nonsense character challenges the illusions and fantasies that trap women like Catherine, reflecting resistance to the roles imposed by a misogynistic society. Her disdain for frivolity and self-pity contrasts with the more vulnerable or victimized female figures, highlighting the varied responses to misogyny. Smith emphasizes how misogyny permeates both public and private spheres, a theme echoed in the novel’s blending of intimate psychological struggles with broader social critiques, such as the suffrage movement represented by Cousin Hannah and the economic hardships faced by characters like Esther Longtree. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “She had not been merely another old maid dreaming with her ivory-boned knitting needles”: Cousin Hannah is contrasted with Miss MacIntosh, whose “knitting needles of ivory bone” click in Chapter 1. The old maid evokes the conventional, passive, and somewhat melancholy image of a lonely woman who spends her days in repetitive, quiet activities (like knitting by the sea) and who waits anxiously or emptily for time to pass or for some change to occur. This image is described as “empty servitude,” implying a life of resignation, routine, and lack of agency or engagement with the world. Cousin Hannah is depicted as the opposite of this: a dynamic, courageous, and active woman who fought fiercely in the suffrage movement, who journeyed to far and dangerous places, and who lived a life of bold purpose and unyielding spirit. She did not waste her life in passive waiting or empty rituals but instead took charge of her destiny, battling for women’s rights and freedom.
  • “one who had seen the burning bush”: “And he looked, and, behold, the bush burned with fire, and the bush was not consumed” (Exodus 3:2). See The Bible.
  • “pitched her tents in strange lands from which no living man returns”: “The undiscover’d country from whose bourn / No traveller returns” (Shakespeare, Hamlet 3.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “this old hunter with the glittering eye”: “He holds him with his glittering eye” (Samuel Taylor Coleridge, “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner,” 1798). The eye returns later, when Hannah is “this old New England suffragette with the glittering eye.” See Romantic Poets.
  • “She had wished to overthrow man, both as to the marital state”: The suffrage movement did not oppose marriage. The anti-suffrage movement opposed suffrage because it feared that women would be “masculinized” and that this in turn would “feminize” men. Under coverture, after marriage the man and woman became one legal unit that the man controlled. Women in many cases were not allowed to own property, sign contracts, earn a salary, or enter education without their husbands’ permission. See Women’s Suffrage and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “paternal domination, the regnancy of the past and of the future”: Kate Millett’s Sexual Politics is a foundational feminist text analyzing the ways patriarchal power operates through sexual and social relationships, emphasizing how gender roles and power imbalances shape personal and political dynamics (Kate Millett, Sexual Politics, Doubleday, 1970). The novel portrays complex gender dynamics, especially through Vera’s mother Catherine and Miss MacIntosh. Catherine inhabits a world of illusion and decay, surrounded by hallucinations and dominated by male absences and betrayals. Miss MacIntosh, by contrast, embodies a practical, no-nonsense counterpoint to Catherine’s dreamlike passivity, and she strongly critiques illusions and instability. This tension reflects the gendered power struggles Millett describes, though in a symbolic and psychological mode. Cousin Hannah, the suffragette figure in the novel, represents a militant, radical feminist presence, dedicated to overthrowing patriarchal domination, which aligns with Millett’s critique of male control over women’s bodies and lives. Cousin Hannah’s battles for women’s freedom and her rejection of traditional feminine roles resonate with Millett’s political feminism. The novel’s exploration of pregnancies, stillbirths, and female suffering (notably through Esther Longtree’s story) touches on the control of women’s sexuality and reproduction, key concerns in Sexual Politics. The novel’s treatment of female bodies as sites of pain, mystery, and societal expectation parallels Millett’s analysis of the political control exerted through sexuality. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “a dream retreating at cockcrow”: Hamlet’s father’s ghost “faded on the crowing of the cock” (Shakespeare, Hamlet 1.1). See Shakespeare.
  • “why so many great Antarctic explorers had left home”: Richard E. Byrd named Marie Byrd Land in Antarctica for his wife, Marie, in 1929. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “riding in a desert place with her Mahound”: Mahound, a medieval European corruption of the name Muhammad, was used by Christians to depict him as a god worshipped by pagans or a demon who inspired a false religion.
  • “the memorial art which Mr. Spitzer practiced”: Memorial art has evolved from ancient civilizations’ elaborate tributes to the deceased to modern expressions of remembrance and commemoration, reflecting cultural beliefs and societal values across different eras.
    • Mr. Spitzer’s own memorial art is music, the “memorial music sobbing as he sobbed” of Chapter 54. See Joachim Spitzer.
  • “a stained glass window in memory of her”: Memorial windows were a fashion of American churches from the 1880s into the 1930s, many of them made by the studios of Louis Comfort Tiffany.
  • “the emperor and the unicorn”: The emperor and the unicorn may refer to The Unicorn Tapestries (about 1495-1505) at The Cloisters, part of the Metropolitan Museum of Art, in New York City. The imagery is thought to represent “the beloved tamed,” with fertility imagery.
    • The Lady and the Unicorn, six tapestries of about 1500, hang in the Musée de Cluny in Paris.
  • “the lady and the blackamoor”: The pairing may recall a seventeenth-century broadside, “A Lamentable Ballad of the Tragical End of a Gallant Lord and a Virtuous Lady, with the Untimely End of Their Two Children, Wickedly Performed by a Heathenish Blackamoor Their Servant” (printed by A. Milbourn, London, about 1686-1693). The ballad casts its Black servant as a monster, a racist stock figure of the period.
  • “great gold-eyed white ptarmigans”: Ptarmigans are medium-sized game birds in the grouse family. See Birds.
    • They turn white in winter, all but the black of the tail.
  • “even like Queen Maud Mountains”: Roald Amundsen named the range for Queen Maud of Norway in November 1911, as his party climbed the Axel Heiberg Glacier toward the South Pole. See Exploration and the Poles and Mountains.
  • “expecting Madame DuBarry with her Persian hound”: Madame du Barry, the last royal mistress of Louis XV of France, was guillotined in 1793 during the French Revolution.
    • The Persian hound is likely the saluki, also called the Persian greyhound. See Dogs.
  • “a plague of fiery locusts, those who have no king”: “The locusts have no king, yet go they forth all of them by bands” (Proverbs 30:27). The locusts of Revelation do have one: “they had a king over them, which is the angel of the bottomless pit” (Revelation 9:11). See The Bible and Revelation and the Apocalypse.
  • “he never stepped on terra firma without assuming that it was terra incognita”: Terra incognita means unknown or unexplored territory. It embodies the unknown, the potential for discovery, and the possibility of adventure, but also the risk of the unknown. Terra firma means firm land or solid earth and often symbolizes the present, the familiar, and the grounded.
  • “the dance of the hours”: In Greek myth the Horae, the Hours, were goddesses of the seasons and of order. Ponchielli’s ballet “Dance of the Hours,” from his opera La Gioconda (1876), shows the hours of dawn, day, evening, and night. See Greek Mythology and Music and Composers.
  • “the pattern of a minuet”: The minuet, a slow French court dance in triple time, was the fashion at the court of Louis XIV.
  • “as Sherman marching to the sea”: Sherman marching to the sea was a military campaign of the American Civil War through Georgia. See The Civil War.
    • It ran from Atlanta to Savannah, November 15 to December 21, 1864.
  • “Napoleon at the gates of Moscow”: Napoleon at the gates of Moscow may refer to Moscow being occupied in 1812 by French Emperor Napoleon Bonaparte’s army during the Napoleonic Wars. See Napoleon and Wellington.
    • The city burned, and the retreat through the Russian winter destroyed his army.
  • “heron-footed King Canute”: King Canute or Cnut the Great was the king of England, Denmark, and Norway.
    • Henry of Huntingdon tells that he set his chair on the shore and commanded the tide not to rise. When it wet his feet, he said, “Let all men know how empty and worthless is the power of kings” (Henry of Huntingdon, Historia Anglorum, about 1130, trans. Thomas Forester, 1853).
    • Three kingdoms made him “triple-crowned.”
  • “she had yearned for marriage, or she had loved a man”: Young’s source for Cousin Hannah, Charles Studin’s suffragist “battle-axe,” fought men all her life, yet for forty years brought home sealed trunks that proved at her death to hold “a complete bridal trousseau” each. “She had a dream too” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Cousin Hannah and the Chapter 31 resources.
  • “Or she had yearned for a homunculus”: A homunculus is the tiny man the alchemists claimed they could grow in a flask, as in De natura rerum (1537), attributed to Paracelsus. The “dwarf” symbolizes a small, subservient male figure, a “gentleman’s gentleman,” who would serve her but not stand as an equal or partner. It contrasts with her grandeur and strength. Catherine believes Hannah could never have been made to confess such a wish, but Young’s source for Hannah kept a secret dream of marriage. See the entry above. The house later offers its own gentleman’s gentleman, “his head a steaming silver teapot, to wait on her.”
    • A gentleman’s gentleman is a valet, a phrase made famous by P. G. Wodehouse’s Jeeves, who first appeared in 1915.
  • “For hers was, indeed, this buried life”: “But often, in the world’s most crowded streets, / But often, in the din of strife, / There rises an unspeakable desire / After the knowledge of our buried life” (Matthew Arnold, “The Buried Life,” 1852).
  • “the infinite steeplechase stretching between two leaning steeples”: The first recorded steeplechase was run in County Cork in 1752, when Cornelius O’Callaghan and Edmund Blake raced from the church at Buttevant to the steeple of St. Leger church at Doneraile, about four and a half miles.
  • “the great battle of the white eunuchs with the black eunuchs”: In the Ottoman palace, white eunuchs, mostly from the Balkans and the Caucasus, served the inner palace under the Chief White Eunuch, while black eunuchs, enslaved in Africa, guarded the harem. From the late sixteenth century the Chief Black Eunuch outranked him (Jane Hathaway, The Chief Eunuch of the Ottoman Harem: From African Slave to Power-Broker, Cambridge University Press, 2018). A eunuch is a man who has been castrated. Throughout history, castration often served a specific social function. The earliest records for intentional castration to produce eunuchs are from the Sumerian city of Lagash in the 2nd millennium BCE. Over the millennia since, they have performed a wide variety of functions in many different cultures: courtiers or equivalent domestics, for espionage or clandestine operations, castrato singers, concubines, or sexual partners, religious specialists, soldiers, royal guards, government officials, and guardians of women or harem servants. Eunuchs would usually be servants or slaves who had been castrated to make them less threatening servants of a royal court where physical access to the ruler could wield great influence. Eunuchs were seen as more trustworthy and less interested in establishing their own dynasty. In cultures that had both harems and eunuchs, eunuchs were sometimes used as harem servants. In the Ottoman Empire, eunuchs were typically slaves imported from outside their domains. A fair proportion of male slaves were imported as eunuchs. The Ottoman court harem in Istanbul was under the administration of the eunuchs. Black eunuchs were African slaves who served the concubines and officials in the Harem together with chamber maidens of low rank. An important figure in the Ottoman court was the Chief Black Eunuch. In control of both the Harem and a net of spies among the black eunuchs, the Chief Eunuch was involved in almost every palace intrigue and thereby could gain power over either the sultan or one of his viziers, ministers, or other court officials. One of the most powerful Chief Eunuchs was Beshir Agha in the 1730s, who played a crucial role in establishing the Ottoman version of Hanafi Islam throughout the Empire by founding libraries and schools (Wikipedia). Cousin Hannah’s eunuch “had become a father,” though a great sheik had used him to guard his “black veiled love” “in supposedly perfect safety.” “Dying for love,” he was strangled and left in a desert courtyard. This story conveys the vulnerability and tragic fate even of those placed in seemingly powerful or protected positions. It may also evoke the theme of betrayal and the harsh realities beneath appearances. The eunuch, as a figure who was mutilated and powerless, yet tasked with guarding love, can be interpreted as a symbol of suffering, control, and the complex dynamics of power and helplessness, themes that resonate with Cousin Hannah’s battle against patriarchal domination and social constraints. The anecdote contrasts with Cousin Hannah’s hardy temper and unyielding spirit. While the eunuch was pitiful and ultimately victimized, Cousin Hannah was described as courageous, unyielding, and victorious in her suffrage fight.
  • “gates guarded by minotaurs and dragons and speaking birds”: The Minotaur, half man and half bull, was kept at the center of the Cretan labyrinth until Theseus killed it. See Greek Mythology.
  • “Had she not crossed into Mesopotamia”: Mesopotamia is a historical region of Western Asia within the Tigris-Euphrates river system.
    • Young said she “had read the histories of mountain climbers, of suffragette captains, of travelers to the Middle East” (Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs, “A Conversation with Marguerite Young,” Review of Contemporary Fiction 23, no. 1, Spring 2003 (1989 original)).
    • Gertrude Bell crossed these deserts and helped draw the borders of Iraq in 1921, yet by 1909 she was honorary secretary of the Women’s National Anti-Suffrage League. See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “Had she not crossed over into Transjordania”: Transjordania, the Emirate of Transjordan, was a British protectorate established in 1921.
  • “Did she not know Felix Arabia”: Felix Arabia, properly Arabia Felix, “fortunate Arabia,” was the Roman name for the fertile southwest of the Arabian Peninsula, roughly today’s Yemen.
  • “She had wished to rescue the slave, not only the economic slave but also the slave of love”: Cousin Hannah’s struggle is presented in terms of gender emancipation and suffrage activism. The economic slavery she aims to end is linked to the patriarchal and marital structures. Her battle is against male dominance and the social and domestic oppression of women. See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “She had caused many a flutter in many a dovecote”: “Like an eagle in a dove-cote, I / Flutter’d your Volscians in Corioli” (Shakespeare, Coriolanus 5.6). See Shakespeare.
  • “until he heard the angelus”: The Angelus is a Catholic devotion commemorating the Incarnation or the Annunciation.
    • It is announced by the Angelus bell, rung morning, noon, and evening. Jean-François Millet painted two peasants pausing in a field at its sound in The Angelus (1857-1859).
  • “great wentletraps reaching to mother-of-pearl clouds”: Wentletraps are small, often white, very high-spired sea snails.
    • The name comes from the Dutch wenteltrap, a winding staircase, which is why they stand beside the “intricate mother-of-pearl circular stairways.”
  • “thin as the deceptive Pleiades”: The Pleiades, the Seven Sisters, also Messier 45, is an open star cluster. See Astronomy and Greek Mythology.
    • Most eyes see only six stars, and the myth of a lost Pleiad grew to explain the missing seventh.
  • “fireflies like fragments of dead souls”: In Japan, “Any firefly may be a ghost— who can tell?” Two kinds are “popularly named Genji-botaru and Heike-botaru,” and “a legend avers that these fireflies are the ghosts of the old Minamoto and Taira warriors” (Lafcadio Hearn, “Fireflies,” in Kottō, Macmillan, 1902). See Fireflies.
  • “an organizer of chaos”: Amanda Vajskop reads the Babylonian Enuma Elish and the Genesis creation story, in which male creators take over woman’s part in bringing forth life and women are cast as destabilizing and dangerous, a fear that patriarchy uses to keep women subordinate (Amanda Vajskop, “Finding Patterns in the Chaos: Woman as Chaos Agent in Creation Myths,” Denison Journal of Religion 5, article 7, 2005). The novel is deeply invested in themes of chaos versus order, illusion versus reality. Vera’s narrative continuously navigates between dreamlike confusion and the search for meaning or “ultimate harmony.” Miss MacIntosh represents order and common sense amid the chaos of Catherine’s opium-induced visions and the fragmented lives of other characters. The novel portrays characters with fragmented selves: Vera’s own complex consciousness, Miss MacIntosh’s evolving physical and psychological state (including her baldness and amputated breast), and the multiple personae like Cousin Hannah and Mrs. Hogden. Vera’s act of recounting, reflecting, and seeking patterns in her experiences and the lives around her is itself a search for coherence in fragmentation. The novel’s richly symbolic and layered narrative demands readers engage in deciphering meaning from a kaleidoscope of images and voices. The novel’s intense focus on female embodiment, sexuality, motherhood, loss, and survival complements critical perspectives on misogyny and sexual politics. Cousin Hannah, “an organizer of chaos,” takes up the part these myths assign to woman and turns it against the order men have made. See Genesis and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “of Stygian night which knew no time”: Stygian refers to the River Styx in the underworld of Greek mythology. See Greek Mythology.
  • “toppling cloud-topped towers”: “The cloud-capp’d towers, the gorgeous palaces, / The solemn temples, the great globe itself, / Yea, all which it inherit, shall dissolve” (Shakespeare, The Tempest 4.1). See Shakespeare.
