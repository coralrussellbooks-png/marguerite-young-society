Summary

Catherine imagines inviting Hannah back for a winter of card games. She reviews Hannah's suffrage career on every front, her conquests and her refusal of any memorial window, and all the things Hannah was not: no dancer, no hostess, no keeper of private confessions. She concludes that Hannah was married to the suffrage movement as if it were her husband. Mr. Spitzer notes Catherine's habit of always taking the opposite view.

Key passages

From the research

  • Early in the chapter, the narrative contrasts Cousin Hannah’s fiery spirit and audacity with Mr. Spitzer’s cautious, prideful, and somewhat pitiable nature. Mr. Spitzer is portrayed as ignorant of cards, jealous, embittered by failures and losses, and unable to compete or engage fully in life’s “games.” Cousin Hannah, in contrast, is described as a lively, dashing, and indomitable player who had outwitted everyone and had “played a mean game of cards” which metaphorically represents her boldness in life and politics. This contrast highlights themes of courage versus timidity, engagement versus withdrawal, and vitality versus stagnation.
  • The chapter extensively praises Cousin Hannah’s life and character. She is portrayed as a “great pioneer” and “great captain” in the suffrage movement, a woman who had no time for trivialities or passive existence. Her life was devoted to overthrowing male domination and advocating for women’s rights on every front, domestic and foreign, and even in unknown territories. The metaphorical imagery of her riding horseback through vast deserts, confronting dragons and minotaurs, and facing physical dangers portrays her as an almost mythic figure whose courage and commitment transcended ordinary limitations.
  • Cousin Hannah’s rejection of typical feminine roles and sentiments is a key passage. She had no time for “feminine wiles,” “self-pity,” or “intimate revelations.” Unlike the romantic or domestic aspirations often associated with women of her era, she was married to the suffrage movement itself. This passage emphasizes her total dedication, as well as the radical nature of her life and beliefs, setting her apart from both societal norms and from Catherine.
  • Catherine imagines Cousin Hannah returning as a ghost or spirit, invited to stay in the large, empty house with its many vacant rooms and servants. This passage is important as it reflects Catherine’s complex feelings of admiration, longing, and unresolved tensions. It also contrasts Cousin Hannah’s vivid, active presence with Mr. Spitzer’s “vacant face” and absence, underscoring the symbolic displacement of vitality by inertia and jealousy.
  • The chapter reflects on the idea that Cousin Hannah is “more vivid in death than she had been in life,” having “escaped this wide-meshed net of years.” This passage considers the nature of memory, legacy, and the afterlife, suggesting that Cousin Hannah’s spirit and influence endure powerfully even when forgotten by others. It contrasts the enduring flame of her character with the fading presence of others, including Mr. Spitzer.
  • Vera’s mother, Catherine, sees herself as fragmented with multiple partial selves and vague thoughts, compared to Cousin Hannah’s remarkable unity of character and steadfastness. This passage offers insight into the psychological complexity and self-reflection of Vera’s mother, emphasizing themes of identity, coherence, and internal conflict.
  • The chapter describes Cousin Hannah’s “hardy temper,” her unflinching courage in the face of threats, dangers, and opposition, and her refusal to compromise or surrender. Her willingness to face “greater dangers than most men know” and to cross forbidden boundaries symbolizes her heroic stature and her commitment to her cause.
  • Towards the end, Catherine is portrayed as “beautifully obtuse,” often taking contrary views, bewildered by familiar surroundings, and suffering from claustrophobia even when thinking of vast cosmic spaces. This reflects her emotional and mental state as conflicted and detached, yet proud of Cousin Hannah. The mention of their “day” being her “night” and their “winter” her “summer” encapsulates the theme of opposites and contrasts running through the chapter

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Cousin Hannah

  • A formidable, spirited woman and a leader in the suffrage movement.
  • Described as a “great captain,” “old New England suffragette,” “stone horseman,” and a pioneer who had traveled extensively and fearlessly.
  • Known for her strength, courage, and refusal to conform to traditional feminine roles.
  • She is remembered as someone who never sought pity or protection and who had no time for courtly graces or domestic bliss.
  • Her life was devoted to overthrowing man’s dominance, both socially and domestically.
  • She represents emancipation, freedom, and unyielding resolve.
  • Even in death, she remains vivid and influential, symbolized by images of snowy mountains and celestial journeys.
  • Refused memorial tributes like stained glass windows, showing her disdain for conventional commemoration.

Mr. Spitzer

  • Portrayed as cautious, timid, and jealous; he lacks the boldness and dynamism of Cousin Hannah.
  • He is ignorant of cards, suspicious of Cousin Hannah, and bears grudges linked to personal failures and family misfortunes.
  • His presence is seen as an obstacle to Cousin Hannah’s legacy and spirit.
  • Mr. Spitzer is described as “corporeal, fat, soft-jowled, many-valved, sleepy-eyed,” symbolizing inertia and lack of willpower.
  • He is contrasted sharply with the fiery and active Cousin Hannah.
  • Catherine believes Mr. Spitzer should no longer linger in the house or in memory, making room for Cousin Hannah’s continued influence.

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • Catherine holds a complicated view of both Cousin Hannah and Mr. Spitzer.
  • Proud of Cousin Hannah and sometimes wistful about their relationship.
  • She is reflective, sometimes “unreasonably proud” of Cousin Hannah and often alone, conversing with shadows and memories.
  • Her own life is described as fragmented, filled with multiple selves and illusions.
  • She contrasts with Cousin Hannah’s unity of character and decisiveness.
  • Catherine blends admiration, doubt, and longing.

Research: Themes

Freedom and Emancipation

See: Freedom and Escape

  • Cousin Hannah embodies the fight for women’s rights and freedom from traditional roles.
  • Her life is a metaphor for breaking boundaries: social, geographic, and existential.
  • The suffrage movement is portrayed as her husband, her master, and her true love.

Memory and Legacy

See: Memory and the Past

  • The chapter meditates on how people are remembered after death.
  • Cousin Hannah remains vivid in memory, more alive in death than in life.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s legacy is one of bitterness and inertia, suggesting that memory favors the bold.

Conflict and Opposition

  • The tension between Cousin Hannah and Mr. Spitzer symbolizes broader conflicts: between courage and timidity, action and passivity, truth and deception.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s jealousy and bitterness contrast with Cousin Hannah’s fearless spirit.

Isolation vs. Connection

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Catherine’s isolation contrasts with Cousin Hannah’s active engagement with the world.
  • Despite physical distance and death, Cousin Hannah communicates and influences across time and space.

Death and Immortality

See: Death and Mortality

  • Death is depicted not as an end but as a transformation.
  • Cousin Hannah’s death elevates her to a symbolic, almost mythic status.
  • Mr. Spitzer’s survival past her death is seen as a burden.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Cards and Games

See: Cards, Games, and Chance

  • The metaphor of cards and poker reflects strategy, risk, and deception.
  • Cousin Hannah is a “mean game” player; Mr. Spitzer distrusts her, believing she cheats.
  • This imagery symbolizes life’s gambits and the characters’ differing approaches to fate and challenge.

Nature and the Elements

  • Frequent references to snow, stars, deserts, mountains, and storms symbolize the harshness and grandeur of Cousin Hannah’s life.
  • Snow and ice often signify coldness, isolation, and purity.
  • The natural imagery links Cousin Hannah to timeless, elemental forces.

Music and Dance

See: Music and Instruments

  • Music is associated with Mr. Spitzer. It represents internal worlds and subtle emotional states.
  • Cousin Hannah rejects courtly graces and formal dances, symbolizing her rejection of conventional femininity and societal constraints.

Light and Darkness

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light

  • Light (stars, jewel reflections, candles) contrasts with darkness (shadows, fog, cosmic darkness).
  • Cousin Hannah is connected with shining stars and blazing eyes, symbolizing her enduring spirit.
  • Darkness often surrounds Mr. Spitzer and Catherine’s fragmented self.

Research: Resources

  • The "dwarf" symbolizes a small, subservient male figure, a "gentleman’s gentleman," who would serve her but not stand as an equal or partner. The mention of this figure highlights Cousin Hannah's rejection of the traditional gender roles and domestic dependencies that women often experienced, especially regarding men. She was too ambitious and engaged in the far-reaching suffrage movement to tolerate any such diminutive or limiting relationship. The metaphor underscores her fierce independence and commitment to the cause over personal or romantic attachments. The "dwarf" is not a literal person but a metaphorical symbol of the kind of male subservience or domestic service Cousin Hannah refused. It contrasts with her grandeur and strength, emphasizing her extraordinary character as a suffrage leader who eschewed typical gender expectations and relationships. This interpretation aligns with the overall depiction of Cousin Hannah as a powerful, untamed figure, a pioneer and warrior for women's rights, who was never confined by societal norms about women and men.
  • Western Union messengers is Western Union, an American company first established in 1851 and leader of the telegraphy industry.
  • Cousin Hannah is compared to Miss MacIntosh. “She had not been merely another old maid dreaming with her ivory-boned knitting needles, her balls of grey yarn, her purling of grey stitches by the unravelling surf, her empty looks, her continual looks at an alarm clock or a darkening sky as if the stars might be another alarm clock suddenly ringing. Such empty servitude…” It evokes the conventional, passive, and somewhat melancholy image of a lonely woman who spends her days in repetitive, quiet activities (like knitting by the sea) and who waits anxiously or emptily for time to pass or for some change to occur. This image is described as “empty servitude,” implying a life of resignation, routine, and lack of agency or engagement with the world. Cousin Hannah is depicted as the opposite of this: a dynamic, courageous, and active woman who fought fiercely in the suffrage movement, who journeyed to far and dangerous places, and who lived a life of bold purpose and unyielding spirit. She did not waste her life in passive waiting or empty rituals but instead took charge of her destiny, battling for women’s rights and freedom.

  • Kate Millett’s Sexual Politics is a foundational feminist text analyzing the ways patriarchal power operates through sexual and social relationships, emphasizing how gender roles and power imbalances shape personal and political dynamics. The novel portrays complex gender dynamics, especially through Vera’s mother Catherine and Miss MacIntosh. Catherine inhabits a world of illusion and decay, surrounded by hallucinations and dominated by male absences and betrayals. Miss MacIntosh, by contrast, embodies a practical, no-nonsense counterpoint to Catherine’s dreamlike passivity, and she strongly critiques illusions and instability. This tension reflects the gendered power struggles Millett describes, though in a symbolic and psychological mode. Cousin Hannah, the suffragette figure in the novel, represents a militant, radical feminist presence, dedicated to overthrowing patriarchal domination, which aligns with Millett’s critique of male control over women’s bodies and lives. Cousin Hannah’s battles for women’s freedom and her rejection of traditional feminine roles resonate with Millett’s political feminism. The novel’s exploration of pregnancies, stillbirths, and female suffering (notably through Esther Longtree’s story) touches on the control of women’s sexuality and reproduction, key concerns in Sexual Politics. The novel’s treatment of female bodies as sites of pain, mystery, and societal expectation parallels Millett’s analysis of the political control exerted through sexuality.
  • Joan Smith’s Misogynies discusses the pervasive nature of hatred and discrimination against women in society, exploring cultural, social, and psychological roots of misogyny. The novel vividly portrays the suffering, marginalization, and complex interior lives of women like Catherine, Esther Longtree, and Mrs. Hogden, who each embody different facets of women’s oppression and resilience. The presence of misogyny is implicit in their isolation, the betrayals they endure, and their struggles to assert identity and selfhood. Miss MacIntosh’s pragmatic, no-nonsense character challenges the illusions and fantasies that trap women like Catherine, reflecting resistance to the roles imposed by a misogynistic society. Her disdain for frivolity and self-pity contrasts with the more vulnerable or victimized female figures, highlighting the varied responses to misogyny. Smith emphasizes how misogyny permeates both public and private spheres, a theme echoed in the novel’s blending of intimate psychological struggles with broader social critiques, such as the suffrage movement represented by Cousin Hannah and the economic hardships faced by characters like Esther Longtree.
  • While less widely known, Amanda Vajskop’s work Finding Patterns in the Chaos investigates how meaning, order, and identity can be discerned within apparent disorder, especially in contexts of trauma, fragmentation, and complex social realities. The novel is deeply invested in themes of chaos versus order, illusion versus reality. Vera’s narrative continuously navigates between dreamlike confusion and the search for meaning or “ultimate harmony.” Miss MacIntosh represents order and common sense amid the chaos of Catherine’s opium-induced visions and the fragmented lives of other characters. Vajskop’s focus on identity formation amid chaos parallels the novel’s portrayal of characters with fragmented selves: Vera’s own complex consciousness, Miss MacIntosh’s evolving physical and psychological state (including her baldness and amputated breast), and the multiple personae like Cousin Hannah and Mrs. Hogden. Vera’s act of recounting, reflecting, and seeking patterns in her experiences and the lives around her mirrors Vajskop’s idea of finding coherence in fragmentation. The novel’s richly symbolic and layered narrative demands readers engage in deciphering meaning from a kaleidoscope of images and voices. The novel’s intense focus on female embodiment, sexuality, motherhood, loss, and survival complements critical perspectives on misogyny and sexual politics.

  • A eunuch is a man who has been castrated. Throughout history, castration often served a specific social function. The earliest records for intentional castration to produce eunuchs are from the Sumerian city of Lagash in the 2nd millennium BCE. Over the millennia since, they have performed a wide variety of functions in many different cultures: courtiers or equivalent domestics, for espionage or clandestine operations, castrato singers, concubines, or sexual partners, religious specialists, soldiers, royal guards, government officials, and guardians of women or harem servants. Eunuchs would usually be servants or slaves who had been castrated to make them less threatening servants of a royal court where physical access to the ruler could wield great influence. Eunuchs were seen as more trustworthy and less interested in establishing their own dynasty. In cultures that had both harems and eunuchs, eunuchs were sometimes used as harem servants. In the Ottoman Empire, eunuchs were typically slaves imported from outside their domains. A fair proportion of male slaves were imported as eunuchs. The Ottoman court harem in Istanbul was under the administration of the eunuchs. Black eunuchs were African slaves who served the concubines and officials in the Harem together with chamber maidens of low rank. An important figure in the Ottoman court was the Chief Black Eunuch. In control of both the Harem and a net of spies among the black eunuchs, the Chief Eunuch was involved in almost every palace intrigue and thereby could gain power over either the sultan or one of his viziers, ministers, or other court officials. One of the most powerful Chief Eunuchs was Beshir Agha in the 1730s, who played a crucial role in establishing the Ottoman version of Hanafi Islam throughout the Empire by founding libraries and schools. The eunuch was used by a great sheik to guard a "black veiled love," supposedly in perfect safety, yet he was ultimately strangled and left to die in a desert courtyard. This story conveys the vulnerability and tragic fate even of those placed in seemingly powerful or protected positions. It may also evoke the theme of betrayal and the harsh realities beneath appearances. The eunuch, as a figure who was mutilated and powerless, yet tasked with guarding love, can be interpreted as a symbol of suffering, control, and the complex dynamics of power and helplessness, themes that resonate with Cousin Hannah's battle against patriarchal domination and social constraints. The anecdote contrasts with Cousin Hannah's hardy temper and unyielding spirit. While the eunuch was pitiful and ultimately victimized, Cousin Hannah was described as courageous, unyielding, and victorious in her suffrage fight.
