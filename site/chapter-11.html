Summary

In this very short chapter, Vera wonders how she will endure her isolation. She cannot live like her mother, and she trusts herself to know where dream ends and reality begins, as Miss MacIntosh taught her. Meanwhile Catherine works through her alphabet of phantom guests, the P's, Q's, and R's: Pericles, Admiral Peary, Penelope's web, the Pleiades, and Sir Walter Raleigh, who removes his head along with his plumed hat. Mr. Spitzer, as always, has missed it all.

Key passages

From the research

  • The chapter opens with Vera pondering how to endure isolation, recognizing that unlike her mother Catherine, mere illusions are insufficient. She is aware of the boundary between dream and reality, a lesson imparted by Miss MacIntosh, who denies the existence of what does not exist. This sets the thematic framework of the chapter, highlighting the tension between illusion and reality, and the difficulty of coping with solitude without resorting to self-deception.
  • Catherine is described as being in a "wakeful coma," journeying through an opiate-induced voyage through various symbolic "places" starting with the letter P (Pericles, Penelope’s web, the Pleiades, penguins) and moving onto the Q’s and R’s. This voyage represents a surreal and metaphorical experience, suggesting that Catherine’s consciousness moves through layers of history, mythology, geography, and fantasy. These symbolic names imply a vast, intricate mental landscape, blending real and imagined elements.
  • The Q’s include the Queen Charlotte Islands, Queen Maud Mountains, and a naval battle, each representing different aspects of isolation, coldness, and distance. Queen Maud Mountains, in particular, is portrayed as an imperious, frigid visitor to Catherine’s bedroom, embodying a chilling and overwhelming presence that silences and immobilizes. The description of her skirt as "five hundred miles long and made of pure Brussels lace" evokes a surreal and vast majesty, emphasizing Catherine’s awe and envy.
  • Catherine’s mental journey perhaps continues to the R’s, a group connected by "silken relationships" despite never having met in reality. This assembly includes historical, mythical, and symbolic figures such as Mr. Res Tacamah (a drug bottle), the Egyptian Pharaoh Ramses I, II, III (representing fragmented selves and layered identities), runes, royal masts, Roland, Rimski-Korsakov, Sir Joshua Reynolds, and others. The richness of this imagery suggests a complex and paradoxical inner world where time, space, and identity blur.
  • Mr. Spitzer appears as a figure who perpetually misses Catherine’s gatherings of extraordinary company, including great composers, painters, and mythological judges like Rhadamanthus. There is a tone of irony and loss here, as Mr. Spitzer is condemned for forgotten crimes and fails to witness Catherine’s vibrant and mysterious social realm, underscoring themes of absence and missed connection.
  • Among the remarkable guests, Sir Walter Raleigh stands out, described in a lavish white satin suit and associated with a society devoted to atheistic propositions, such as man existing before Adam and God being an idea or even "dog spelled backward." This philosophical and irreverent note introduces questions about belief, existence, and the nature of divinity, adding a layer of intellectual challenge to Catherine’s dreamlike world.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • Reflects on isolation and the difficulty of enduring it without resorting to illusions.
  • Maintains a clear boundary between dream and reality.

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s mother)

  • Existing in a semi-conscious, dreamlike coma.
  • Her mental state is depicted as a voyage through symbolic landscapes and figures.
  • Acts as a hostess to a surreal assembly of historical, mythological, and symbolic guests.
  • Denies loss, existing in a universe where things are lost but preserved in other forms.

Mr. Spitzer

  • A character who visits Catherine every evening and listens to her dream-like gatherings.
  • Tied to legal errands and earthly concerns, contrasted with Catherine’s ethereal dream world.
  • Judged by Rhadamanthus in the dream for forgotten crimes, symbolizing guilt and judgment.

Research: Themes

Isolation and Endurance

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Vera contemplates how to endure isolation, contrasting herself with her mother, who copes differently through dreams and illusions.
  • Vera is grounded in reality, aware of the dividing line between dream and reality, unlike her mother who dwells in a liminal, dreamlike state.

Dream vs. Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • Catherine inhabits a dream world where places and people symbolize different stages or elements of her mental and emotional journey.
  • Her dream is a voyage through alphabetic symbolic “stations” (P’s, Q’s, R’s), each representing complex ideas, places, or characters.
  • Catherine's dream reflects a universe where loss is denied or transformed rather than accepted.

Memory and Loss

See: Memory and the Past · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Catherine’s dream denies loss, imagining a universe where things are lost but not lost forever.
  • This suggests a coping mechanism for dealing with grief or fading consciousness.

Identity and Multiplicity

See: Identity and the Self

  • Catherine’s dream involves figures like Rameses I, II, and III, representing fragmented selves and layered identities.
  • The dream hints at disjointed time and space, and the coexistence of past and present selves.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Alphabetical Journey (P’s, Q’s, R’s)

See: The Bus and the Journey

  • Represents a symbolic progression or mental voyage.
  • P’s include Pericles, Admiral Peary, Penelope’s web, the Pleiades, and formal penguins.
  • Q’s include Queen Charlotte Islands, Queen Maud Mountains, and naval battles.
  • R’s include a wide array of figures and concepts: Mr. Res Tacamah (a drug bottle, Catherine’s opium/laudanum), Egyptian pharaohs (Rameses I-III), runes, roulades, rubrics, royal masts, rose windows, Roman numerals, riddles, red-caps in Grand Central Station, Roland, Rimski-Korsakov, Sir Joshua Reynolds, the Great Ram constellation, Rhadamanthus, Sir Walter Raleigh, and more.

Queen Maud Mountains

See: Mountains

  • Personified as a cold, snobbish, imperious houseguest, symbolizing perhaps a harsh, distant presence.
  • The detail of her five-hundred-mile-long Brussels lace skirt evokes grandeur, coldness, and isolation.

Mr. Res Tacamah

  • A symbolic figure representing a drug bottle and shifting identities, suggesting notions of altered consciousness or dependency.

Rameses I, II, III

See: Angels, Devils, and Myth

  • Symbolize fragmented identities and the confusion of time within the dream.
  • The pharaoh unwrapping himself reveals the layered nature of self and memory.

Sir Walter Raleigh

  • Imagined with a white satin suit embroidered with pearls and gems.
  • His decapitation and request to be called “Ocean or Water” suggest themes of transformation, fluidity, and martyrdom or sacrifice.
  • Represents atheistic propositions and philosophical skepticism within the dream.

Research: Resources

  • Brussels lace is well known for its delicacy and beauty. Originally it was only made from the finest spun linen thread, which was spun in dark damp rooms to keep the thread from becoming too brittle. Only one ray of light was allowed into the room, and it was arranged so that it fell upon the thread.
  • Queen Charlotte Islands or Haida Gwaii are islands off the Pacific coast of Canada.
  • Gyrfalcon is the largest of the falcon species.
  • Queen Maud Mountains or Queen Maud Land is a section of Antarctica that has been claimed as a dependent territory by Norway.
  • Ross Sea is a deep bay in Antarctica.
  • Mahomet may refer to the Mahomet play by Voltaire. The play is a direct assault on the moral character of Muhammad. Omar is a known historical figure who became second caliph; the characters of Seid and Palmira represent Muhammad's adopted son Zayd ibn Harithah and his wife Zaynab bint Jahsh, though in no way their life resembles the characters. Pierre Milza thought the play criticizes not just Islam but the intolerance of the Catholic Church and its crimes done on behalf of Christ. Voltaire's own statement about it in a letter in 1742 said he tried to show what horrible excesses of fanaticism, led by an impostor, can drive weak minds to commit acts. It is only in another letter dated from the same year that he explains that this plot is an implicit reference to Jacques Clément, the monk who assassinated Henri III in 1589.
  • Rx is Latin for recipere and tacamahac is an aromatic resinous substance used in ointments and incense.
  • Menpehtyre Ramesses I (or Ramses) was the founding pharaoh of ancient Egypt's 19th Dynasty. The dates for his short reign are not completely known but the timeline of late 1292–1290 BC is frequently cited as well as 1295–1294 BC. While Ramesses I was the founder of the 19th Dynasty, his brief reign mainly serves to mark the transition between the reign of Horemheb, who had stabilized Egypt in the late 18th Dynasty, and the rule of the powerful pharaohs of his own dynasty, in particular his son Seti I, and grandson Ramesses II.
  • Ramesses II commonly known as Ramesses the Great, was an Egyptian pharaoh. He was the third ruler of the Nineteenth Dynasty. Along with Thutmose III of the Eighteenth Dynasty, he is often regarded as the greatest, most celebrated, and most powerful pharaoh of the New Kingdom, which itself was the most powerful period of ancient Egypt. He is also widely considered one of ancient Egypt's most successful warrior pharaohs, conducting no fewer than 15 military campaigns, all resulting in victories, excluding the Battle of Kadesh, generally considered a stalemate.
  • Usermaatre Meryamun Ramesses III was the second Pharaoh of the Twentieth Dynasty in Ancient Egypt. Some scholars date his reign from 26 March 1186 to 15 April 1155 BC, and he is considered the last pharaoh of the New Kingdom to have wielded substantial power. His long reign saw the decline of Egyptian political and economic power, linked to a series of invasions and internal economic problems that also plagued pharaohs before him. This coincided with a decline in the cultural sphere of Ancient Egypt.
  • Runes are letters from ancient Germanic alphabets, also called runic alphabets or futharks, which were used primarily to write Germanic languages before the adoption of the Roman alphabet. They are also recognized as symbols with mysterious or magical significance, often used in divination and fortune-telling. Runes were typically carved into wood, stone, or bone.
  • Roulade is an elaborate embellishment of several notes sung to one syllable.
  • A rubric is a word or text written in red ink for emphasis.
  • A royal mast is the top platform of a sailing ship.
  • Rose window is a circular window found in churches and cathedrals.
  • Roman numerals is a numeric system created by ancient Romans.
  • A red-cap in this case is probably a porter at a train terminal.
  • Grand Central Station is a train station on 42nd street in Midtown Manhattan in New York City.
  • Roland was a military leader under Charlemagne who died in the battle at Roncevaux. His life was turned into an epic tale, The Song of Roland which is a highly romanticized account of the Battle of Roncevaux Pass and Roland's death. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, Roland is named Orlando and in the Heaven of Mars together with others who fought for the faith.
  • Rimski-Korsakov was a prominent Russian composer, a member of the group The Mighty Handful (composed music influenced by Russian culture and folklore), and a significant figure in shaping Russian classical music. He is known for his operas, orchestral pieces like Scheherazade and Capriccio Espagnol, and for influencing generations of Russian composers through his work as a professor at the Saint Petersburg Conservatory.
  • Sir Joshua Reynolds was an English painter who specialised in portraits. The art critic John Russell called him one of the major European painters of the 18th century, while Lucy Peltz says he was "the leading portrait artist of the 18th-century and arguably one of the greatest artists in the history of art." He promoted the "Grand Style" in painting, which depended on idealisation of the imperfect. He was a founder and first president of the Royal Academy of Arts and was knighted by George III in 1769. He has been referred to as the 'master who revolutionised British Art.’
  • The Great Ram may refer to the constellation Aries from the zodiac.
  • Raphael’s tomb may refer to Raphael, a famous Italian painter who was buried in the Pantheon.
  • Ronsard was a prominent French Renaissance poet, born in 1524, known for being a leader of the La Pléiade literary movement and for his influential works like Les Odes and Les Amours. He was often called the "prince of poets" and was influential in developing the French sonnet and other poetic forms. Ronsard, with his friend Joachim du Bellay, founded the group of poets known as La Pléiade, which aimed to elevate the French language through poetry inspired by classical works. His most famous works include Les Odes (1550) and Les Amours (1552), which helped establish him as a leading poet. His sonnets are particularly admired, with "Mignonne, allons voir si la rose" being one of his most famous poems. He is credited with perfecting the 12-syllable Alexandrine line of French verse and is known for his rich imagery, classical references, and exploration of themes like love, beauty, and the fleeting nature of life. Ronsard also served as a poet and chaplain to King Charles IX and was a major literary figure during the French Renaissance. He continued to revise and publish his works throughout his life.
  • Rhadamanthus was a famous king of Crete who later in Greek mythology became a judge of the dead.
  • The sister ravens and brother doves may refer to two fairy tales from Italy and Germany.
  • Sir Walter Raleigh was an English statesman, soldier, spy, writer, poet, and explorer.
  • Raleigh's poetry is known as the plain style. His writing contains strong personal treatments of themes such as love, loss, beauty, and time. Most of his poems are short lyrics that were inspired by actual events. In poems Raleigh expresses a contempt of the world attitude more characteristic of the Middle Ages than of the dawning era of humanistic optimism. But his lesser-known long poem, The Ocean's Love to Cynthia, expresses a melancholy sense of history. The poem was written during his imprisonment in the Tower of London. Raleigh wrote a poetic response to Christopher Marlowe's, The Passionate Shepherd to His Love, entitled, The Nymph's Reply to the Shepherd. Raleigh gives an almost line-for-line refutation of Marlowe's sentiments. Years later, the 20th-century poet William Carlos Williams would add, Raleigh was Right, to the conversation. The first line from the poem, “We cannot go to the country, for the country will bring us, no peace…”
