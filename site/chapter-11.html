Chapter 11

Summary

In this short chapter, Vera wonders how she will bear her isolation. She cannot live on illusion as her mother does, and she trusts herself to know where the dream ends and reality begins, as Miss MacIntosh taught her. She imagines how far her mother has traveled through her opium dream, which runs by the alphabet. The P’s must be past by now. The Q’s are hard company: the Queen Charlotte Islands, and the Queen Maud Mountains, who visits as a frigid, silent house guest in a lace skirt five hundred miles long. The R’s are the charming ones, guests who never met in life: Mr. Res Tacamah, the drug bottle; a pharaoh who unwraps into Rameses I, II, and III; Roland, Rimski-Korsakov, Sir Joshua Reynolds, Rhadamanthus, and Sir Walter Raleigh, who takes off his head with his plumed hat. Mr. Spitzer has missed them all, and Catherine will tell him so.

Key passages

From the research

  • Everything Catherine sees in this chapter is Vera’s guess. “She must have reached,” “Perhaps my mother had already passed on.” Vera, who prides herself on knowing where the dream ends, spends the chapter dreaming her mother’s dream for her.
  • The chapter opens with Vera pondering how to endure isolation, recognizing that unlike her mother Catherine, mere illusions are insufficient. She is aware of the boundary between dream and reality, a lesson imparted by Miss MacIntosh, who denies the existence of what does not exist. This sets the thematic framework of the chapter, highlighting the tension between illusion and reality, and the difficulty of coping with solitude without resorting to self-deception.
  • Catherine is described as being in a “wakeful coma,” journeying through an opiate-induced voyage through various symbolic “places” starting with the letter P (Pericles, Penelope’s web, the Pleiades, penguins) and moving onto the Q’s and R’s. This voyage represents a surreal and metaphorical experience, suggesting that Catherine’s consciousness moves through layers of history, mythology, geography, and fantasy. These symbolic names imply a vast, intricate mental landscape, blending real and imagined elements.
  • The Q’s include the Queen Charlotte Islands, Queen Maud Mountains, and a naval battle, each representing different aspects of isolation, coldness, and distance. Queen Maud Mountains, in particular, is portrayed as an imperious, frigid visitor to Catherine’s bedroom, embodying a chilling and overwhelming presence that silences and immobilizes. The description of her skirt as “five hundred miles long and made of pure Brussels lace” evokes a surreal and vast majesty, emphasizing Catherine’s awe and envy.
  • Catherine’s mental journey perhaps continues to the R’s, a group connected by “silken relationships” despite never having met in reality. This assembly includes historical, mythical, and symbolic figures such as Mr. Res Tacamah (a drug bottle), the Egyptian pharaoh Rameses I, II, III (representing fragmented selves and layered identities), runes, royal masts, Roland, Rimski-Korsakov, Sir Joshua Reynolds, and others. The richness of this imagery suggests a complex and paradoxical inner world where time, space, and identity blur.
  • Mr. Spitzer appears as a figure who perpetually misses Catherine’s gatherings of extraordinary company, including great composers, painters, and mythological judges like Rhadamanthus. There is a tone of irony and loss here, as Mr. Spitzer is condemned for forgotten crimes and fails to witness Catherine’s vibrant and mysterious social realm, underscoring themes of absence and missed connection.
  • Among the remarkable guests, Sir Walter Raleigh stands out, described in a lavish white satin suit and associated with a society devoted to atheistic propositions, such as man existing before Adam and God being an idea or even “dog spelled backward.” This philosophical and irreverent note introduces questions about belief, existence, and the nature of divinity, adding a layer of intellectual challenge to Catherine’s dreamlike world.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • Catherine’s calculations admit “no theory of loss in the universe where all things were lost.” Who is right, Catherine or Vera?
  • Queen Maud Mountains hides her face behind a parasol “which happened to be my mother’s.” What does Catherine see when she looks at her guest?
  • The pharaoh learns he “had been partitioned into many selves without his knowing it.” Who else in the novel has been divided this way?

Characters

  • Vera says no “mere illusion would suffice me,” then imagines her mother’s dream in detail. How sure is she of the line between them?
  • Catherine is “this great hostess who was in exile.” What is she exiled from?
  • Rhadamanthus finds Mr. Spitzer “guilty of many forgotten crimes.” What crime might Catherine charge him with?

Connections

  • Mr. Res Tacamah was “the drug bottle with ears” in Chapter 2. What has changed in him?
  • Mr. Spitzer’s silent music in Chapter 2 meets Rimski-Korsakov’s music “which was not altogether silent.” Why does Catherine compare them?
  • Raleigh asks to be called “Ocean or Water.” How does the sea keep entering Catherine’s closed room?

Reflection

  • Is there a kind of loneliness that imagination makes worse rather than better?
  • Catherine never leaves her bed, so the mountain comes to her. What is gained and lost by living this way?
  • Can a person know where her own dreams end?

Research: Characters

Vera Cartwheel

  • Reflects on isolation and the difficulty of enduring it without resorting to illusions.
  • Maintains a clear boundary between dream and reality.

Catherine Cartwheel (Vera’s Mother)

  • Existing in a semi-conscious, dreamlike coma.
  • Her mental state is depicted as a voyage through symbolic landscapes and figures.
  • Acts as a hostess to a surreal assembly of historical, mythological, and symbolic guests.
  • Denies loss, existing in a universe where things are lost but preserved in other forms.

Mr. Spitzer

  • Absent from every gathering; Catherine will remind him afterward of all he missed.
  • Tied to legal errands and earthly concerns, contrasted with Catherine’s ethereal dream world.
  • Judged by Rhadamanthus in the dream for forgotten crimes, symbolizing guilt and judgment.

Miss MacIntosh

  • Her denial of “those things which did not exist” is the lesson Vera leans on to tell dream from reality.

Research: Themes

Isolation and Endurance

See: Isolation and Connection

  • Vera contemplates how to endure isolation, contrasting herself with her mother, who copes differently through dreams and illusions.
  • Vera is grounded in reality, aware of the dividing line between dream and reality, unlike her mother who dwells in a liminal, dreamlike state.

Dream versus Reality

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • Catherine inhabits a dream world where places and people symbolize different stages or elements of her mental and emotional journey.
  • Her dream is a voyage through alphabetic symbolic “stations” (P’s, Q’s, R’s), each representing complex ideas, places, or characters.
  • Catherine’s dream reflects a universe where loss is denied or transformed rather than accepted.

Memory and Loss

See: Memory and the Past · Grief, Loss, and Mourning

  • Catherine’s dream denies loss, imagining a universe where things are lost but not lost forever.
  • This suggests a coping mechanism for dealing with grief or fading consciousness.

Identity and Multiplicity

See: Identity and the Self

  • Catherine’s dream involves figures like Rameses I, II, and III, representing fragmented selves and layered identities.
  • The dream hints at disjointed time and space, and the coexistence of past and present selves.

Research: Resources

  • “entertaining the P’s, the Q’s, the R’s”: “Mind your P’s and Q’s,” the old warning to watch one’s manners, suits a hostess who greets every guest by letter.
  • “her opiate voyage through nowhere”: Thomas De Quincey’s opium dreams in Confessions of an English Opium-Eater (1821) carried him to Egypt and the East: “I was buried, for a thousand years, in stone coffins, with mummies and sphinxes.” See Opium, The History of Opium, and the Chapter 4 resources.
    • “Nowhere” is what Thomas More’s Utopia (1516) means in Greek, ou-topos, “no place.”
  • “Pericles”: Pericles (c. 495-429 BC) led Athens in its golden age and had the Parthenon built. See Shakespeare.
    • Shakespeare’s Pericles, Prince of Tyre (c. 1608) is a voyage play. Its hero loses his wife, buried at sea in a chest, and his daughter, and finds both again.
  • “Admiral Peary”: Robert E. Peary claimed the North Pole in 1909 and was made a rear admiral in 1911. Matthew Henson, the Black explorer beside him, by his own account reached the spot first and got little credit for decades. In 1897 Peary brought six Inuit from Greenland to New York for the American Museum of Natural History, and four died within months. The museum staged a fake burial for the boy Minik while it kept his father Qisuk’s skeleton. See Exploration and the Poles, Society, Class, and Race, and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “Penelope’s web”: In the Odyssey, Penelope weaves a shroud for Laertes by day and unravels it by night to hold off the suitors. See Homer’s Epics and the Chapter 5 resources.
  • “the Pleiades”: The Pleiades are the seven daughters of Atlas, set in the sky as a star cluster in Taurus. They return at the end of the chapter in Ronsard’s Pléiade. See Astronomy and Greek Mythology.
  • “a naval battle, an island, and a continent”: The battle is never named. Quiberon Bay (1759) is the famous Q: Admiral Hawke chased the French fleet into a rocky bay off Brittany in a November gale.
  • “Queen Charlotte Islands”: The Queen Charlotte Islands, off the Pacific coast of Canada, were named in 1787 for the ship Queen Charlotte, which was named for George III’s queen. They are the home of the Haida and are now called Haida Gwaii. See Birds.
    • The gyrfalcon is the largest of the falcons, a bird of the Arctic. White gyrfalcons were gifts for kings, and Catherine kept falconer’s gloves in Chapter 2.
    • Goldeneyes are diving ducks called “whistlers” for the sound of their wings in flight.
  • “a wild swan absent, one wild swan more”: In Andersen’s “The Wild Swans” (1838), eleven brothers are turned into swans, and their sister weaves nettle shirts to free them. The last shirt is unfinished, and the youngest is left with a swan’s wing. See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Birds.
  • “admitted no theory of loss in the universe where all things were lost”: Lavoisier’s law of the conservation of mass (1789), as it is usually summed up: “Nothing is lost, nothing is created, everything is transformed.”
  • “the proud, cold, utterly snobbish Queen Maud Mountains”: The Queen Maud Mountains rise at the head of the Ross Ice Shelf, on the south edge of the Ross Sea. Roald Amundsen found them in 1911 on his way to the South Pole and named them for Queen Maud of Norway, a daughter of King Edward VII of England. Queen Maud Land is a different region of Antarctica, claimed by Norway. See Exploration and the Poles, Mountains, and Snow, Ice, and Winter.
    • The Ross Sea, a deep bay of the Southern Ocean, is named for James Clark Ross, who sailed into it in 1841.
  • “colder than Boston society”: John Collins Bossidy’s toast (1910): “And this is good old Boston, / The home of the bean and the cod, / Where the Lowells talk only to Cabots, / And the Cabots talk only to God.” See Society, Class, and Race.
  • “coming like the mountain to Mahomet”: The proverb comes from Francis Bacon’s essay “Of Boldness” (1625). Mahomet called a hill to come to him, and when it would not, he said, “If the hill will not come to Mahomet, Mahomet will go to the hill.” Here the mountain comes to Catherine, who never leaves her bed. See Mountains.
  • “made of pure Brussels lace”: Brussels lace is well known for its delicacy and beauty. Originally it was only made from the finest spun linen thread, which was spun in dark damp rooms to keep the thread from becoming too brittle. Only one ray of light was allowed into the room, and it was arranged so that it fell upon the thread. See Clothing, Hats, and Skirts.
  • “pyramid-shaped ices”: Ices were molded into shapes for grand dinners. The pyramids Catherine serves come just before the pharaoh arrives. See Ancient Egypt.
  • “Mr. Res Tacamah who was the drug bottle”: His name may join Rx to tacamahac. Rx stands for the Latin recipe, “take,” at the head of a prescription, and tacamahac is an aromatic resin used in ointments and incense. In Chapter 2 he was “the drug bottle with ears to hear her ravings.” See Mr. Res Tacamah, Opium, and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “Rameses I, II, III”: The royal mummies were found in 1881 in a hidden tomb at Deir el-Bahri, where priests had moved and rewrapped them, some in other kings’ coffins. Shelley’s “Ozymandias” (1818) takes its name from the Greek form of Ramesses II’s throne name, User-maat-re. See Ancient Egypt, Romantic Poets, and the Chapter 2 resources.
    • Menpehtyre Ramesses I (or Ramses) was the founding pharaoh of ancient Egypt’s 19th Dynasty. The dates for his short reign are not completely known but the timeline of late 1292-1290 BC is frequently cited as well as 1295-1294 BC. While Ramesses I was the founder of the 19th Dynasty, his brief reign mainly serves to mark the transition between the reign of Horemheb, who had stabilized Egypt in the late 18th Dynasty, and the rule of the powerful pharaohs of his own dynasty, in particular his son Seti I, and grandson Ramesses II.
    • Ramesses II, commonly known as Ramesses the Great, was an Egyptian pharaoh. He was the third ruler of the Nineteenth Dynasty. Along with Thutmose III of the Eighteenth Dynasty, he is often regarded as the greatest, most celebrated, and most powerful pharaoh of the New Kingdom, which itself was the most powerful period of ancient Egypt. He is also widely considered one of ancient Egypt’s most successful warrior pharaohs, conducting no fewer than 15 military campaigns, all resulting in victories, excluding the Battle of Kadesh, generally considered a stalemate.
    • Usermaatre Meryamun Ramesses III was the second pharaoh of the Twentieth Dynasty in ancient Egypt. Some scholars date his reign from 26 March 1186 to 15 April 1155 BC, and he is considered the last pharaoh of the New Kingdom to have wielded substantial power. His long reign saw the decline of Egyptian political and economic power, linked to a series of invasions and internal economic problems that also plagued pharaohs before him. This coincided with a decline in the cultural sphere of ancient Egypt. He took Ramesses II’s throne name and modeled his temple at Medinet Habu on Ramesses II’s, one king dreaming another’s dreams.
  • “partitioned into many selves without his knowing it”: The Egyptians held that a person was made of many parts: the ka, the life force; the ba, the soul that flies from the tomb; the name; the shadow; and the heart, weighed at judgment. See Ancient Egypt and Identity and the Self.
  • “runes, roulades, rubrics, royal masts, rose windows, Roman numerals, many riddles, many red-caps in the Grand Central Station”: Catherine’s R’s run from letters and songs to ships, churches, and numbers.
    • Runes are letters from ancient Germanic alphabets, also called runic alphabets or futharks, which were used primarily to write Germanic languages before the adoption of the Roman alphabet. They are also recognized as symbols with mysterious or magical significance, often used in divination and fortune-telling. Runes were typically carved into wood, stone, or bone.
    • A roulade is an elaborate embellishment of several notes sung to one syllable.
    • A rubric is a word or text written in red ink for emphasis. In the Book of Common Prayer, the rubrics are the instructions printed in red, the stage directions of the services in Chapter 10.
    • A royal mast is the topmost section of a sailing ship’s mast, above the topgallant.
    • A rose window is a round window of stained glass found in churches and cathedrals.
    • Roman numerals are a numeric system created by ancient Romans. They number Rameses I, II, III.
    • A red-cap is a railroad porter. Grand Central’s red caps were mostly Black men from 1909 on. The terminal did not pay them, and they lived on tips until the 1940s. In 1937 red caps organized the International Brotherhood of Red Caps.
    • Grand Central, officially Grand Central Terminal (1913), stands at 42nd Street and Park Avenue in Midtown Manhattan.
  • “Roland who was killed at Roncevalles”: Roland was a military leader under Charlemagne who died at the Battle of Roncevaux Pass in 778, when Basques ambushed Charlemagne’s rear guard. The Song of Roland (c. 1100) turns them into Saracens and has Roland blow his horn too late. In Dante’s Divine Comedy, Roland is named Orlando and placed in the Heaven of Mars with others who fought for the faith (Paradiso 18). See Dante.
  • “Rimski-Korsakov who never came except in Mr. Spitzer’s absence”: Rimski-Korsakov was a prominent Russian composer, a member of the Mighty Handful, five composers who drew on Russian folk music, and a significant figure in shaping Russian classical music. He is known for his operas, orchestral pieces like Scheherazade (1888), named for the bride who tells a tale night after night to stay alive, and Capriccio Espagnol, and for influencing generations of Russian composers through his work as a professor at the Saint Petersburg Conservatory. He sailed around the world as a young naval officer. See Music and Composers and Fairy Tales and Folklore.
    • “music which was not altogether silent”: Mr. Spitzer has written “only silent music, that which, for many years now, no one else had ever heard” (Chapter 2).
  • “Sir Joshua Reynolds”: Sir Joshua Reynolds (1723-1792) was an English painter who specialized in portraits. He promoted the “Grand Style” in painting, which depended on idealization of the imperfect. He was a founder and first president of the Royal Academy of Arts and was knighted by George III in 1769.
  • “the constellation known as the Great Ram”: The Great Ram is Aries, the first sign of the zodiac, the golden ram whose fleece Jason sought. See Astronomy and Greek Mythology.
  • “Raphael’s tomb”: Raphael (1483-1520), the Italian painter, is buried in the Pantheon in Rome. Pietro Bembo’s epitaph reads, “Here lies Raphael, by whom Nature feared to be outdone while he lived, and when he died, feared that she herself would die.”
  • “the songs of Ronsard”: Ronsard was a prominent French Renaissance poet, born in 1524, known for being a leader of the La Pléiade literary movement and for his influential works like Les Odes and Les Amours. He was often called the “prince of poets” and was influential in developing the French sonnet and other poetic forms. Ronsard, with his friend Joachim du Bellay, founded the group of poets known as La Pléiade, which aimed to elevate the French language through poetry inspired by classical works. His most famous works include Les Odes (1550) and Les Amours (1552), which helped establish him as a leading poet. His sonnets are particularly admired, with “Mignonne, allons voir si la rose” being one of his most famous poems. He is credited with perfecting the 12-syllable Alexandrine line of French verse and is known for his rich imagery, classical references, and exploration of themes like love, beauty, and the fleeting nature of life. Ronsard also served as a poet and chaplain to King Charles IX and was a major literary figure during the French Renaissance. He continued to revise and publish his works throughout his life. The group took its name from the Pleiades, the seven stars among Catherine’s P’s. Ronsard wrote his poems to be sung, and composers set them in his lifetime.
  • “Tracing a lost heir to no property”: Mr. Spitzer has “practiced law in only the most desultory fashion, often without fee, preferring the lost causes” (Chapter 2). See Joachim Spitzer and the Chapter 2 resources.
  • “Rhadamanthus”: Rhadamanthus, son of Zeus and Europa and brother of Minos, was a king of Crete who became a judge of the dead. In Virgil’s Aeneid (Book 6), he forces the dead to confess the crimes they hid on earth, as he finds Mr. Spitzer “guilty of many forgotten crimes.” He returns in Chapter 14. See Greek Mythology.
  • “the sister ravens, the brother doves”: The Grimms’ “The Seven Ravens” and Giambattista Basile’s “The Seven Doves” (1634-36) both tell of brothers changed into birds and a sister who saves them. Young makes the ravens sisters. See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Birds.
  • “Sir Walter Raleigh in his marvelous white satin suit embroidered with seed pearls”: Sir Walter Raleigh (c. 1552-1618) was an English statesman, soldier, spy, writer, poet, and explorer. He was famous at Elizabeth’s court for the pearls and jewels on his clothes, even his shoes. See Sir Walter Raleigh and Exploration and the Poles.
    • Raleigh’s poetry is known as the plain style. His writing contains strong personal treatments of themes such as love, loss, beauty, and time. Most of his poems are short lyrics that were inspired by actual events. In poems Raleigh expresses a contempt-of-the-world attitude more characteristic of the Middle Ages than of the dawning era of humanistic optimism. But his lesser-known long poem, The Ocean’s Love to Cynthia, expresses a melancholy sense of history. The poem was written during his imprisonment in the Tower of London. Raleigh answered Christopher Marlowe’s “The Passionate Shepherd to His Love” with “The Nymph’s Reply to the Shepherd.” Raleigh gives an almost line-for-line refutation of Marlowe’s sentiments. Centuries later, William Carlos Williams added “Raleigh Was Right” (1940) to the conversation. It begins, “We cannot go to the country / for the country will bring us / no peace.”
  • “when he removed his white plumed hat, removed also his head”: Raleigh was beheaded at Westminster on October 29, 1618. Of the axe he said, “This is sharp medicine, but it is a physician for all diseases.” His widow kept his embalmed head for the rest of her life. The poem he wrote the night before begins, “Even such is Time, that takes in trust / Our youth, our joys, our all we have, / And pays us but with earth and dust.” See Sir Walter Raleigh.
  • “his intimate name, Ocean or Water”: Elizabeth called him “Water.” Spenser named him “the Shepherd of the Ocean” in Colin Clouts Come Home Againe (1595), and Raleigh wrote The Ocean’s Love to Cynthia, with the Queen as the moon who rules the tides. See Sir Walter Raleigh and The Sea and Water.
  • “a society of night devoted exclusively to atheistic propositions”: “The school of night” comes from Love’s Labour’s Lost (4.3): “Black is the badge of hell, / The hue of dungeons and the school of night.” The phrase became a name for Raleigh’s circle with Thomas Harriot and Christopher Marlowe, men accused of doubting Genesis and God. In 1594 an inquiry at Cerne Abbas examined charges of atheism against Raleigh’s circle. See Sir Walter Raleigh, Shakespeare, and Genesis.
    • “man had existed before Adam”: The Baines note (1593) charged Marlowe with saying that ancient authors “have assuredly written of above 16 thousand years agone whereas Adam is proved to have lived within 6 thousand years.” See the Chapter 9 resources on pre-Adamites.
    • “God is nothing but an idea in the mind”: Ludwig Feuerbach’s The Essence of Christianity (1841) argued that God is human nature projected outward.
    • “God is dog spelled backward”: The word read backward is the last of the R’s “many riddles.”

Fine lace made by hand in Brussels, Belgium, from linen thread.

Thickened or stiffened, as with cold.

An attendant at a royal court.

Very cold; stiffly unfriendly.

Icy; like a glacier.

The largest falcon, a bird of the Arctic.

Proud and commanding.
Lustrous
Shining with a soft glow.

An optical illusion, common in deserts, in which hot air bends light so that distant water or cities seem to appear.

A drug made from opium; anything that dulls the senses.

Curved like a parabola; also, like a parable.
Paradox
A statement or situation that seems to contradict itself and yet may be true.

A light umbrella carried for shade from the sun.

Ghostly; like a phantom or an illusion.
Plumed
Decorated with feathers.
Proposition
A statement put forward as true, to be argued.
Red-cap
A railroad porter who carries luggage.
Rose window
A round church window of stained glass, with tracery spreading from the center like petals.
Roulade
A rapid run of notes sung to one syllable.

The topmost section of a sailing ship’s mast, above the topgallant.

A heading or instruction printed in red, as in a prayer book.
Rune
A letter of the old Germanic alphabets, often thought to hold magic.
