Research notes: Chapter 26 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 26

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Peron Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

The chapter opens its deepest wound here. Mr. Spitzer is not one person. He is constitutively doubled: identical twin to his dead brother Peron, carrying that brother's face, debts, and unlived life. The text makes this structural, not metaphorical:

"living his brother's life and dying his brother's death, it seemed to him, and so he looked back upon himself as one who was dead."

This is not psychological confusion. This is ontological plurality made literal. The self cannot be singular when the body that carries it is shared, when the face another wore is the face you see in the mirror. Mr. Spitzer does not know where Joachim ends and Peron begins, and crucially, the text never resolves this. Not One Self is not presented as a wound to be healed but as the permanent condition of his existence. He is the living and the dead simultaneously. He is the conservative and the reckless, the faithful and the absent, the musician and the lawyer: all at once, irresolvably.

Even his professional identity confirms this multiplicity: he deals with "heirs who had been heiresses, heiresses who had been heirs", as if the whole world of inheritance mirrors his own inability to be a single, stable self.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter is saturated with constructed realities that have achieved the force of actual ones. The Boston lady who believed herself Cleopatra is the clearest emblem:

"an elderly Boston lady who, having imagined all her life that she was the reincarnation of Cleopatra...having had...a Sphinx built in her adjoining garden...a rival Sphinx, a greater pyramid than her neighbor could afford"

This is not presented as pathology. It is presented as a life fully lived inside an illusion that generated real consequences: real Sphinxes, real cats, real legal estates, real income streams. The illusion became the architecture of reality. Pillar 2 says "reality is not what we find; it is what we make", and this Boston Cleopatra built her reality with full commitment, down to the Egyptian securities in her estate.

Meanwhile, Mr. Spitzer's mother inhabits her own constructed world of "sleeping car porters with golden roosters on their heads and headless horsemen", displacing the visible Mr. Spitzer with narcotic visions. Her opium is not escapism; it is an alternative construction of the real, one that the text treats with the same ontological weight as Mr. Spitzer's legal documents.

The chapter quietly insists: there is no sunlit world outside the cave. Mr. Spitzer's own vision of heaven, "a state of mind when we should have completely forgotten who we were", is itself another constructed illusion, a dream of oblivion he builds to make the present bearable.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This may be the chapter's most dominant philosophical register. Mr. Spitzer does not grieve and recover. He accumulates grief as sediment, and the text renders this with almost geological patience:

"the attitude of a perpetual grief which could not seem to settle upon any one point of view"

The phrase could not "settle upon any one point of view" is crucial. His grief is not focused, not directed, not moving toward resolution; it is diffuse, atmospheric, climatic. It is the weather inside which all his thinking occurs.

The shock of identifying his brother's body in the morgue, "among homeless men and fallen women and faceless creatures and bloated corpses of the dead", is described as something he "continually relived", not something he passed through. Grief here is not a stage. It is a recursive loop, a permanent present tense dressed as past tense.

Even his professional life is organized around grief: he guards the dead, administers their wishes, visits their houses, pays their debts. He is, structurally, a man whose entire vocation is the management of accumulated loss. And his personal grief (for his brother, for his refused marriage proposal, for his unfinished music) layers beneath this professional grief without displacing it.

"the words which were never to be spoken"

That phrase enacts Pillar 3 perfectly. The unspoken words are not behind him. They are still present, still pressing, still the weather.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Mr. Spitzer is defined by his willingness to stay in the room. He sits up with corpses. He visits the dead lady's house and pays his respects to the cats. He returns, week after week, to a woman who ignores him:

"His brother would not have sat up all night with any corpse, for his brother had been afraid of death."

The contrast with Peron is the philosophical hinge of the chapter. Peron was the man of action, the man of the present moment, the man who would have "swept my mother into the living sunlight". But Mr. Spitzer is the Witness: the one who stays, who watches, who endures the passage without intervening:

"Mr. Spitzer did, the passage of a dying soul from this world to the next"

This is Pillar 4 made biographical: "the central ethical stance is not to fix or conclude, but to stay in the room." Mr. Spitzer cannot rescue his mother. He cannot finish his music. He cannot resolve his identity. But he stays. He watches. He pays the bills, brings the winter violets, endures the ignoring. His faithfulness is not productive. It is attentive. And the text presents this (quietly, without sentimentality) as the more profound ethical act.

His legal profession mirrors this: he does not make reality, he witnesses it, reading old wills, entering dim rooms, searching through secret drawers. He is professionally constituted as the one who sees what others have left behind.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Mr. Spitzer's unfinished music is the clearest emblem of Pillar 5. It is never completed. It exists only "for himself or for no mortal ear". Yet he returns to it. The chapter asks whether his brother would have "attempted to finish, as Mr. Spitzer would always ask, Mr. Spitzer's unfinished music, even to add a hesitant note".

The answer is clearly no: Peron would not have returned to the unfinished thing. But Mr. Spitzer does. He builds anyway, knowing the building is provisional. The music is the figure for his whole existence: a recursive return to something that cannot be completed because completion would require a self that is single, stable, and free from grief; none of which he is.

His legal work also operates recursively: "his brother's debts to pay long after he surely seemed to have paid them". The debts return. He pays them again. The building is never finished. This is not failure; it is, as Pillar 5 insists, how it works.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

The chapter is structurally enacting this pillar. It is itself an unfinished sentence: a long, winding, subordinate-clause-laden meditation that never arrives at a conclusion. The prose style performs the ethics of incompletion.

Mr. Spitzer's marriage proposal was never completed; it was refused "for the last fickle and unconstant time", which implies it was refused multiple times, which implies it was offered multiple times, which implies he kept returning to an incompletion he could not resolve. His offer of "the ideal and incorruptible marriage" was itself too complete, too ideal, and the text gently suggests that this very demand for perfection was what made it impossible.

His vision of heaven, "a complete oblivion", is the fantasy of completion, of the concluded sentence. But the text places this vision inside a man who is himself the ongoing sentence, who cannot conclude, who pays the same debts again and again. The demand for completion is, as Pillar 6 says, "its own violence against experience."

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Every significant figure in this chapter lives at the edge of categories. The Boston lady is neither mad nor sane; she is a fully functioning eccentric whose illusions have legal standing. The cats are neither pets nor heirs; they are legal property owners. The old caretaker is described as "like an old mummified pharaoh wrapped in his body which was his boat"; neither fully living nor fully dead.

Mr. Spitzer himself is the marginal figure par excellence: neither the dead brother nor fully the living one, neither the musician he wished to be nor entirely the lawyer he became, neither lover nor stranger to my mother. He exists in the categorical gaps:

"heirs who had been heiresses, heiresses who had been heirs"

These are not anomalies in his professional world. They are evidence, as Pillar 7 insists, of the constructed nature of the categories themselves. The marginal cases (the Cleopatra lady, the cats-as-heirs, the twin who lives his dead brother's life) are not exceptions to the legal and social order. They are the places where that order's arbitrariness becomes visible.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This pillar may be the most structurally embedded in the chapter's form. The prose itself operates sedimentarily; each sentence deposits a new layer of past onto the present, and the present is always being pressed upward by what lies beneath.

"His professional problems were easier when they were simple, certainly, and not so involved as his personal problems."

The chapter then sinks immediately into the past (the Boston lady, the cats, the caretaker) and these memories are not decorative. They are the ground Mr. Spitzer stands on as he sits in my mother's room. The past is not behind him. It is beneath him, pressing up.

His brother's death is the deepest stratum:

"that hour of terrible shock he continually relived"

The word "continually" is the key. The past is not receding. It is accumulating. Every new day adds another layer of Peron's debts paid, another layer of my mother ignored, another layer of music unfinished: all pressing upward through the present moment.

Even the cats embody sedimentary time: "each having nine lives", they seem exempt from time's passage, accumulating lives rather than losing them. The estate itself (Egyptian securities, New England shipping, government bonds) is a sediment of historical time compressed into a legal document.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely self-conscious about the constructedness of its own language. Mr. Spitzer must "elaborately apologize" for using his brother's language, the language of horse racing and competition, because language carries its speaker's location inside it. Peron's language ("all horses should come in first") is not neutral; it encodes a worldview of competition and immediacy that is philosophically incompatible with Mr. Spitzer's elegiac register.

Mr. Spitzer's own language is characterized by its hesitations, its qualifications, its subordinate clauses: "he could believe in probably no hell", "he tremulously believed", "as he would always say, slightly yawning". This is language that names its own uncertainty, that refuses to speak from nowhere, that locates itself inside a particular consciousness shaped by grief, doubt, and philosophical scrupulousness. The contrast with Peron's implied directness (a man of action, not of elaborately qualified sentences) is itself a philosophical argument: the honest language is the hesitant one, the one that knows it is constructed, the one that cannot make confident declarations because it knows the ground is always shifting.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Mr. Spitzer gestures toward transcendence (heaven, oblivion, "the heavenly harmonies forever fixed above the floods of time") but the text consistently returns him to the immanent. His transcendence is always inside the ordinary world, not beyond it.

The winter violets are the clearest image:

even to "have brought to a sick lady a bouquet of winter violets plucked from a secret place, even in the midst of the whirling snows, the furious blast"

This is not a grand gesture. It is a small, faithful, recurring act: flowers brought through snow to a woman who may not notice them. The sacred here lives exactly where Pillar 10 says it does: "inside the ordinary world." Not in the Egyptian securities or the legal documents, but in the winter violets, in the unfinished music, in the act of staying when you could leave.

The cats on golden dishes "fit for the dead pharaohs" perform the same function: the sacred (Egyptian royalty, pharaonic ritual) has migrated entirely into the immanent (Siamese cats, a Boston garden, a legal estate). The transcendent has not disappeared. It has become the texture of the ordinary.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This is perhaps the pillar that most completely illuminates the chapter's deepest structure. Mr. Spitzer is explicitly described as the place where time crosses itself:

"living his brother's life and dying his brother's death, it seemed to him, and so he looked back upon himself as one who was dead."

He is not a self moving through time. He is where Peron's time and Joachim's time cross. He carries his brother's face, pays his brother's debts, lives in the space his brother's death opened. He did not choose this. It is structural. The crossing point is not a metaphor; it is his literal ontological condition as an identical twin whose brother died.

And he is simultaneously constituting the ground others will stand on: he guards my mother's estate, pays the servants, maintains the house. He is the sediment being deposited for whoever comes after. His unfinished music is the cultural inheritance he is leaving: "for no mortal ear", perhaps, but still being written, still being deposited.

"the journey might be endless like some of those journeys taken by my mother"

The journey through the lower world to reach the upper world; this is the Crossing Point made spatial. You must pass through what was deposited before you. The ancestors are in the sediment. The carrying continues.
