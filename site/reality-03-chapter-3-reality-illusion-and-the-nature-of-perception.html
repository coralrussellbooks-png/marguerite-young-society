Chapter 3: Reality, Illusion, and the Nature of Perception

On Chapter 3 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Reality and Illusion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 2: Introduction to Major Characters and Themes · Exploring Reality and Illusion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling · Chapter 4: Identity, Selfhood, and Otherness →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

Every character in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling looks at the world through something. Catherine looks through opium. Miss MacIntosh looks through common sense, which she holds as firmly as her black umbrella. Mr. Spitzer looks through failing eyes that split every object in two. The bus driver carrying Vera toward What Cheer looks through whiskey and a curtain of his own hair. Vera looks through all of them at once, since each has had a hand in teaching her how to see. The novel never gives us a character who looks at the world through nothing.

That absence is the subject of this chapter. Chapter 1 set out the claim that illusion is primary: most of what passes for solid ground is built and then kept up by repetition. Perception is the hardest test of that claim. Money and borders are easy to recognize as agreements. Seeing feels different. The eyes open and the world is simply there. Young’s wager is that even this is made. Every act of seeing is already an act of interpretation. Her dreamers and her realists do not differ because one group sees while the other imagines. Both groups imagine. What separates them is how much they know about it.

Catherine’s Case for the Dream

Chapter 2 shows what Catherine’s coma looks like. What matters here is that she has reasons for it, and the reasons add up to a theory of perception.

Young states it in one passage: “My mother pretended that the real was the dream, that the dream was the real.” The verb is pretended. Catherine is not fooled. She knows she has switched the terms, and she defends the switch as common to everyone, “a universal failure, that on which the whole of life depended.” Then she gives her argument. Reality carries “an aspect of fateful disappointment, of falling short, just through its being unreal, just through its being realized, through its omissions of other realizations.” Every real event is one possibility chosen at the cost of all the others, “the other train through the other landscape.” The dream keeps every train running: “If ears could see, if eyes could hear, if hearts could be everywhere and omniscient!”

This is a serious position, and the novel lets it stand at full strength. Catherine’s complaint about reality is not that it hurts, though it has. Her complaint is that perception narrows. To see one thing is to miss everything else, and a mind that has once imagined everything will find the one thing thin. Her dreams answer that thinness by refusing to choose. As a result she “could not tell where the divide was between what was real and what was unreal, for the unreal things were real to her.” Young goes further: “The dreams begot the only realities, and the dreams had begotten her, and she was their creation.” Catherine does not stand outside her illusions, arranging them. They made her, and they go on making her.

That sentence is Pillar Two in its most extreme form. Even the self who might step back to judge the dream is one of the dream’s products. It is also where the novel shows the price. A mind that refuses every limit also refuses every other person, because other people are limits: they are there, particular, and they cannot be dreamed away. The daughter at the bedside becomes one more figure among the guests. The dream protects Catherine from disappointment by protecting her from everything real enough to disappoint her, and that includes love.

Miss MacIntosh’s Framework

Against this, Miss MacIntosh holds a theory of perception as plain as her corset: “Truth was this bare simplicity, this unequivocal distance from one point to another point.” For her, seeing is measuring. The world is there, the eye crosses it in a straight line, and the only error is adding something along the way. Seeing is also a moral act. “The truth was better than the lie, even should the lie seem truer than the truth,” she teaches, and “ours was the freedom to make a choice.” Catherine says the dream made her. Miss MacIntosh claims the right to choose.

Young honors this. Miss MacIntosh is “the one person never dreaming where all might dream,” a guide of whom Vera says “never would she secede her common sense to the illusion.” The same sentence gives her away, though. She would also never believe “that there was not a framework in which all must live.” Her realism does not rest on bare perception. It rests on faith in a framework, a structure she trusts because it has to be there, since she could not live without it. That is exactly what the pillar describes. She has not found bedrock. She has decided to treat one construction as bedrock, and she keeps it standing through daily repetition: the tasks, the proverbs, the exercise, the order.

Late in the novel she says so herself. Chapter 2 tells the story of the baldness and the wig, and Chapter 7 reads it as an unmasking. Afterward she speaks to Vera while “straightening her wig.” Love between people, she says, “is a system of mutual deception.” Then comes the sentence that revises everything she taught: “If I warned you against those lies and illusions surrounding us, I meant only those which we ourselves have made. Every man has a porcelain crown like mine.”

The confession is exact. She does not take back her war on illusion. She narrows it. Some illusions are simply part of being human, the crowns everyone wears over what time and loss have taken, and those she no longer condemns. Her quarrel was always with the illusions people build on purpose to hide from themselves. This is a far more livable position than the one she began with, and it is close to the position of this book. The line between honest and dishonest is not the line between the real and the made. It runs through the made.

Seeing Double, Seeing Drunk

Between the two women stand the men whose eyes fail them, and their failures make the argument physical.

Chapter 2 portrays Mr. Spitzer’s divided self, and Chapter 7 gives it a longer meditation. Here what matters is his eyes. He “found it increasingly difficult to focus his vision, to tell the difference between near and far.” He walks through “constantly changing streets, here in this world where everything which was not was and everything which was was not, including Mr. Spitzer with his portfolio, his address book, and a map of Boston.” He carries the map as if checking it would help. It is Miss MacIntosh’s framework shrunk to pocket size, a paper certainty held up against a world that has stopped holding still.

Lost in the fog of his own thinking, he reaches the central image of this chapter. He wonders whether to submit himself to “that great searchlight of consciousness which pointed out the real, unreal, drew a line between the lights and the shadow.” Then Young turns the image around: “The searchlight made the light it lived by. The light lighted only the light and never the darkness, for when the darkness was lighted, it was not the darkness but was the light.” Consciousness cannot inspect anything outside itself, because whatever it inspects is already lit and already its own. The dark as it is in itself, the world before anyone sees it, cannot be seen. Looking turns the thing looked at into a thing seen. Mr. Spitzer’s bad eyes only make obvious what is true of good ones.

Moses Hunnecker, the bus driver, makes the same point in a rougher voice. Driving through a downpour, singing and half shouting at his passengers, he boasts of “my long hair streaming like a curtain over my eyes, but I can see better when I am drunk than you can see when you are sober.” He can “see through the windshield,” and he can “see what’s coming from before, behind, having this rear mirror view.” Then he adds a line that could hang over Catherine’s bed: “It’s always the same, whichever medicine you take, always the same result.” Opium, whiskey, common sense, the lawyer’s map: each is a medicine for the same condition, the impossibility of seeing without an instrument. The man at the wheel of the novel’s one vehicle is drunk, half blinded by his own hair, and watching behind him in a mirror, and the bus goes forward anyway.

Vera Between the Lenses

Vera inherits both theories and trusts neither for long. At one point she claims her nursemaid’s gift: “I would always be able to tell just where the dream ended, where the reality began, even as Miss MacIntosh had taught me through her denial of those things which did not exist.” The phrase “through her denial” is the crack. Miss MacIntosh did not teach the line between dream and waking by showing what was real. She taught it by refusing what was not. The line was drawn by a refusal, which means someone drew it.

Elsewhere Vera admits how little the lesson held. She flees her mother’s “background of illusion” and finds it waiting ahead of her “even as the foreground,” until “there seemed no truth but what the erroneous mind provided.” As a child she would shut her eyes against Miss MacIntosh and “look toward the wild ocean when she spoke, for that seemed a vision more secure.” The ocean, which holds nothing fixed, felt safer than the nursemaid, who showed only fixed things. Even as a child, Vera suspected that the solid world was the less trustworthy picture.

The night of the unmasking confirms the suspicion, and Vera’s account of it is the novel’s fullest statement about perception. “That night, the curtain of illusion had been stripped away, it seemed, even as if the illusion were the only reality, and our self-deception was the only rose.” Behind the curtain she did not find a truth. She found knowledge: “that each must wear a mask which screens him from himself,” that “the external world we take for granted is but insubstantial as a mad man’s dream,” and that “we are always bald when we are robbed of our illusions.” The unveiling does not deliver the real. It delivers a bare head, and the bare head at once becomes a new image, charged with pity, fear, and love. Stripping away one illusion uncovers the next layer instead of bedrock, and what the eye finds there is still shaped by the one who sees it.

Perception as Reading: Merleau-Ponty and Weil

Two twentieth-century thinkers help name what Young dramatizes.

Maurice Merleau-Ponty argued that perception is never a passive intake of data. The perceiver is a body already in the world and already leaning toward it, and the world arrives already carrying meaning. “Because we are in the world,” he wrote, “we are condemned to meaning.” No one in Young’s novel escapes that sentence. Catherine’s centaurs, Mr. Spitzer’s shifting streets, and Miss MacIntosh’s straight line between two points are all meanings the world has been made to wear. Merleau-Ponty also named the unargued trust beneath ordinary seeing, the conviction that what appears is simply the world itself. He called it perceptual faith. Miss MacIntosh’s “framework in which all must live” is perceptual faith raised into a creed, and her late confession is what happens when that faith looks at itself.

Simone Weil comes at the same ground from another side. In her essay on the notion of reading, she argued that people never meet bare sensation. They read the world the way they read a page, and the reading grips them before any judgment can step in: a letter bringing news of a death strikes the body before the eye has finished taking in the ink. Elsewhere she described the imagination as a filler of the void, always at work closing the gaps through which something truer might enter. Her discipline of attention asks a person to hold those gaps open and to wait without filling them.

Weil is the most fitting philosophical companion for this novel because she takes illusion as seriously as Young does, as a veil laid over everything. The two part ways at the end, however. Weil hoped attention might lift the veil and show something real beyond it. Young’s novel, and the pillar this book follows, doubt there is a beyond to reach. What survives of Weil’s hope is her patience, the willingness to look without rushing to close the gap. That is what Vera learns, slowly, on the bus.

Shelter and Prison

It is tempting to sort the novel’s illusions into good ones and bad ones, the protective and the destructive. The novel refuses the sorting. Illusion shelters and illusion imprisons, usually both at once and through the same act. Catherine’s dream spares her the disappointment of the real and costs her the daughter who is real. The canary that Mr. Spitzer keeps replacing, followed in Chapter 5, keeps her alive and keeps her deceived. Miss MacIntosh’s wig lets her walk into the world with dignity and leaves her lonelier than anyone in the house. There is no clean way to take the shelter without the walls.

What the novel offers instead is a difference in how illusions are held. Catherine knows she pretends and cannot stop. Miss MacIntosh denies that she pretends until the denial fails, and then, straightening her wig, she tells the truth about it. Mr. Spitzer carries his map knowing it will not help. Moses Hunnecker drives on through the rain. None of them sees without a lens, and none of them could. The characters the novel treats most tenderly are the ones who come to know the lens is there.

This is how seeing the construction loosens its hold. It does not dissolve the illusion. Vera never reaches a world without masks, and the book never pretends she could. What changes is the grip. An illusion known as an illusion can be worn lightly, revised, set down for a while, or shared with someone who wears one too. An illusion taken for bedrock has to be defended at any cost. Young’s great realist and her great dreamer end up closer than either would have believed. Both make worlds, and both are bald beneath the crown. The difference that remains is candor, and in this novel candor is the nearest thing to clear sight anyone is given.

Reflection Questions

  1. Catherine argues that reality disappoints “just through its being realized,” because it shuts out every other possibility. Where does that argument ring true, and where does it break down?

  2. Miss MacIntosh finally separates the illusions “surrounding us” from those “which we ourselves have made.” Is that a real distinction? Which illusions in ordinary life would fall on each side?

  3. Mr. Spitzer’s searchlight “made the light it lived by.” Is there any way to know what the world is like before anyone looks at it?

  4. Moses Hunnecker claims to see better drunk than sober. What medicines do people use to make the world bearable to look at, and are some more honest than others?

  5. The unmasking leaves Vera with the knowledge that “each must wear a mask” rather than with the truth. Is that knowledge a loss, a gain, or both?

  6. If candor, rather than contact with bedrock, is what separates one way of seeing from another, what would it look like to hold one’s own framework honestly?
