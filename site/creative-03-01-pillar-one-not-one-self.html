Pillar: Pillar 1 Not One Self · Across every book

Or, Why Your Inner Crowd is an Anti-Fascist Superpower

We live in a culture that is absolutely obsessed with consistency. From the time we are kids, we are told to find our "true self," lock down our personal brands, and stay predictable.

Mainstream society treats a change of mind as a weakness, and holding contradictory feelings is often viewed as a moral flaw. We are under constant pressure to trim away our rough edges so we can fit neatly into a single, easily digestible box.

But Marguerite Young looks at all that pressure and tells us we are looking at identity completely backwards.

You Are a Verb, Not a Noun

Young's first big claim is wonderfully liberating: you are not a single, solid, unchanging object. You are a living, breathing crowd of different selves all navigating the world at the exact same time.

This inner multiplicity is not a problem to be solved or a mental health crisis to fix; it is the absolute, healthy condition of being alive. Think about it: the version of you that sits at a holiday dinner with your family is vastly different from the version of you that collaborates on a creative project, which is different again from the quiet voice inside your head when you are grieving a loss.

Traditional philosophy has spent centuries trying to protect the idea of a single, unified "I" ruling over our minds. But Young invites us to look at the actual experience of being human. As she beautifully demonstrates, the self is a verb, not a noun. It is an ongoing process of becoming a winding stream that flows in multiple directions at once.

Breaking the Sorting Machine

So, how does acknowledging your inner crowd protect you from tyranny?

The demand for a single, simple identity is not just an irritation: it is a highly effective political technology used to control populations.

Authoritarian movements and dictators thrive on a population that can be easily sorted. They need clean, rigid boundaries to build their power: us versus them, the loyal patriot versus the dangerous outsider, the productive worker versus the citizen who does not belong.

The more complex your inner world is, the harder you are for a regime to manage. When you accept your own internal multitudes, you actively jam their sorting machine.

When you are honest about your own complexity, you cannot be neatly filed away as just an enemy or just a loyalist. You become fundamentally un-sortable.

Because you recognize the diverse, shifting voices inside yourself, you are far less likely to fall for propaganda that tries to turn other human beings into flat, one-dimensional monsters. Your inner crowd becomes your ultimate shield against total control.

The Refusal: James Baldwin

This is not an abstract principle. The stakes of refusing to be sorted become clear when we look at what happens to people who actually do it.

The American writer James Baldwin spent his entire career holding contradictions that the dominant system wanted to reduce to a single label. He was a Black man writing in a literary tradition that had tried to erase Black presence. He was a gay man in an era when homosexuality was treated as a perversion to be hidden or fixed. He was an American writer living much of his life in exile in France. He was a literary artist who wrote essays of scorching political clarity. He was a man shaped by Christian faith and fierce atheism at the same moment.

The machinery of control wanted to file him away in a single box: "the gay writer," or "the Black writer," or "the protest novelist." Any single label would have made him manageable, his work categorizable, his threat containable.

But Baldwin refused. He wrote directly about this refusal, insisting that he would not reduce himself to fit the system's categories. In his essays and novels, he held all of these identities at once, refusing to apologize for the contradictions or to choose one over the others. His very refusal to be sorted made his work almost impossible to control. The system could not pin him down, could not use him as a symbol to settle a single debate, could not make him serve a single political narrative.

His inner crowd was not hidden or ashamed. It was on display, complex and irreducible, on every page. That complexity is what gave his work its devastating power. Because Baldwin would not simplify himself, his readers could not simplify the world he was describing to them. When you read Baldwin, you cannot sort people into clean categories. You cannot look away from the full humanity of those the system tries to reduce.

The regime does not love this. It cannot tolerate people who refuse to stay in a single box, because those people cannot be used as reliable tools. They become un-predictable, un-manageable, and contagious. Their refusal gives others permission to refuse as well.

The Practice

Try to pay close attention to the different "characters" that wake up inside you depending on where you are and who you are with. When a contradiction pops up (like feeling deeply lonely while surrounded by friends, or feeling incredibly brave and terrified at the exact same moment), do not try to fix it or force yourself to choose just one emotion.

Let the contradictions sit in the room. Releasing the demand to be perfectly consistent is the very first act of creative resistance.

Pay attention to when external systems or people try to flatten you into a single identity. Notice the pressure to choose: are you the caregiver or the person with your own needs? Are you the professional or the person with a messy inner life? Are you the victim of a loss or the person who is healing and moving forward? Notice that these forced choices are always a form of control.

The truth is that you are all of these things at once. Your refusal to pick one and discard the others is not a failure of clarity. It is a form of freedom.
