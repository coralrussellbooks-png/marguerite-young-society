Chapter 1. The House Is Full

Definition from research

In the twenty-fourth chapter of Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, the servants in Catherine Cartwheel’s seaside house sit arguing about where and when they are. “One would say that it was Roman in the morning but Greek in the afternoon or that the Duke of Wellington was surely expected.” Then the rooms are assigned. “Admiral Peary was to be put into the blue room which was already occupied by Plato’s boat, et cetera. Sir Christopher Wren and Jenny Lind were to be put into the rose. Spinoza and Shakespeare and Mrs. Sydington were already in the green.” The house, the narrator says, “was always crowded to the roof,” its bedrooms “filled with famous guests sleeping tier above tier according to rank and station but with little regard for century.”

This is one of the few places in a novel of more than a thousand pages where philosophers appear by name, and it is worth noticing what happens to them. Plato does not arrive with an argument. He arrives as a boat, taking up space in a room that must also hold an Arctic explorer. Spinoza shares the green room with Shakespeare and with a Mrs. Sydington whom no history remembers. The phrase “et cetera” does quiet, devastating work. The great systems of Western thought are filed alongside a soprano, an architect, and a duke, and the filing is done by servants who cannot agree on the century.

The same thing happens earlier. In the ninth chapter, Catherine lies in “an unearthly coma,” receiving “her distinguished circle which never diminished and which included Pythagoras who had a golden thigh and had discovered heaven’s harmony, the symphonious music of the silver-toned spheres.” Pythagoras is given his legend and his great idea in a single breath, and then the circle keeps expanding. “The P’s were crowding upon her,” the passage continues, “planets, Pythian games, polygons, Prussians, petunias, pseudopsias which are the delusions of sight.” The mathematician who heard the music of the spheres is one P among petunias, and right beside him stands a medical term for seeing what is not there. The placement is a verdict. The sentence does not say Pythagoras was wrong. It says he is one more guest in the house of illusion, and that the house has room for everyone.

In the fourteenth chapter, Catherine “would ordinarily not ask for the appearance beyond the appearance, mainly because she was aware of it and knew that the dog was Socrates.” The joke runs deep. Pythagoras taught that souls pass from body to body, and here the most famous questioner in philosophy has been reborn as a household pet. The phrase “the appearance beyond the appearance” names the move Plato made at the foundation of Western metaphysics, the claim that behind the shifting world of appearances stands a truer world of forms. Catherine does not refute this. She simply declines to ask, because she already knows what she would find. Behind every appearance there is only another appearance, and the dog is Socrates.

Even Copernicus appears, in the same chapter, as “the author of that great celestial revolution which had exiled the dear God to the void and had robbed us of the evidence of our senses.” In a single phrase, the scientific revolution becomes one more event in the long history of illusion. The senses were wrong about the sun, and so the senses cannot be trusted, and so the ground under every realist falls away.

When readers first meet these passages, it is natural to think Young is arguing with the philosophers, or gathering them up, or trying to illuminate what they meant. The scenes suggest something else. An argument requires two parties standing on the same ground. In Catherine’s house, no one stands on ground at all. Young is not in conversation with philosophy. She is housing it. She gives each system a room, lets it sleep beside the others, and waits to see what happens when the house becomes crowded to the roof.

The Second Door

If named philosophers were the only evidence, the claim would be thin. A dozen mentions in a book of this size could be decoration. But the named guests are only the first of two ways philosophy enters the novel. The second way is quieter and far more pervasive. Young takes a philosopher’s central image, removes the name, and gives the image to a character to live inside.

Friedrich Nietzsche is never named in the novel, but his images run through it. His most famous line warns that whoever gazes long into an abyss will find the abyss gazing back. In the twenty-fifth chapter, Mr. Spitzer, the careful old attorney, walks through the world never knowing “what the next step would bring, whether he would step on nothing, whether it would be a stairway or a dissolving stairway or a chasm filled with the songs of dead birds, whether he would stare at that wild, precipitous abyss which would stare at him.” Nietzsche’s abyss sat before the heroic thinker who dared to look. Young moves it underfoot. It becomes the floor of an ordinary walk to the office. Later Mr. Spitzer fears he has “stepped upon a pulsing cloud where he had thought there was no pavement” (ch. 45), and elsewhere “Every crack might suddenly be the abyss” (ch. 52). There is nothing to stare into from a safe height, because everyone has been walking on the abyss all along.

In Thus Spoke Zarathustra, the prophet climbs a mountain path with a dwarf, the spirit of gravity, riding on his shoulders, and at the gateway called Moment he first proposes the thought of eternal recurrence. In the forty-fifth chapter of Young’s novel, Cousin Hannah, the old suffragette and mountain climber, lies dying and raves that she is “climbing naked through mountains of snow.” Then she cries out, “Who is that dwarf, a turret on his head, a rose at his collar? It is the rose of consciousness.” The mountain, the climber, and the dwarf are all present. But the dwarf now wears a turret, and the turret belongs to the novel’s own house, which Vera later describes as “the dark house with its golden turrets and towers” (ch. 82). The spirit of gravity has put on the architecture of illusion as a hat.

Eternal recurrence itself appears openly. In the forty-second chapter, Mr. Spitzer, mourning his brother, asks: “Should not eternal recurrence be the one law of life, and should not the great lamp-lighter going his rounds through darkened heavens light that lamp which had gone out and was himself shining through clouds?” The doctrine that Nietzsche offered as the supreme test of a life is proposed here by a grieving man who wants his brother back. And the novel does not leave it there. Five chapters later the idea returns, softened, as “life forever repeating itself as the rain recalls the rain and the star recalls the star,” followed by a telling qualification: “This tendency of an image to repeat an image, perhaps with variations, was something to be considered, weighed, taken into account” (ch. 47). By the fifty-ninth chapter it has broken. Mr. Spitzer tries to compose music from the paths of billiard balls, only to discover that “no game could doubtless ever exactly repeat itself. The ivory balls would not roll twice the same.” (ch. 59)

Across seventeen chapters, a philosophical doctrine is proposed, qualified, and undone, and it is undone by nothing more than lived experience piling up. No one refutes eternal recurrence. It simply cannot survive the accumulation of one man’s actual days.

What Kind of Book This Is

These two doors, the named guests and the unnamed images, point to the argument of this book. Marguerite Young did not write a novel that comments on philosophy. She built a philosophical system and used fiction as its demonstration. Her method is to take a way of understanding the world, place it inside a human life, and then keep adding experience until the understanding gives way. She does this to religious faith, to romantic imagination, to heroic action, to hope, to common sense, and to the doctrines of the philosophers themselves. Every category fails under the weight of what keeps arriving.

A skeptical reader will object at once. If every system fails under accumulation, then Young’s system must fail too, and the phrase “complete philosophical system” contradicts itself. This book takes that objection as its center rather than its weakness. Young’s system is complete precisely because it includes its own undoing. It is not a set of doctrines to be believed. It is a way of seeing how every doctrine breaks, joined to a way of living after the break. Young said as much, late in her life, to the interviewers Ellen G. Friedman and Miriam Fuchs: “When you have examined all the illusions of life and know that there isn’t any reality, but you nevertheless go on, then you are a mature human being.”

The chapters that follow set out that system in four movements. The rest of Part One teaches how to read Young’s sentences as arguments and lays the biographical ground from which the novel grew. Part Two follows the trials, one lived system at a time. Part Three states the principles that remain once the trials are over. Part Four turns to consequences: politics, ethics, and the novel’s ending, which is the system’s final demonstration.

The novel itself supplies the book’s title. In the fifty-second chapter, Mr. Spitzer searches the forty trunks that Cousin Hannah brought back from forty voyages, trying rusted key after rusted key, and understands at last that “never would that lock open which should open upon all mysteries at once.” A few lines later the thought is completed: “There was no master key.” Every philosopher in Catherine’s crowded house arrived carrying a master key. Young’s achievement was to show, one life at a time, that none of them fits every lock, and then to ask what a person does with a ring of keys that only open some doors.
