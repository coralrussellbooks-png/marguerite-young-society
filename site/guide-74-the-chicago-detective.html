Chapter 74: The Chicago Detective

← Chapter 73 · Guide Home · Chapter 75 →

Content note: The Esther Longtree chapters (72 to 81) deal with stillbirth and pregnancy loss, rape, infanticide, racist violence including lynching, and cruelty to children. This chapter is one of the hardest in the novel: Esther describes being raped by the Chicago detective, and the chapter deals with racism.

Research notes: Chapter 74

Characters: Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · The Chicago Detective

Themes: Death and Mortality · Grief, Loss, and Mourning · Memory and the Past · Women, Gender, and Marriage

Symbols: Angels, Devils, and Myth · Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

Allusions: Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · Pregnancy, Abortion, and Midwifery

Chapter 73 told you that a Chicago detective was circling Esther, that she feared arrest, that the undertaker had complained she brought him no business because she hid the evidence of her stillborn children in streams and snow and tree stumps. The detective was a threat at the edges of the previous chapter’s frame.

Chapter 74 brings him fully into focus, and what comes into focus is one of the novel’s most disturbing configurations: the detective is also the rapist. The man investigating Esther is the man who assaulted her. The figure of authority pursuing her for the deaths of children is the same figure whose violence set the terrible cycle in motion. He comes “three times in three different years,” in three different disguises. He has not solved the mystery. He has not found a murderer. But he always finds Esther. This is a chapter about the machinery of judgment brought to bear on a woman who has done nothing wrong, operated by the very man who wronged her. Young does not make this easy to read. She is not trying to.

Esther also, in this chapter, reveals something she has not told anyone: she was adopted. And she hints at a real stillborn from when she was very young: a death that may have been the first, the one that started everything. Her childhood reproaches her. She doesn’t fully understand why.

What Actually Happens

Esther continues her account, her voice marked now by the triple repetitions that entered in Chapter 73: the epizeuxis that gives her speech its quality of grief pressing against language. She describes the detective’s repeated appearances: three times, three different years, three different disguises. He comes looking for evidence of murder. He catalogues his clues with the precision of the Golden Age detective novel: eyeglasses, footprints, a calling card, a bone button, a thread, “a single spot of brown blood.” He solves “all mysteries but his own, and” his own is the one that matters: that he is what he is hunting.

He raped her, she says, when he thought she was dead, or perhaps sleeping, or perhaps simply because she was there and he could. She describes it with the detachment of someone describing weather: a whirl, an aching, a dullness. It was over too soon. It left behind evidence: the symptoms, the signs of his presence in her body. Another pregnancy. Another absence.

He is, she notes, the kind of detective who only deals “with the deaths of little white children.” The deaths of little Black children are “not his business, there being so many and so unimportant,” in his accounting. Young states this without additional commentary. The accounting speaks for itself.

He gets confessions from murderers “by confessing his own imaginary crimes.” He is trapped in the system he enforces, both agent and victim of the machinery, unable to solve the mystery at the center of his own life.

Esther fears the trial she imagines waiting for her: the jury of women who will look at her enormous pregnant body and her history of hidden stillborn and reach conclusions she cannot counter. She is innocent. She has also hidden everything. These two facts coexist and cannot be reconciled by a jury that only sees the second one.

She is adopted, she says. She was not told this for a long time. She hints at something that happened when she was very young: a real death, a real hiding, a first stillborn or something worse that her memory approaches and then retreats from. Her childhood reproaches her. “Who was innocent? Who was guilty?” She is not sure. She does not explain further.

Despite all of this, Esther ends the chapter radiant. She has “done much lying but no laying,” she says; she has been misjudged, misread, accused of immorality she did not commit in the way they imagine. She is self-sufficient. She is luminous. “No one from the outside world” can annoy her or frighten her. She is more hopeful now than she has ever been. Time’s passage has helped her, not hindered her. The little shadow boxer is still there.

The Detective as Both Perpetrator and Pursuer

The Chicago detective is this chapter’s central paradox, and Young constructs it without flinching. He is the man who assaulted Esther, who came to her when she appeared dead or sleeping, who left his evidence in her body, who produced at least one of the stillborn children she has been hiding. He is also the man sent to investigate the disappearance of those children. He is the crime and the investigation of the crime simultaneously. This is not a mystery novel’s twist. It is a structural observation about power. The person with authority to investigate, to accuse, to bring to trial is also the person who committed the original violation. His investigation of Esther is therefore not a search for truth but a performance of authority over a woman he has already violated: a continuation of the original act by institutional means. He catalogues clues. He solves mysteries. He does not solve his own.

Young gives him the full toolkit of Golden Age detective fiction: eyeglasses, footprints, a calling card, a bone button, a single thread, a spot of blood. The clues are meticulous and they lead nowhere, or rather, they lead him back to Esther every time, which is where he was going anyway. The investigation is circular. The mystery is not designed to be solved. It is designed to maintain his presence in her life.

The Racial Accounting

One of the chapter’s most precise and most chilling moments is the description of the detective’s professional scope: he only deals “with the deaths of little white children.” The deaths of little Black children are not his business. There are too many of them. They are not important enough.

Young states this in Esther’s voice, flatly, without irony, because Esther has no irony to spare for it. It is simply true. It is the accounting of a system that has decided, by the allocation of investigative resources, which children’s lives matter and which do not. The detective is not a corrupt aberration from this system. He is the system operating correctly, according to its own values. This passage sits alongside the woman in curlers from Chapter 71, her insistence that there are no Black people in the county and no one wants any, as Young’s documentation of the specific texture of American racism in the mid-twentieth century Midwest. It is not ideological in the abstract. It is institutional, practical, embedded in the professional scope of the man sent to protect children. Some children. The ones whose deaths count.

Esther’s Adoption and the First Death

The revelation that Esther is adopted arrives without ceremony in the middle of the chapter and without full explanation. She was not told for a long time. She hints at something that happened when she was very young: a first stillborn, or perhaps something else, a death in the circumstances of her earliest girlhood that her memory circles without landing. Her own childhood reproaches her. She asks: “Who was innocent? Who was guilty?”

Young does not answer. The ambiguity is deliberate. What Esther may be carrying (in addition to everything else, in addition to the little shadow boxer) is guilt about something that happened before she was fully old enough to understand it. A death that may not have been her fault, that she may have been too young to prevent, that is now entangled in her mind with all the subsequent losses. She cannot separate them. Her childhood is reproaching her for something she cannot fully name.

Her adoption is also relevant to her situation in another way: she does not know her biological history, her ancestry, the medical inheritance of her body. The “everlasting pregnancy” may have roots she cannot trace because she does not know where she came from. The mystery of her condition is also the mystery of her origin.

The Shroud with Many Pockets

One of the chapter’s central images is a shroud, the garment of the dead, with many pockets. A shroud with pockets has nowhere to go. The dead do not need pockets; they have no belongings to carry, no errands to run. A shroud with many pockets is a garment of death that has been prepared for living: the carrying of things forward, the retention of evidence, the keeping of secrets. Esther wears this shroud figuratively. She is a woman whose body keeps carrying things that have died. The pockets hold the fragments: an eye, an ear, a fingernail, the evidence of the detective’s presence, the first death of her girlhood. She cannot empty the pockets. The shroud does not come off.

Esther’s Radiance

After everything in this chapter (the detective, the assault, the adoption, the first death, the fear of trial, the accusation of murder for children she did not kill) Esther ends radiant. Suffused with radiance, the chapter says. Self-sufficient. “No one from the outside world” can annoy her or frighten her.

This is not denial. Esther knows everything that has happened. Her clarity is one of the chapter’s recurring emphases: she is luminous inside, whatever her body and the world have done to her. She has been misread as mulish, as crazy, as immoral, as a murderer. She has “done much lying but no laying.” She is more hopeful now than she has ever been.

Time has helped her. This is one of the stranger things Esther says, because time has brought her assault, her hidden stillborn, the detective’s repeated visits, the undertaker’s complaint, the imagined jury of women. But time has also brought her further from the first death, further from the girlhood that reproaches her, closer to the conviction that the little shadow boxer will make it. The passage of time has, for Esther, accumulated more hope than despair. This is not logical. It is true.

What to Hold Onto

The detective who is both the crime and the investigation. The man with the eyeglasses and the calling card and the bone button, who solves all mysteries but his own, who comes back “three times in three different years” and always finds Esther.

The racial accounting: only “the deaths of little white children.” Stated flatly. The system, operating correctly.

Esther adopted, her childhood reproaching her for something she cannot name. A first death before all the others. “Who was innocent? Who was guilty?” She does not know.

The shroud with many pockets. A garment of death prepared for living, for carrying things forward. And Esther radiant, at the end. Suffused with light. More hopeful now than ever. Time’s passage has helped her. The little shadow boxer is still there, still fighting, still two hearts.
