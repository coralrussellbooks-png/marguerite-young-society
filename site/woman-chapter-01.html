Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 1

← The Route Down · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 2 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

The novel puts its thesis on its first pages. On the bus, in the mist, a double-headed man stares into nothing, and the narration turns to the line the whole book will spend eighty-two chapters proving: "every heart is the other heart," "every soul is the other soul." This is the illusion of the individual, stated plainly at the outset, and it is the key to everything that follows. It is larger than a challenge to a male-centered idea of the self. It is the book announcing that separate selfhood is the one illusion it means to dissolve.

The world is going out of focus: "the ghost of the world," "the face of the ambiguous waters," the fading light and mist. This is not a figure for women's uncertain social place. It is the first appearance of real and dream as one country, the book's refusal of any firm line between the seen and the dreamed. The double-headed man is the first body that is already more than one, plural selfhood made literal before the reader has met anyone.

Against this dissolving world stand two poles. Vera's search for Miss MacIntosh, already underway and already without end, is the unfinished quest that will structure the book, and its grief has no bottom. Miss MacIntosh herself is introduced as the one who holds still, a "simple woman with a broken nose" who keeps "truth to nature" and refuses illusion. She is a model of a grounded woman, but the reason the book plants her here, as the least dissolving character it has, is so that her own coming-apart later will carry the most weight. Madge, the pregnant girl painted and adorned over an alienated interior, is the first sketch of the self as a mask or a series of masks or even a series of multiple masks, and of a pregnancy that is already dread rather than promise.
