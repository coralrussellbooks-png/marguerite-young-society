Chapter 5. Mr. Spitzer, Peron, and the Divided Self

Definition from research

The Promise

Mr. Joachim Spitzer, the old attorney who keeps Catherine company, has built a system that looks like the opposite of hers. His is caution. He is “a man so punctual, so unperceptive as himself acting always according to his routines” (ch. 25). He “claimed never to have placed a bet on even that which he had been most certain of” (ch. 2). Where Catherine trusts nothing and so can imagine anything, Mr. Spitzer trusts nothing and so risks nothing. His system promises safety through restraint.

His twin brother Peron embodied the opposite system. Peron was “insolent, a gambler, a spender of borrowed money, a quick suicide” (ch. 2). He bet on everything and lost. Between them, the brothers divide the world into two clean categories: the careful life and the reckless one. It is the old philosophical contest between prudence and passion, reason and appetite, played out by twins.

The Loading

The division does not hold. Cousin Hannah, who disliked them both, always insisted that “they were really identical, not nearly so different as they had supposed” (ch. 30). After Peron’s death, Mr. Spitzer cannot decide what to carve on the tombstone, “never deciding which brother it was, and perhaps he never would decide” (ch. 31). The categories of the careful brother and the reckless brother begin to blur, and the novel keeps adding to the blur for chapter after chapter.

Peron left behind a watch, and Mr. Spitzer wears it. It marks “the recurring years, the unknown flow of time, the backward flow, seasons, days, hours, half-minutes, quarterminutes, seconds as of a hair’s breadth, every conceivable departmentalization” (ch. 27). The instrument of perfect measurement belongs to the man who lived without measure, and it is worn by the man who measured everything. Time itself, divided into the finest categories, runs backward on his wrist.

Mr. Spitzer tries one philosophical system after another in his grief. He proposes eternal recurrence (ch. 42), as Chapter 1 showed, and watches it dissolve into billiard balls that “would not roll twice the same” (ch. 59). He walks the city unsure “whether he would step on nothing” (ch. 25). He comes to feel that “there was no reality, and there was nothingness” (ch. 54). And in the same chapter he reaches a sentence that could stand as a summary of the whole novel’s method: “Distortions seemed to cause distortions, perhaps because one principle had not been correct.”

The Break

The break is the collapse of personal identity itself. In the forty-seventh chapter, he wonders whether he really knows “which brother he was, for had he not lived on both sides, died on both sides.” By the forty-ninth, the novel states it outright: “he did not know which brother he was, and this was the crux of his mystery.” He asks, “Was it Mr. Spitzer who was dead, perhaps living only by that dream which moved and stirred through a dead man’s thoughts?”

Philosophers have argued for centuries about what makes a person the same person over time. Locke pointed to memory. Others pointed to the body, or the soul. Mr. Spitzer tests every candidate and finds that none of them settles the question. His memories are shared with his brother. His body is his brother’s twin. His soul, if he has one, cannot say whose it is.

And then comes the most radical sentence of all. The answer to his question, the narrator says, “would have signified little or nothing, not altering his experience in the least degree or doing so in ways which were unrecognizable” (ch. 49). This is where the category of personal identity truly fails. It is not that the question has no answer. It is that the answer would change nothing about how he lives. The category has become weightless.

What Is Left

What survives in Mr. Spitzer is a stance toward others, and it may be the most humane thing in the book. “Whose illusions could he challenge? He had learned to respect other people’s mirages, especially as he existed only as a mirage himself” (ch. 51). A man who does not know which brother he is cannot sneer at anyone else’s confusion. His own uncertainty becomes the ground of his tolerance.

He is also the one who searches Cousin Hannah’s forty trunks, trying rusted key after rusted key. He could have broken them open, “but he was not violent,” and he proceeded “carefully as one whose own violences were suppressed” (ch. 52). He had “a due respect for damaged properties which should not be further damaged by him.” When he concludes that “There was no master key,” he does not force the locks. He leaves the mysteries their dignity.

His music, which no one ever hears, carries the same lesson. An old piano mender tells him “that there had always been a missing keyboard. There had always been a string missing in the harp of God,” and Mr. Spitzer wonders if that missing string is his own (ch. 51). The divided self is not a flaw in an otherwise complete creation. It is part of a creation that was never complete.

In the last chapter, Vera reports that Mr. Spitzer finally knew, “for what might seem only a shining moment in the long darkness of time or timelessness, which brother he truly was.” The answer is “the dead.” Even this resolution is framed as a moment, not a conclusion, and it arrives only at the point where identity no longer matters to anyone but the one who dies.
