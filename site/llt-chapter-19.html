Chapter 19

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh

Pillars: Reality Is Constructed · The Past Doesn't Go Away · The Sacred Is Here

The Questions

The inventory of Miss MacIntosh’s things, wig, false teeth, corset, borrowed hats, lays out the parts of her plainness. If the plain self was assembled from props, what was it?

A “Christian woman with an honest mind” keeps a gambling deck with the “deuce of clubs,” the omen of death, turned up. What holds those “double motions” in one person?

The room is all crumbling cherubim and broken compasses. What is the decay doing in the chapter?

Are the worn, patched, useful things Miss MacIntosh’s virtue, or the stage-dressing of a built plainness?

The chapter itemizes the construction. Where the unsealing lifted the surface and the masks were named, this one lays out the parts: the wig, the false teeth, the corset, the mismatched borrowed clothing, the baseball cap and whistle and oilskin hat, the black umbrella carried indoors “like a shield.” The plain self was assembled. Every prop that made Miss MacIntosh look like unadorned bedrock is here on the table, and seen together they show that the plainness was Reality Is Constructed all along, a built surface as carefully kept as any of Catherine’s dreams, only built to look like no construction at all.

And the plurality is in the objects too. A “Christian woman with an honest mind” who calls gambling “the crafty Devil’s handiwork” keeps a deck of cards with the “deuce of clubs,” the omen of death, turned up. The chapter names these “dualities in one heart, these double motions,” and refuses to reconcile them. She is not a hypocrite to be exposed; she is a crowd, like everyone in the book, holding the honest woman and the gambler, the believer and the fatalist, in one unsorted self.

The room is The Past Doesn't Go Away made into a place. Crumbling cherubim, broken acanthus leaves, a broken ship’s compass, a torn almanac, cracked walls, everything decaying, everything the sediment of a past that will not stay behind. Miss MacIntosh tries to hold it off, cheese-cloth draped over the crumbling heads, the Bible chained to the table, small orders imposed on a general ruin. The imposing does not work, and the failure is the point: the past keeps rising, the decay keeps winning, and no chained Bible stops it.

The worn, patched, useful things are the chapter’s finest ambiguity. Saved for a “rainy day,” mended and re-mended, they are genuinely The Sacred Is Here, the resourceful tending of a plain life. And they are also the stage-dressing of the built plainness. The chapter will not let the reader choose. The umbrella is a real umbrella and a shield and a prop, all at once, and Miss MacIntosh is a real plain woman and a construction of plainness, both held open.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that turns the inventory into an exposure, Miss MacIntosh unmasked as a fraud in wig and false teeth, and the reading that takes her worn useful things as pure unbuilt virtue. Neither the caught-out hypocrite nor the saint survives the chapter.

The footing is that the plainness was assembled, a construction built to look like none, which is Reality Is Constructed at its most disguised; the contradictions in her are the plural self, not hypocrisy; and the decaying room is the past refusing to stay behind, unstoppable by any small imposed order. The worn objects are ordinary virtue and stage-dressing at once, and the chapter holds both. Even the plainest self is assembled, and the plurality and the decay are real. The chapter itemizes without concluding, and leaves Miss MacIntosh both plain woman and built plainness, unreconciled.

For Reflection

If one laid out on a table every prop that makes one look like oneself, what would be left over as the self?

What “double motions” does one hold that a closer look would refuse to reconcile?

Which of one’s plain, useful, virtuous things are also, honestly, stage-dressing?
