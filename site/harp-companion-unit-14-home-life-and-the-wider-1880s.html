Unit 14, pp. 305-352: Home life and the wider 1880s

Part of a reading of Harp Song for a Radical (1999), Marguerite Young’s life of Eugene Victor Debs.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Unit 13 · Harp Song for a Radical: A History Companion · Unit 15 →

Background for this unit: The History in Harp Song for a Radical

Debs marries Kate Metzel in 1885 and honeymoons in the East as the Statue of Liberty arrives in New York harbor. Around the marriage Young sets the decade's scandals and struggles: a kidnapped child, a preacher's adultery trial, a president's assassin, and an Irish word, "boycott," crossing the Atlantic. The unit ends with the Burlington strike of 1888, which taught Debs that craft unions fighting alone would lose.

Major entries

Debs's marriage and household (pp. 311-321, 333-350)

  • What happened: On June 9, 1885, Debs married Katherine Metzel at St. Stephen's Episcopal Church in Terre Haute (p. 315). She came from a German family of Louisville, and her stepfather, John Baur, kept a Terre Haute drugstore. They had no children. In 1890 they built the house on North Eighth Street (p. 340) that is now the Debs Home museum.

  • The family circle: His sister Emilie married the electrical engineer C. Odilon Mailloux in 1883 (p. 311). His sister Marie married the florist John G. Heinl, who designed the floral locomotive for a brotherhood celebration (p. 310). His brother Theodore became his lifelong secretary and partner in the work (pp. 318-320).

  • How Young uses it: Kate has been cast by many biographers as cold to Debs's causes. Young treats the marriage with more sympathy, through their letters signed with railroad pen names such as "Signal" and "Headlight" (p. 336).

  • Further reading: Salvatore, Eugene V. Debs (1982), chapter 3.

The Statue of Liberty and Pulitzer's campaign (pp. 323-326)

  • What it was: France's gift, designed by Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi with an iron frame by Gustave Eiffel. It arrived in pieces off Sandy Hook on June 17, 1885, and was met by General Charles P. Stone, the engineer in charge of its assembly (p. 326).

  • The pedestal: Congress and wealthy New Yorkers failed to fund the base. In 1885 Joseph Pulitzer's New York World printed the names of every donor, and more than 120,000 people, most of them giving under a dollar, raised over \$100,000 (p. 325). Emma Lazarus's poem "The New Colossus" (1883), with its line "Give me your tired, your poor," was written to raise money for the pedestal.

  • The dedication: President Grover Cleveland dedicated the statue on October 28, 1886. Chauncey Depew, the New York Central Railroad's lawyer, gave the main oration (p. 326). It came less than six months after the Haymarket bombing, when immigrants were being blamed for radicalism.

  • Further reading: Edward Berenson, The Statue of Liberty: A Transatlantic Story (2012).

The Burlington strike of 1888 (p. 349)

  • What happened: In February 1888 the engineers and firemen of the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad struck for better pay and an end to wage classifications. The railroad hired replacements, some from the Knights of Labor, which had its own grudges against the brotherhoods, and brought in Pinkerton guards. The strike was lost by the end of the year.

  • Why it matters: Debs saw the lesson plainly: rail workers divided by craft could each be beaten one by one. In his January 1888 essay "The Vitality of Labor Organizations" (p. 349) and after, he pressed for federation. That path led to the Supreme Council of the United Orders of Railway Employees (1889) and then to the American Railway Union (1893).

  • Further reading: Shelton Stromquist, A Generation of Boomers: The Pattern of Railroad Labor Conflict in Nineteenth-Century America (1987).

The boycott and the Irish Land War (pp. 351-352)

  • What it was: In 1880 tenants in County Mayo, organized by the Irish National Land League, refused all dealings with Captain Charles Boycott, a land agent who evicted them. His name became the word for collective shunning. Young quotes Debs's account of it (p. 352).

  • Why it matters: American unions, especially the Knights of Labor, adopted the boycott widely in the 1880s. Courts soon ruled many boycotts illegal conspiracies, a key weapon in the later use of injunctions against labor, including against Debs in 1894.

  • Where the record differs: Young quotes Debs calling Boycott "an Englishman, owner of landed estates" (p. 352). He was the agent for an absentee landlord, Lord Erne, not the owner.

  • Parnell: Charles Stewart Parnell, the Irish nationalist leader, was ruined in 1890 when he was named in the divorce of Katharine ("Kitty") O'Shea. Debs wrote on it in the magazine in January 1891 (p. 351).

  • Further reading: Joyce Marlow, Captain Boycott and the Irish (1973).

David M. Parry and the manufacturers' crusade (pp. 306-307)

  • Who he was: An Indianapolis carriage and buggy manufacturer (1852-1915) who became president of the National Association of Manufacturers from 1902 to 1906. He led its open-shop campaign against unions and wrote The Scarlet Empire (1906), a novel imagining socialism as a tyranny beneath the sea.

  • Where the record differs: Young calls him "George A. Parry" and his group the "American Association of Manufacturers" (p. 306). She uses the phrase "the Scarlet Empire" for the poor districts of Indianapolis (p. 307), a sly borrowing from his book.

Shorter entries

  • Charley Ross (p. 306): A four-year-old kidnapped from Germantown, Philadelphia, on July 1, 1874. The first American kidnapping for ransom to become a national sensation, it was never solved.

  • Charles H. Black (p. 305): An Indianapolis carriage maker credited locally with one of the first gasoline automobiles, around 1891.

  • The Beecher-Tilton scandal (pp. 312-313): Theodore Tilton sued Henry Ward Beecher in 1874 for adultery with his wife, Elizabeth. The 1875 trial ended in a hung jury. Victoria Woodhull had first published the charge.

  • The Franco-Prussian War (p. 310): Prussia and its German allies defeated France in 1870-1871 and annexed Alsace, including the Debs family's Colmar. Daniel Debs sided passionately with France.

  • The Brooklyn Bridge (p. 323): Opened in 1883. The editor Frank Harris claimed to have worked in its caissons as a young man. As editor of Pearson's Magazine he later published Debs's writing.

  • Garfield and Guiteau (pp. 330): Garfield was shot on July 2, 1881, and died on September 19 at Elberon, New Jersey. Guiteau had lived at the Oneida Community in the 1860s. Horatio Alger rushed out a Garfield biography, From Canal Boy to President (1881).

  • Maud Gonne and William Butler Yeats (p. 352): Gonne, an Irish nationalist, campaigned against evictions. Yeats loved her for decades without success.

  • Colonel J. B. Maynard (p. 350): A Civil War veteran and friend of the brotherhoods.

  • Joel Chandler Harris (p. 342): The Atlanta author of the Uncle Remus tales (1880). His home was called the Wren's Nest.

  • Riley's poems (pp. 346-348): "The Old-Fashioned Roses" and "An Old Sweetheart of Mine" were among the favorites Young mentions. Admirers called Riley "the Hoosier Theocritus."

  • Popular songs (p. 334): "Katie Lee and Willie Grey" and "The Old Oaken Bucket" (1817), which Young says an elocutionist recited for the newlyweds.

Allusions

  • Venus de Milo (pp. 324, 327): The ancient statue in the Louvre, set beside Lady Liberty.

  • Esmeralda, Cosette, The Man Who Laughs (p. 324): Characters and a novel of Victor Hugo.

  • Camelot and Elaine (p. 343): Tennyson's Arthurian idylls.

  • Rodin's The Thinker (p. 349): The sculpture of 1880-1904.

  • The Bridge of Sighs (p. 351): The Venetian bridge prisoners crossed to their cells.

  • Eros and Dan Cupid (pp. 311, 339): The gods of love.

  • Hawthorne's The House of the Seven Gables (p. 342): The 1851 novel of an inherited curse.
