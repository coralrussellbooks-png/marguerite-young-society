Chapter 82: The End Is the Beginning

Research notes: Chapter 82

Themes: Death and Mortality · Illusion and Reality · Music, Sound, and Silence

Symbols: Clouds and Storms · Music and Instruments · Silence and Deafness · The Bus and the Journey · The House

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Angels and Archangels · Deafness and Sign Language · Homer's Epics · Iowa, Indiana, and the Midwest · The Bible · The History of Opium

This chapter carries Young’s use of Alpha and Omega, which To All My Darlings traces through the novel, to its simplest form: the end is the beginning, and the beginning is the end. After eighty-one chapters, the grey bus in the Midwest mist, Moses Hunnecker drinking and praying at the wheel, Madge sleeping her resistant sleep, Vera looking out at the grey American landscape wondering what she will find at What Cheer, Iowa; after Catherine and her opium and her pearls and her Italian thief; after Mr. Spitzer and his twin; after the Tavern and Dr. O’Leary and the hangman and Mrs. Hogden; after ten chapters with Esther Longtree and her stillborn and her Walter and her shadow boxer, the novel returns to where it began. The bus has arrived. The journey is over, or a version of it is over. Vera is home, or somewhere that used to be home. And everything she went looking for has changed into something she did not expect.

Catherine is dead. The sea-blackened house with its gold towers and cornices is gone: destroyed by a storm, scattered along the shore, its chandeliers lying broken in the sand. The only thing Vera has left from her inheritance is the string of pearls around her neck: the impossible arithmetic of her mother’s lost estate, the thing that multiplies when it is lost.

And she has fallen in love with a stone deaf man.

What Actually Happens

Vera mourns three deaths: Catherine’s, the old clam-digger’s, and one that may be harder to name, perhaps the death of the self she was looking for, the clarity she hoped Miss MacIntosh would restore. She is in love with “an older love than memory provided.” Catherine died while Mr. Spitzer carried her in his arms “into the garden by the sea.” He had waited years to tell her he loved her. He told her. She died as he spoke. The timing is the novel’s most bitter joke and most tender observation simultaneously: love arrives, is declared, and the beloved is already leaving. It was too late and it was the only moment available and it was enough and it was not enough. Mr. Spitzer then reveals what has been hidden since the beginning: he is not Joachim. He is Peron: the twin who survived by taking his dead brother’s place, who has been living Joachim’s life all this time, who has been Mr. Spitzer when he was always really Jock. His life, he says, has been a celestial joke. His grief over the old clam-digger, the loyal valet who drowned in the storm that destroyed Catherine’s house, is greater than his grief over Catherine. The man he loses most is not the woman he loved but the companion who was simply there, every day, without drama, until suddenly he was not.

Vera’s understanding of Miss MacIntosh deepens here, at the end. She loved Miss MacIntosh late, only when she could see past the surface of her character, past the rubber mackintosh and the whaler’s hat and the two and two making four. Underneath the practicality, the clearness, the plain average steadiness: a phantasmagoric dream. Miss MacIntosh dismissed religious belief as mockery and yet sang hymns. She believed in the cold surface of things and yet was drawn to the ocean’s depths and drowned in them. She was real, and she was not real in the way Vera thought, and Vera loved her most when she understood that the two things were simultaneously true. And then: the stone deaf man. He comes into Vera’s life in this final chapter, or she finds him, or hears his voice calling out for Lucie Bell, the silent girl who was not his love because she could not answer him. He hears with his eyes and his fingers. He lives in a world of silence that is not empty but full: full of the sounds the mind makes when the ears stop, of music remembered and music imagined. He says life is imaginary. This is an imaginary world. He cannot hear the silent horn that Gabriel will blow.

Vera loves him. They will marry. They will invite everyone. Their children, he believes, will transcend his limits: will hear what he cannot, carry forward what he has preserved in silence.

Vera says: “Esther Longtree would be me.”

Catherine’s Death and the House in the Storm

Catherine, who has been alive in the novel only in the mode of someone who has been dying for decades, who took to her bed and never left it, who lived entirely inside her opium hallucinations and her accumulated dreamwork: finally dies. The sea-blackened house goes with her. Storm, tide, dissolution: the material form of Catherine’s world is scattered along the shore.

What remains: the pearls. Young has been preparing this since Chapter 71, when Vera put on the pearls for the Tavern. Catherine believed they “could not be lost or stolen.” She believed that losing them made them multiply. The pearls are the only inheritance that survived. They are impossible and they are real. Catherine’s death, when it comes, is described as a change of state rather than a cessation. She had not walked for many years. She had not been entirely in the world for longer. Vera had been seeing her mother as a “beautiful lady sleeping in her swan boat”; by the end, she sees her as poor, as if “evicted from her house long before she died and wandered like a beggar through the streets.” The transformation is not in Catherine but in Vera’s seeing: she has learned, through eighty-two chapters of circling, to look at what is actually there rather than what she needs to see.

Mr. Spitzer Is Peron

This revelation has been structurally prepared throughout the novel: the twin brothers, one dead, one surviving by taking the dead one’s name and place; the question of which twin did the killing and which was killed; the guilt of the survivor who has been living as his brother, but it still lands with weight when it arrives. Mr. Spitzer has been Peron all along. He is Jock. He has been the wrong twin in the right place, or the right twin in the wrong name, for the entire novel.

The parallels to Esther Longtree’s situation are precise: she too has been the twin who survived, accused of killing Rosemary before birth. She too has carried that guilt as the organizing principle of her identity. Mr. Spitzer’s celestial joke (living as Joachim, who he is not) is Esther’s joke inverted: she lived as Esther, who she was told she had no right to be.

His grief for the old clam-digger exceeds his grief for Catherine. This is one of Young’s most precisely observed truths about loss: the death that destroys you is not always the dramatic one, the romantic one, the one with a name. Sometimes it is the companion who was simply present (the valet who buried himself in the sand, who developed nervous tics, who became over time barely distinguishable from the landscape) and who drowned quietly in the storm while Mr. Spitzer was declaring his love for someone else.

Miss MacIntosh, Finally Seen

Vera’s retrospective understanding of Miss MacIntosh in this final chapter is the novel’s most important statement about its own central figure. She loved Miss MacIntosh late. She could not love her fully while Miss MacIntosh was being Miss MacIntosh: plain, practical, real, dismissing illusions, insisting on two and two making four. It was only when Vera could see past that surface, when she understood that the woman who dismissed faith still sang hymns, that the woman who believed in the cold honest surface of things was drawn to the ocean’s depths: that she could love her fully.

Miss MacIntosh’s hymns in this chapter are the novel’s final gift from her. She sang them while performing mundane tasks, dismissing the religious belief they embodied, and singing them anyway. They are hymns of being lost, of needing guidance, of the waters drowning the soul: “Lo, she would lift her eyes to the hills from whence cometh help.” “Thou didst turn Thy face from me, and I was troubled.” “The waters have drowned us. The stream has gone over our soul.” These are not the hymns of someone who has certainty. They are the hymns of someone who has been through the water and survived and is still not sure if survival was the right outcome. Miss MacIntosh drowned in the ocean. She was drawn to the depths. She had hidden beneath her wig and her rubber mackintosh and her plain-average-clear-eyed practicality an entire phantasmagoric inner life, and the ocean claimed it. Vera could not have known this while she was looking for her. She knows it now that she has stopped looking and started mourning. The search ends not in What Cheer, Iowa but here, in the understanding that arrives too late and is therefore the only understanding available.

The Stone Deaf Man

He arrives at the novel’s end as its strangest and most necessary figure. He is stone deaf: deaf since he was fourteen, when all the waters of creation smashed against his ears. He hears with his eyes and his fingers. He lives in the silence that is not empty but full of the sounds the mind generates in the absence of the actual: music imagined, voices remembered, the world rebuilt from what was known before it went quiet. He cannot hear Lucie Bell: the silent girl he loved, who loved a jukebox or a tired marathon skater instead of him, who did not stay. He calls for her in his voice, this man who cannot hear his own voice. Vera hears him. Vera falls in love with him. She loves him not despite the deafness but through it, through the paradox of a man who speaks in a world of silence, who communicates in a language that is not language, who is closer to death than the hearing because he cannot hear the horn Gabriel will blow at the end of the world.

Their love is the novel’s final image of connection: not the connection Vera was looking for, not Miss MacIntosh’s plain-and-clear-eyed steadiness, not the cold honest surface of things. Something quieter and stranger. Something that requires a different kind of listening than the kind she arrived on the bus with.

They will marry. They will invite everyone. His deafness will not be passed to their children; he believes this, anyway, and Young lets him believe it. The next generation will hear what he cannot. Life goes on through the accumulation of what the living and the dead pass forward, imperfectly, into futures they cannot quite hear.

“Esther Longtree Would Be Me”

This is the novel’s most important sentence in the final chapter, and Young places it without fanfare. After all of Vera’s searching for Miss MacIntosh, for the plain average woman who believed in facts and schedules and the cold honest surface of things, Vera arrives at the end of the novel and says: Esther Longtree would be me. Not Miss MacIntosh. Not Catherine. Esther.

This is the full pivot of eighty-two chapters. Vera began her journey looking for the woman who represented clarity, practicality, the opposite of her mother’s opium dreaming. She ends by identifying herself with the woman who is always pregnant with the impossible, who loves “all things hurt or silent or broken or dead or living,” who buried her children in rivers and tree stumps and kept setting out breadcrumbs for spirits, who was called Little Jackass and was a murderer before she was born and survived everything that came after with a stubbornness that was neither wisdom nor delusion but simply life insisting on itself.

Esther is the average: the plain, the hidden, the one the world overlooks. And she is also the phantasmagoric dream beneath the surface. She is both, simultaneously, in the way that Miss MacIntosh was both: the cold surface and the depths that claimed her. What Vera has learned, across the entire novel, is that the surface and the depths are not opposites. They are the same thing seen from different angles, and to love the surface is to love the depths, and to look for the plain honest truth of things is to find that it contains a dream, and the dream contains a truth, and Esther Longtree is all of it.

The End Is the Beginning

The novel does not resolve. Miss MacIntosh does not walk back out of the ocean. Walter does not come home to Esther. Catherine’s house does not un-scatter from the shore. Mr. Spitzer does not stop being Peron-pretending-to-be-Joachim. The questions that opened the novel (what is real, what is illusion, what do we do when the only person who told us the truth has disappeared) are not answered. They are, by the end, better questions. Vera has learned to hold them without expecting resolution.

The stone deaf man says life is imaginary. This is an imaginary world. Miss MacIntosh would have disagreed: two and two make four, and don’t let anyone tell you otherwise. And yet she sang hymns. And yet she drowned. The facts and the imagination are not opposites. The cold surface and the depths that claim you are the same water.

The bus is still moving, in some sense, or has arrived at a place that is not the destination Vera expected but is nevertheless where she is. The grey American mist is still there. The world may blow up on a “day of calm just for fun.” The bad men may finish what they started. The little shadow boxer is still fighting. The stone deaf man is listening with his eyes and his fingers. “The dead loved the dead, and” perhaps that is how the world goes on.

Vera came looking for Miss MacIntosh. She found Esther Longtree.

She found herself.

What to Hold Onto

Catherine dying as Mr. Spitzer finally speaks his love. Too late and the only moment. It was not enough and it was what there was.

Mr. Spitzer revealing he is Peron. His life a celestial joke. His greatest grief: the old clam-digger who drowned in the storm, not the woman he loved.

Miss MacIntosh’s hymns: “Lo, she would lift her eyes to the hills.” The waters have drowned us. She dismissed faith and sang it anyway.

The stone deaf man who calls for Lucie Bell in the silence. Who hears with his eyes and his fingers. Who cannot hear the horn Gabriel will blow.

The pearls, scattered along the shore with the chandeliers. Only the pearls remain. Lost and multiplied, as Catherine promised. And Vera’s final sentence: Esther Longtree would be me. The woman who came looking for plainness and clarity and found the woman who is always pregnant with dreams. The woman who learned to love the depths the surface is made of.

A Note on the Journey

You have read Miss MacIntosh, My Darling. Or you have read alongside it, chapter by chapter, with this guide as your companion through the grey American mist.

What you have been inside is one of the longest and most singular novels in the American tradition: 1,198 pages, eighteen years in the writing, published in 1965 to stunned reviews and a readership that did not quite know what to do with it. It is not a novel that offers easy gratification or clear resolution. It asks you to surrender to its rhythms, to accept that meaning accumulates rather than arrives, to trust that the circling and digressing and doubling-back are the form enacting the content.

You have met Vera on her grey bus, and you have stayed with her through the Tavern and Esther Longtree and Mr. Weed and Dr. O’Leary and Mrs. Hogden and all the rest, and you have arrived here at the end, which is the beginning. Miss MacIntosh drowned in the ocean. Esther Longtree is always pregnant with the little shadow boxer. The stone deaf man cannot hear the horn Gabriel will blow. The pearls have multiplied.

Marguerite Young spent eighteen years building this wheel. You have traveled its spokes. That is not a small thing.
