Chapter 63

On Chapter 63 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of Christianity and Religion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated November 24, 2026

← Previous · Religion in MMMD Home · Next →

See also: Chapter 63 · Guide 63 The Wig Comes Off · Wheel Chapter 63 · Index Chapter 63

Characters: Miss MacIntosh

Allusions: Ecclesiastes · Genesis · The Bible

"the Rock of Ages in a land where all things crumbled" borrows Toplady's hymn, God the one firm rock while everything else gives way.

“the simulacrum, the graven image of truth”: Simulacrum is a representation, imitation, or copy of a person or thing. In a postmodern context, it refers to a copy without an original, or a copy that has become detached from reality. (Jean Baudrillard’s Simulacra and Simulation, 1981, is after 1964.) “Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image” (Exodus 20:4, KJV). See The Bible. Earlier in Chapters 3 and 22.

“the Emperor Constantine who had changed the calendar”: Constantine the Great, the first Christian emperor, legalized Christianity and in 321 made Sunday, “the venerable day of the Sun,” the day of rest. Miss MacIntosh scolds him for changing the calendar and for an institutional Christianity that, in her view, set imposed order in place of natural rhythms and common sense. See On Emperor Constantine and Augustine and Christian Theology. In 321 Constantine ordered that “the venerable day of the Sun” be a day of rest for city courts and workshops, the first Sunday law.

“It would be impractical for the infernal spirits to conceive their own damnation”: “The mind is its own place, and in itself / Can make a Heav’n of Hell, a Hell of Heav’n” (John Milton, Paradise Lost, 1.254-255, 1667). See Milton.

“God Who brings the blackness of the day”: “Wilt thou break a leaf driven to and fro?” (Job 13:25, KJV); “and maketh the day dark with night” (Amos 5:8, KJV). See The Book of Job.

“she preferring either the light of the Apocalypse or of Genesis”: “And God said, Let there be light: and there was light” (Genesis 1:3, KJV); “And the city had no need of the sun, neither of the moon, to shine in it: for the glory of God did lighten it” (Revelation 21:23, KJV). See Genesis and Revelation and the Apocalypse.

“Death came like a thief in the night”: “For yourselves know perfectly that the day of the Lord so cometh as a thief in the night” (1 Thessalonians 5:2, KJV). See The Bible.

"I may be sailing, sailing without a shroud" glances at the burial cloth, the linen that wrapped Christ.

“she with her fire red hair gleaming like the lights in a fiery furnace”: The three young men thrown into Nebuchadnezzar’s fiery furnace came out unharmed: “nor was an hair of their head singed” (Daniel 3:27, KJV). See The Bible and Hair, Wig, and Baldness.

"if ever the golden bowl is broken, or the wheel at the cistern," she says, then waves it off, "the Bible exaggerates. You cannot break that which was always broken," turning Ecclesiastes' images of death against the Book itself.

"the mad man leading the mad to his destruction" reworks the blind leading the blind of Matthew 15.

"Heaven preserve you from a beardless man," and "from a hairy woman," is an old proverb of distrust, a warning against appearances that lie.

“The sun and the moon will shine in the same sky”: “The sun shall be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before the great and the terrible day of the LORD come” (Joel 2:31, KJV). See Revelation and the Apocalypse.

"ours is a Pilgrim's Progress from nowhere to nowhere," and "we must walk through seas as if they were flowering cherry orchards," takes Bunyan's pilgrimage and empties it of any destination, faith reduced to a willed pretense of solid ground.

"Who can help me but God Himself Who has already failed and is not?" is Miss MacIntosh's bleakest cry, a God who has failed and does not exist appealed to all the same.

“the Emperor Constantine or King Canute or Crispin or Clarence or Cuthbert”: King Canute, or Cnut, ruled England, Denmark, and Norway. Miss MacIntosh invokes him against those who “waste our lives in the vain expectation of that which might never happen,” dreaming of “what was not, of what would never be.” Catherine names Canute too, in her opium dreams, and Miss MacIntosh insists the “opium paradise was never God’s way of life.” The story of Canute setting his throne on the shore and bidding the tide stand still, to show his courtiers that no king commands the sea, comes from Henry of Huntingdon (twelfth century). See On King Canute and The History of Opium. Crispin or Saints Crispin and Crispinian are the Christian patron saints of cobblers, curriers, tanners, and leather workers. They were beheaded during the reign of Diocletian. “And Crispin Crispian shall ne’er go by” (William Shakespeare, Henry V, 4.3). Clarence may refer to the Duke of Clarence which is a substantive title traditionally awarded to junior members of the British Royal Family. George, Duke of Clarence, was drowned in a butt of malmsey wine in the Tower in 1478, as Shakespeare shows in Richard III. Cuthbert may refer to Cuthbert of Lindisfarne who was an Anglo-Saxon saint of the early Northumbrian church in the Celtic tradition. He was a monk, bishop and hermit, associated with the monasteries of Melrose and Lindisfarne in the Kingdom of Northumbria, today in north eastern England and South Eastern Scotland.

"I am killed by the oldest preacher, he with the flaming hair" is Miss MacIntosh's figure for her own undoing, a preacher cast as a kind of John the Baptist, and a death that is emotional rather than literal.

“Where’s my hair, that woman’s glory?”: “But if a woman have long hair, it is a glory to her: for her hair is given her for a covering” (1 Corinthians 11:15, KJV). See The Bible, Hair, Wig, and Baldness, and Women, Gender, and Marriage. Earlier in Chapter 20.

“Drape all the mirrors with black”: Mirrors in a house of mourning were covered, by Jewish custom during shiva and in Victorian homes, so the soul of the dead would not be caught or the living distracted by vanity. See Mirrors and Reflections and Weddings and Funerals.

“Some may be like Jonah”: Jonah from the Book of Jonah was swallowed by a large fish where he prays to God for three days. He is then released. The story of Jonah in the Bible teaches about God’s universal mercy and grace, showing that his compassion extends to all people, not just the Israelites. It also highlights the dangers of disobedience and prejudice, as the prophet Jonah attempts to flee God’s command to preach to the hated city of Nineveh. “Now the LORD had prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights” (Jonah 1:17, KJV). See The Bible.

“but some may be like Noah wanting to come up”: Noah is the last of the pre-flood patriarchs of the Book of Genesis. He builds an ark and takes pairs of animals in order to survive the great flood. The biblical story of Noah’s Ark signifies a profound message about divine judgment, mercy, and salvation. It illustrates that God judges wickedness and sin but also provides a way for salvation for those who are righteous, like Noah, and those who have faith. The story highlights themes of God’s faithfulness through the covenant of the rainbow, and in Christian theology, the ark is often seen as a foreshadowing of Christ as the ultimate source of salvation. The dove came back with “an olive leaf pluckt off” (Genesis 8:11, KJV). See Genesis. The runner who brought Athens the news of Marathon (490 BC) is said to have cried “Rejoice, we conquer!” and died. Wimbledon has played lawn tennis since 1877. Earlier in Chapter 2.

"What was brought to Noah by a dove?" recalls the olive leaf of Genesis 8, the sign that the flood had passed.

"Here's one who was never born and yet has died" names the stillborn, the unlived life, one of the chapter's litany of the dead, the man dead far from home, the man dead intestate.

“It is woman who makes man’s life so short and robs him of his hair”: “And she made him sleep upon her knees; and she called for a man, and she caused him to shave off the seven locks of his head” (Judges 16:19, KJV). Delilah’s shears took Samson’s strength. See The Bible and Hair, Wig, and Baldness.

"Live each day as if it were the last. For it will be the last for some" turns the old carpe diem toward plain mortality.

“I suffer especially when there are rainbows”: “I do set my bow in the cloud, and it shall be for a token of a covenant between me and the earth” (Genesis 9:13, KJV), God’s promise after the flood. See Genesis.
