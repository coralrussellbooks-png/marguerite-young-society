The Sea-House Servants: The Sane Ones Who Caught the Dream

← The Black Coachman · Teaching a Writing Masterclass · Mr. Res Tacamah →

Spoiler note: This portrait is written for readers who have finished the novel. It discusses the character’s whole story, including how it ends.

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

They are the ones who keep the great sea-blackened house running while its mistress dreams her life away upstairs, the real maids and cooks and valets on Mr. Spitzer's payroll, and by the time the novel looks at them closely they have caught what she has, so that the people hired to hold the line against the dream are living inside it too. Being the sane, waking, wage-earning staff of a house and having gone as imaginary as its phantom guests is what they are.

They run the book's central move backward. The mother dreams companions into being, a chauffeur, a coachman, a man of letters in a medicine bottle, and treats the invented as real. The servants are the reverse operation, real people turning imaginary from the inside. They are "quite comme il faut as to their unusual environment, taking it all for granted," and what they take for granted is a house full of the dead. They hear "the carriage wheels in the driveway" and "the neighings of the dead horses" in the long nights. They discuss the phantom callers the way one discusses the neighbors, "the latest doings of this fine gentleman or that," one who discovered a new planet, one who believes he dwells on Uranus. One of them "did nothing but polish the shoes left out each evening" by guests who were never there, and grumbles about their muddy boots. Another is sure he lives in "a forest of cuckoo clocks." Another imagines he is "Hannibal driving the elephants over the Alps." Just to hear the servants talk, the book says, anyone might have thought the opium dreams were all real, "especially when they were unreal."

Ask the wrong question and this is just atmosphere, a spooky staff for a spooky house. Ask the right one and the servants carry the second pillar of Radical Immanence somewhere it has not yet gone. Reality is constructed rather than found, the book keeps saying, and every version of the claim so far has lived inside one head, the mother's or Vera's or Miss MacIntosh's. The servants prove it is contagious. The construction does not stay sealed in the dreamer who built it. It spreads to the sane people brought in to keep the house, colonizes their waking hours, hands them invented errands and invented selves, until the proof of the made world is not that its author believes in it but that the hired help have caught it like a fever and gone to work inside it.

The figure who keeps them a portrait instead of a blur is Wellington. He is an old valet with "no man to whom he should serve as valet," no master anywhere in the house, and rather than stand idle he has decided he waits on the Duke of Wellington. The other servants call him Wellington. The mother calls him Wellington and asks after "Wellington's appetites." A real servant handed a false identity not by the dreamer but by his own need and the household's habit, given a self out of nothing and answering to it, is the exact mirror of James, the false servant handed a real biography from the outside. James is an absence dressed as a man. Wellington is a man dressed as an absence. Between them they close the circuit the whole section has been drawing, the invented made into something solid and the solid invented, and it is fitting that both should be servants, since a servant is already a person a household would rather see as a function than a face.

What keeps them their own case, and not merely more of Catherine's dreaming, is that they keep one foot in the daylight the phantoms never touch. Their bedrooms hold clocks that tell the real hour, because "it was not the illusion of servants," who must wake and work while every other clock in the house stands stopped. They gamble in the housekeeper's parlor. Mr. Spitzer pays their wages out of his own thinning pockets, keeping a full staff for a woman who is, he half believes, already dead. And they are afraid. They fear she walks at night, having found "a bare footprint on a marble stair" and "the streak of her long white nightgown in the dust." They fear her wine is drugged with a "nepenthe" that would take their "personal sorrows, their corporeal senses, and their powers of reason," put them to sleep in the dream for good, and leave their carriages standing forever in the drive. They are the crossing point between the opium economy upstairs and the real one, people who punch a clock and draw a wage and nonetheless serve tea to the dead, and who know, better than their mistress, exactly how much they stand to lose to the dream, and go on losing it.

The turn for a writer is that a made world is not kept behind the eyes of the one who made it. It leaks. Build a house of the dead convincingly enough and the living people hired to sweep its floors will begin to hear the carriages, polish the phantom boots, answer to borrowed names, and take the whole impossible arrangement for granted. That is the deepest and least comfortable form of the book's claim, and it is the one a writer performs on a reader without meaning to. The invention does not stay on the page. It recruits. The maid trailing her lace petticoats in the dust and the valet waiting on a dead general are what happens to anyone who lives long enough inside someone else's dream, which is a fair description of reading, and of writing, and of the household staff who keep both going, on wages, by a clock that still tells time, in a house where nothing else does.
