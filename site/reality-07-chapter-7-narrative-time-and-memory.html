Reality and Illusion in MMMD

Chapter 7: Narrative, Time, and Memory

← Chapter 6: The Search for Meaning and the Ethical Life · Exploring Reality and Illusion in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling · Chapter 8: What Reality and Illusion Finally Mean →

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Homer Capehorn · Jacqueline White · Joachim Spitzer · Madge Capehorn · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer · Vera Cartwheel

The earlier chapters have treated Young’s ideas: illusion as primary, the self as a crowd, death as something only ever met through what it leaves behind. This chapter turns to the form that carries those ideas. Young’s style is not a coat of paint on her argument. It is the argument. Her sentences build a reality around the reader and then dissolve it, so that anyone who reads the book long enough goes through for themselves what Pillar Two describes. The ground is made, and the reader feels it being made.

Part One: How the Novel Builds Its Dream

The dreamlike quality of the novel comes from a set of techniques that work together. Each can be seen on the page.

The sentence as a wave. Young’s most striking habit is the sentence that refuses to end. Madge Capehorn, the pregnant girl on the bus, is described in one that runs for most of a page: “There were rings on her fingers, cheap cameos of an enormous size and pieces of colored glass, many brass and wooden bracelets on her arms, a gold chain with a heart on her gauze-shrouded ankle,” and on through straw flowers, velvet butterflies, and a coat that “would not close.” The sentence does not just describe Madge. It performs the experience of a mind flooded by detail, unable to rank what it sees. The reader, like Vera, finds no place to rest. Every clause gives birth to another.

The landscape turned to ocean. The Indiana flatlands become sea: “oceanic, dotted by pale pools in the vapors of mist,” with Vera half expecting to see “a lost ship which would never reach port.” Soon the outer world is gone altogether: “There was now no landscape but the soul’s, and that is the inexactitude, the ever shifting and the distant.” The ocean is not a metaphor for the mind here. It is the mind, leaking out and replacing the fields.

The incantatory “because.” When Vera explains why she has come, she gives not one reason but a flood of them: “I had come because of my own heart’s need for an answer,” then because of the dead, a chance remark, a dead girl’s love letters, a deaf musician, “a blind man’s groping for his coffee cup.” The word “because” promises an explanation and delivers a pile-up. No reason outranks another. This is not how people reason, but it is how people feel when they cannot organize their grief.

The catalogue. Catherine’s guests arrive in lists that move without a pause from the historical to the mythical: Bostonians, Platonists, “drowned sailors, gold-turbaned Egyptians, centaurs with lilies in their mouths.” No item is more real than any other. The categories that would make the list absurd have dissolved in the mind doing the listing.

The simile that opens onto an abyss. Young’s comparisons deepen mysteries rather than solve them. The bus driver’s head is “an old, archaic dome of curled sculpture.” The sky over the plains is drained “as if it had suffered, in one slow moment, the ultimate transfusion.” Each “as if” reaches for something familiar and finds something stranger.

The phantom reported as fact. Catherine’s nightly carriage ride through a snowy city is told in the same plain past tense as anything else, down to her uncertainty about “whether it was the city of the living or of the dead” and whether it was “before or after the Revolution.” There are no italics, no hedges, and no signal that this is a vision. Because the grammar of the dream is the grammar of the fact, the reader has no neutral ground from which to sort them.

The cumulative effect. Together these techniques make the novel feel less like an account of a dream than a dream itself. The endless sentence keeps the reader from waking. The ocean removes the ground. The incantation turns narration into ritual. The catalogue puts the hallucinated and the real on the same footing. The simile leads away from clarity. This is Pillar Two carried out on the reader: over a thousand pages, the habit of telling the solid from the imagined wears away.

Part Two: Duality and Doubling

Doubling is the novel’s basic architecture. Nearly every major character is built on a pair, and the pairs do not stay opposed. They collapse into each other.

The Spitzer brothers. Mr. Spitzer keeps “twin iron bedsteads, his own and his brother’s,” and wakes with the feeling that he is two bodies in two beds: “Sometimes he migrated from bed to bed.” Catherine, in her fevers, suspects “that the Mr. Spitzer who loved her was dead, that the wrong Mr. Spitzer was buried in his grave.” The narration reports that he “attended Mr. Spitzer’s funeral” and lets the sentence stay ambiguous. Part Three follows him further.

Miss MacIntosh: the great unmasking. The most shocking doubling is the night Vera, in the dark, touches not red hair but “this bald head, smooth and hard as a billiard ball.” Miss MacIntosh’s own words at that moment split her in two: “Oh, where was my hair?” and then, “Where, oh, where’s Miss MacIntosh, that old fool we used to see here?” She speaks of herself as an absent third person. Vera fights “with shadows, with surfaces which were not,” and afterward names what she found: “I had discovered her essential baldness.” The word “essential” carries the argument. The bare skull is not a flaw in Miss MacIntosh but what lies under the performance of her. The character who most loudly insisted on the difference between the real and the illusory had been keeping up an illusion all along. With that, the novel’s central opposition between Catherine’s dreaming and Miss MacIntosh’s daylight cannot hold. Chapters 3 and 5 follow where this leads.

Catherine and her carriage. Catherine divides herself in her own dream: “Her body was the carriage, and her soul, now it seemed, was the passenger, and her horses were her will, and the coachman was her conscience.” The self becomes a vehicle whose parts no longer agree on where they are going.

The canary. “There had been many little yellow canaries in this cage,” the novel says, while Catherine presumes one continuous life. The bird is one and many at once. Mr. Spitzer’s substitutions are a small version of what the novel does to all its characters: the double is always present, and people are simply not told.

Madge and Jacqueline. On the bus, Madge calls her situation “this marriage of three,” herself, Homer, and the dying girl he loved first. Homer’s attempt to prove that two people cannot be one ends in “I am I, or am I? Am I? Are you? Is she?” Denying the double creates it.

The wedding dream. In Vera’s fourteenth-birthday dream, the preacher cannot tell “who was woman and who was man,” and when the fog lifts, “Mr. Spitzer was the bride.” Miss MacIntosh stands as bridegroom. The dream shows openly what the waking novel suggests: the practical nursemaid and the grieving musician are halves of one divided figure, and the division crosses gender.

The shape of it. The doublings do not resolve. Miss MacIntosh vanishes, Mr. Spitzer keeps migrating between beds, and Catherine never leaves hers. Young’s most radical claim is that the doubled self is not a problem to solve but the permanent human condition, the “essential baldness” beneath whatever wig a soul has learned to wear.

Part Three: Mr. Spitzer and the Self That Cannot Be Probated

Chapters 2, 4, and 5 have drawn on Mr. Spitzer. What remains is the fullest version of his case, because it is the book’s most developed argument about identity and memory.

The brothers were “outwardly identical,” and inwardly opposites. Peron was “quick to live, quick to die,” a gambler who cared only for the moment. Joachim was slow, faithful, musical. Each defined himself against the other, so when one died, the survivor lost not only a brother but the mirror in which he knew himself. “He, Mr. Spitzer, was this soul divided, for he was this twin, and one was dead, and one lived, this confusion reigning like order in his great heart.” Confusion has become so settled that it works as his equilibrium.

Death did not free him from Peron. It deepened the bond. He remembers his brother so completely that he is “living his brother’s life and dying his brother’s death.” He pays Peron’s debts “now with every new day of his life.” He imagines what Peron would have done, and the imagined brother is always bolder than the living one: Peron “would have swept my mother into the living sunlight.” He carries “his dead brother’s continuing but sporadic problems, ... those which were not his in reality but which he had assumed.” “Assumed” means both “took on” and “pretended,” and the line between inheriting a self and performing one has disappeared.

He is a lawyer who spends his working life on wills, deeds, and rightful heirs, and he cannot settle his own case. An “obtuse desk clerk” once told him that Mr. Spitzer was dead. He could not prove otherwise. The self, it turns out, is not an estate that can be probated.

Then there is the glass. Peron fell through a window on the fourteenth floor, and Mr. Spitzer cannot decide whether it was suicide or whether “Peron had not really intended to die.” He himself once walked through a glass wall and survived. “If the floor level had continued on the other side, then Peron would certainly have lived.” The two brothers’ fates, and so their identities as the reckless one and the careful one, come down to architecture.

His music has gone silent, lost in “the lost chords, the silent keys,” falling “on deaf ears, silent ears, dead ears.” He claims his brother’s death silenced it, but Catherine catches him: “She knew that it was before.” The music was fading while Peron lived, as if the loud twin left no room for the quiet one, and removing the loud one did not bring the music back.

Under all of this lies guilt. If he is the right man, he is “the right man in the wrong world,” and if the wrong one, the wrong man in the wrong world. He cannot win the case. “Only through him, as he could not too often insist, his brother lived.” Mr. Spitzer is grief made into a person. He shows how every self is shadowed by those it has loved, how the dead keep thinking through the living, and how the living are always practicing the silence that will become permanent. In the novel’s last chapter, Vera reports that he finally learned, “for what might seem only a shining moment,” “which brother he truly was the dead.” The case closes only in death.

Part Four: How Time Works

The framework this book follows includes a principle called The Past Doesn't Go Away: the past is not behind us but beneath us, pressed into the present like layers of rock. No novel illustrates it more fully. Young does not merely show characters experiencing time differently. She takes apart the idea of time as a line.

The stopped clocks. The clocks in Catherine’s house are “like enchanted cages, each clock having been stopped at a different hour” of her long withdrawal. Every stage of her retreat is present at once, none replacing the one before. Yet “sometimes, in a remote part of the house, especially at night, a stopped clock would suddenly start up,” as if the buried layer had shifted. Time here does not flow. It piles up, stalls, and suddenly moves.

Sequence undone. Catherine lives in a world where “the echo came before the song,” the afterglow before the sunset, and “the death came before the life.” She will “introduce the living among the dead” because she already sees their deaths. This is not only a disordered mind. It is a claim that sequence is a feature of ordinary perception rather than of the world, and that opium has simply removed the convention.

Memory as presence. For Vera and Catherine, memory is not a reaching back. It is the past still here. When Catherine thinks of Cousin Hannah, dead for years, the “years collapsed to a moment.” Vera’s search for Miss MacIntosh is told in the past tense, “Long nights, searching for one who was dead,” but it has never ended. It rides the bus with her.

The late arrival. Chapter 2 described how Mr. Spitzer “saw the traffic signals always a little late,” living at a “second remove” from the present. His jeweled watch tracks seconds and “the revolutions of the planets around the sun,” a “starry wheel.” The watch measures time perfectly and cannot make him live in it.

Miss MacIntosh’s watch. Against all this stands the champion of clock time, whose watch betrays her in a single breath: “Time marches on. There is no time. This dollar watch deceives me.” Her arithmetic lessons (“Twelve hours in a day, twelve hours in a night. That is all we have.”) are an attempt to lay a grid over the flood. The grid needed constant maintenance, and in the end the woman who kept it walked into the sea, where no clock could find her.

What never happened. Catherine carries the book’s farthest claim about time: that what never came to be is not less real for that. “The love which had never come into being was a greater love than love, and there was greater passion in restraint than in passion, greater being in non-being.” The life not lived, the music never written, and the child never born stay present as a pressure on everything that did happen.

Bergson’s duration. Henri Bergson drew the distinction that best names Young’s time. There is measured time, laid out like points on a line and counted by clocks, and there is durée, duration, time as lived: continuous, qualitative, the past carried forward inside each present moment rather than left behind. Bergson believed that the intellect, trained on objects in space, habitually mistakes lived time for the measured kind. Miss MacIntosh’s dollar watch is measured time. Catherine’s stopped clocks, Mr. Spitzer’s late arrivals, and Vera’s endless search are duration. The novel’s form belongs to duration too. Vera’s whole life before the bus ride is folded into her first chapters without transitions, because in duration there is nothing to transition across.

Part Five: Memory and the Story of a Self

Paul Ricoeur argued that a person holds together over time by telling a story. He called the act of weaving scattered events into a meaningful whole “emplotment,” and he distinguished two kinds of identity. Idem is sameness: the body, the habits, the recognizable features. Ipse is selfhood, the self that keeps its promises even when everything recognizable about it has changed.

Young’s novel tests both ideas. Vera’s great list of reasons for her journey, the incantatory “because,” is emplotment refusing to happen. The events will not arrange themselves into a plot. They stay a pile. If a self is a story, then Vera’s self is a story that will not close.

Miss MacIntosh shows the other distinction. Her idem, her sameness, was manufactured: the wig, the corset, the helmet of red hair. When it falls away, what remains is ipse, the self that kept its promise to care for a child who was not hers, day after day, for years. The novel suggests that this is the self worth loving. Vera comes to love “my bald love,” whose “lack would be my love.” The recognizable woman was an illusion. The faithful one was not.

Ricoeur also wrote of a “happy forgetting” that makes it possible to live on after loss. It is worth noticing that almost no one in Young’s novel manages it. Mr. Spitzer cannot forget, Esther hears her children every night, and Vera carries everyone. Their failure to forget is their loyalty, and it is also their weight.

Part Six: The Elemental Grammar

Young’s natural imagery is not decoration. Water, ice, wind, and stars form a vocabulary through which the novel thinks.

Water dissolves boundaries. The ocean around Catherine’s house is the outer form of consciousness, and dying is imagined as drowning: “those who drowned not in the sea are drowned on the shore.” The tide gives Young her most exact image for Mr. Spitzer’s way of moving through the world, “advancing to retreat,” so that if he took eight steps forward, “he must retrace his steps by six in order to see where he had been.” The famous line about every heart being the other heart is water logic: drops without permanent borders.

Ice is the moment arrested. It gathers around Cousin Hannah, whose passion was frozen into crusade: “Nothing so cold as these great mountains rising from a lady’s heart.” It gathers around Mr. Spitzer, who walks as though on thin ice after his brother fell through a surface that did not hold. The stopped clocks are ice logic, and the clock that starts in the night is the thaw.

Wind carries what has no body: voices, spirit, the presence of the dead. Mr. Spitzer’s “dead heart was like a sea shell carrying its own roaring,” the sound of the absent ocean kept in an empty chamber.

Stars hold the paradox of old light, which arrives after its source may be gone. The dead in this novel are starlight. Of Cousin Hannah, “Death had not diminished her.” She is like the dead tree that “only after it is dead” breaks into “a curiously fugitive, wraith-like mist” of flowers. And the smallest things may carry the largest light: “a flying sand grain might grow to be a star even as had that sand grain which flew into Mr. Spitzer’s eye” and “begot this pearl.”

Convergence. The elements keep turning into one another. Water freezes, ice melts, and mist becomes wind. At the end, Mr. Spitzer thins into all of them at once: “water flowed into water.” The self, in this imagery, is not a thing but a pattern, a temporary arrangement of forces that flowed through it before it existed and will keep flowing after. What remains when the form dissolves is a trace: the roar in the emptied shell, the light still traveling, the pearl grown around a grain of grit.

Reflection Questions

  1. Young’s sentences often refuse to rank what they describe. How does it change reading to be denied a sense of what matters most in a sentence?

  2. Miss MacIntosh’s unmasking reveals her “essential baldness.” Is what lies beneath a person’s constructions truer than the constructions, or only different?

  3. Mr. Spitzer cannot settle the case of Spitzer versus Spitzer. Are there parts of any life that cannot be judged by evidence at all?

  4. Miss MacIntosh says, “Time marches on. There is no time.” Can both halves of that sentence be true?

  5. If a self holds together by keeping its promises, which promises hold a life together when its outward sameness is gone?

  6. The novel’s elements keep changing into one another. What trace of a person remains when the form that held them dissolves?
