What the Earth Carries and What the Law Must Acknowledge

A Companion Document to the Constitution

The Philosophical Foundation

Some losses do not resolve with time. The theft of indigenous land, the centuries of enslaved labor, the systematic exclusion of women, queer people, disabled people, and immigrants from the promises this nation made about itself: these are not events the present has moved beyond. They are conditions in the present, carried forward in bodies, in communities, in landscapes, and in the accumulated structures of who has access to what.

The framework this constitution is built on holds that grief is cumulative, that carrying is collective, and that the work of reckoning does not end on a deadline set by law. The people most directly descended from these losses cannot be asked to perform a recovery they have not experienced. When the nation asks them to, it is not asking for healing. It is asking for the appearance of healing so that everyone else can stop looking.

This document describes what honest institutional acknowledgment of historical grief requires, what reparative justice means in practice, and what an ongoing process of reckoning looks like when it is designed to be genuine rather than symbolic.

What Was Done: The Specific Losses

Indigenous Peoples and the Theft of Land

The land on which the United States exists was taken. That sentence is not contested by serious historians. The mechanisms varied: treaty, war, forced removal, starvation, the deliberate destruction of the food systems on which specific peoples depended. The treaties that were signed were routinely violated when the land they protected became desirable to settlers and the political will to honor them evaporated. The Indian Removal Act of 1830, the forced marches that followed, the reservation system that concentrated people on the least productive land available, the allotment system that broke up reservation land and transferred it to white ownership: these were not the incidental costs of westward expansion. They were the policy.

The federal boarding school system, which the Interior Department's own investigation traced from 1819 through 1969 across more than four hundred schools, operated with the explicit goal of destroying indigenous languages, cultures, family structures, and identities. The motto of the Carlisle Indian Industrial School, the first federally funded boarding school, was "Kill the Indian, save the man." Children were taken from their families, forbidden to speak their languages, punished for practicing their cultures, and in documented cases subjected to abuse, forced labor, and death. The graves of children who did not survive are still being located on school grounds across the country. Several federally run boarding schools still operate today, though under a changed mission. The people whose parents and grandparents were taken are alive. The trauma is not historical. It is present.

The effects are documented consistently in the research on indigenous communities: elevated rates of poverty, substance use, suicide, and disease that are inseparable from the historical conditions that produced them. The Indian Health Service, the federal agency responsible for healthcare for indigenous people as part of treaty obligations, is chronically underfunded at a fraction of what other federal healthcare programs receive per capita. The treaty obligation exists. The funding to honor it never has been adequate.

Enslaved Labor and Its Aftermath

The United States was built with enslaved labor. The cotton economy that financed the industrial development of the North, the agricultural infrastructure of the South, the wealth that became the capital base of American economic expansion: all of it was produced in significant part by people who were legally property, who could not own the product of their labor, whose family relationships had no legal standing, and whose bodies were subject to violence without legal recourse.

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery in 1865 with an exception: it remained legal as punishment for crime. The Black Codes that followed immediately recriminalized Black freedom, and the convict leasing system restored forced labor under the exception the amendment provided. The reconstruction of white supremacy after Reconstruction, through law, through terror, and through the systematic exclusion of Black Americans from the New Deal programs, the GI Bill, and the Federal Housing Administration loans that built the white middle class, continued the effects of slavery by other means.

The wealth gap between Black and white Americans today is the direct and traceable result of these specific policies. The destruction of the Greenwood district of Tulsa in 1921, in which a prosperous Black community was burned by a white mob with the participation of local law enforcement; the systematic redlining of Black neighborhoods by the FHA; the exclusion of domestic workers and agricultural laborers, predominantly Black and Latino, from the original Social Security Act: these were specific government decisions about whose wealth would be protected and whose would be extracted or destroyed. The gap they produced has not closed.

The Eugenics Program

The United States operated a forced sterilization program that targeted people the state had decided should not reproduce: people with disabilities, people with low incomes, Black and brown people, immigrants, and people whose behavior was classified as deviant. The Supreme Court gave it constitutional cover in its 1927 decision in Buck v. Bell, which upheld the forced sterilization of Carrie Buck. That decision has never been formally overturned. Forced sterilizations continued in California prisons as recently as 2010. The people who were sterilized without consent are part of a harm the nation has never formally renounced.

The Exclusion of Women from Economic and Civic Life

Women were excluded from voting until 1920, and the Nineteenth Amendment's passage did not extend voting rights to Black women in the South, where voter suppression effectively continued until the Voting Rights Act of 1965. Women were legally barred from many professions, from jury service, from holding property independently of their husbands, and from credit in their own names until the Equal Credit Opportunity Act of 1974. The economic consequences of generations of exclusion from the accumulation of wealth and professional standing are present in the wage gap, the wealth gap, and the concentration of unpaid caregiving work that persist today.

LGBTQ+ People and the Violence of Criminalization

Homosexuality was classified as a mental illness by the American Psychiatric Association until 1973. Same-sex conduct was criminalized in many states until the Supreme Court's 2003 decision in Lawrence v. Texas. The AIDS crisis of the 1980s killed tens of thousands of people while the federal government deliberately withheld a response, a documented policy choice made by an administration that considered the affected populations expendable. The people who died, the communities that lost them, and the generation that came of age under the terror of the epidemic without government protection represent a loss the nation has not acknowledged.

What Acknowledgment Requires

The truth and reparative justice process established in Article VII is not a one-time event. It is an ongoing institution, funded permanently, operating continuously, and subject to direct democratic oversight by the communities most directly affected. It draws on the models of truth and reconciliation processes from other countries while being honest about what those processes have and have not achieved.

The South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission created a public record that could not be denied and gave many survivors the experience of being heard by an official body. It did not produce economic reparation and did not fully address the structural inequalities apartheid had built. The Canadian Truth and Reconciliation Commission produced ninety-four calls to action documenting what the residential school system had done. Implementation of those calls to action has been slow and incomplete. The lessons are clear: acknowledgment without repair is incomplete, and repair without structural change reproduces the conditions it claims to address.

The process this constitution establishes has four components that operate simultaneously rather than sequentially.

The First Component: The Public Record

The historical record of what was done, to whom, by whom, and with what consequences is documented in full and made publicly accessible. This means the specific policies, the specific decisions, the specific people who made them, and the specific communities who bore the consequences. It includes documents that agencies have classified or withheld. It includes the testimony of survivors and descendants. It is maintained as a living archive, updated as new evidence emerges and as descendants contribute their knowledge of what was carried in families and communities.

The Second Component: Community-Controlled Acknowledgment

The communities most directly affected by each history have genuine authority over how that history is acknowledged. The federal government does not design a single acknowledgment process and impose it on all affected communities. It funds and supports the processes those communities design for themselves. Indigenous nations, Black communities, the descendants of people forcibly sterilized, LGBTQ+ communities, and other affected groups each have the resources and the authority to determine what honest acknowledgment looks like from their perspective. The process is multilingual, community-rooted, and not bounded by a deadline.

The Third Component: Material Repair

Acknowledgment without material repair is the performance of reckoning rather than reckoning itself. The specific forms of material repair are determined through the democratic process, with the affected communities having genuine decision-making power over what repair looks like for them. The framework includes:

Tribal sovereignty honored in full: existing treaties are honored, land held in trust is returned where communities seek its return, and federal obligations under existing treaties are funded at levels adequate to meet them. The Indian Health Service is funded at parity with other federal healthcare programs. Tribal nations have genuine governmental authority over their territories without requiring federal approval for decisions within their sovereign jurisdiction.

Reparations for the descendants of enslaved people: the specific mechanism is developed through the democratic process, with the communities most directly affected having genuine authority over the design. The precedent exists. The United States paid reparations to Japanese Americans interned during World War II, to the survivors of the Tuskegee syphilis study, and to the survivors of other specific documented government wrongs. The scale of the wrong of slavery and its aftermath is larger than any of these precedents. The scale of the repair is proportional.

Restoration for survivors of forced sterilization and their families, where survivors are living: direct compensation and full access to reproductive healthcare and support.

Investment in the communities most directly damaged by specific historical policies: the reservation communities where the Indian Health Service has been underfunded, the neighborhoods redlined by federal policy, the communities destroyed by the war on drugs, the places where the cumulative burden of specific government decisions is most visible in the present landscape.

The Fourth Component: Structural Change

Reparative justice that does not change the structures that produced the harm will need to be repeated. The structural changes are the other companion documents to this constitution: the criminal justice provisions that address the racial construction of the carceral system, the economic floor that addresses the wealth gap, the healthcare provisions that address Indian Health Service underfunding and documented disparities in how Black patients are treated by the medical system, the voting rights provisions that address the suppression of Black and indigenous votes, the education provisions that address curriculum erasure and the school-to-prison pipeline. The truth and reparative justice process is not separate from these structural changes. It is the honest account of why they are necessary.

On the Objection That It Is Too Long Ago

The most common objection to reparative justice is that the wrongs occurred too long ago: the people who committed them are dead, the people most directly harmed are dead, and the current generation cannot be held responsible for what their ancestors did. This objection misunderstands what reparative justice is addressing.

The wealth gap between Black and white Americans is not the product of events that ended in 1865. It is the product of specific government decisions made in the twentieth century. Many of the people directly affected by redlining, by exclusion from the GI Bill, by the destruction of specific communities, are alive. Many more are the direct children and grandchildren of people who were affected.

The underfunding of the Indian Health Service is not a historical wrong. It is a current wrong, renewed in each budget cycle. The treaties that were violated are not old documents with no current legal standing. They are binding legal agreements that the United States has continued to violate.

The question is not whether the loss is present. It is. The question is whether the institution is honest about what it knows.

On the Objection That It Divides Rather Than Unites

The second common objection is that focusing on historical wrongs divides the nation rather than uniting it. This objection has the causation backwards. The division is produced by the unaddressed wrong, not by addressing it. The nation that asks the people most directly affected by historical violence to absorb that violence quietly in the interest of national unity is not asking for unity. It is asking for silence.

Honest acknowledgment of what happened, material repair of what can be repaired, and structural change that prevents the reproduction of the same patterns: this is what unity on honest terms requires. Unity that requires some people to pretend they have not been harmed is not unity.

The Ongoing Process

The truth and reparative justice process does not have an end date. What it has is a structure: permanent funding, community authority, democratic oversight, and the commitment to honesty about what is known and what remains to be known.
