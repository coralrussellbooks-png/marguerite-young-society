Chapter 42: The Deathbed of the Suffrage Captain

← Chapter 41 · Guide Home · Chapter 43 →

Research notes: Chapter 42

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Themes: Death and Mortality · The Search for Meaning

Symbols: Horses and Carriages · The House · The Sea and Water · Weddings and Funerals

Philosophy: Marguerite Young on Her Craft · Nietzsche

Allusions: Fairy Tales and Folklore · Greek Mythology · Women's Suffrage

We have been circling Cousin Hannah's death for a very long time. The novel has approached it obliquely, through memory and myth, through Catherine's grief-that-looks-like-logic and Mr. Spitzer's second-remove consciousness and the long meditation on what she was and what she stood for and where she might have gone. Chapter 42 brings you to the room.

Mr. Spitzer is there. He is the last living witness, the only one who was present. And what he finds (what he has been unable to tell Catherine, what he has been obtusely, infuriatingly silent about across all these chapters) is this: the great suffrage captain died sobbing. Delirious. Calling out for things unnamed. Reduced, in her final hours, to something unrecognizable from the woman who had ridden through deserts and broken conventions and “played a mean game of cards” and never asked for pity.

Young holds this with absolute steadiness. She does not make it a diminishment. She makes it the truth.

What actually happens in Chapter 42

The chapter opens with Catherine traveling to Cousin Hannah's deathbed, not in any physical sense, since Catherine cannot walk, but in a glass carriage drawn by alabaster horses, driven by a black coachman, passing through surreal landscapes of mirrors and fountains and sleeping cities. Whether this is a dream, a hallucination, a memory, or something the novel asks us to take seriously as a real event in its own terms, Young does not specify. Farewell, she tells us, is “a matter of opinion as to the time, the place, the person.”

Mr. Spitzer, meanwhile, is preoccupied with lists: lost ships, sunken argosies, passengers whose fates are unrecorded, coordinates that no longer correspond to anything on the surface. He has spent his life cataloguing what cannot be found. This is, we understand, his way of thinking about Cousin Hannah: about all the things she was that are now unlocatable.

And then: the deathbed itself. Mr. Spitzer at the side of the woman he has observed and envied and resented and mourned for decades, watching her die. She is unrecognizable. The fiery captain, the storm king, the great warrior of the suffrage movement, is reduced to a figure who sobs and cries out in her delirium. Snow seems to fall across her face. The white tent of her death chamber suggests a desert or a pagoda: East and West collapsing together in her final room, as they collapsed together in her life.

Mr. Spitzer reflects on what all her battles actually achieved. He is not certain. He is not sure the world she fought to change has changed enough to justify the cost she paid. He keeps vigil anyway.

The glass carriage and the meaning of farewell

Catherine's journey to the deathbed in her glass carriage is one of the novel's most purely dreamlike passages.

A glass carriage is transparent: you can be seen inside it, and you can see out. It is also fragile: the fairy tale material, Cinderella's vehicle that will become a pumpkin at midnight, the conveyance that cannot last. That Catherine makes this journey in glass is Young's way of saying: this visit is real and it is not real, it is visible and it is made of something that shatters, it is a farewell and a goodbye to someone who may already be beyond farewell.

The alabaster horses turn their heads like foam. The coachman is black: Catherine's consciousness evoked in dream form, as To All My Darlings notes. The sleeping cities they pass through are every city Catherine has visited only in imagination, which is to say, every city. She arrives at a deathbed she never physically reached, says a goodbye she never physically said, and returns to her own bed where she has always been. This is what Catherine's kind of love looks like: vast, surreal, and conducted entirely in the interior. It counts. Young means it to count.

Mr. Spitzer and the lost ships

Mr. Spitzer's obsession with lost ships and unrecorded passengers is one of the chapter's quietly perfect details. He is a man who researches what cannot be recovered, who catalogs the disappeared, who tries to find coordinates for things that have no location. This is grief dressed as a profession.

Every ship on his list is Cousin Hannah. Every lost passenger is the person she was before the dying, the person she was at thirty, at forty, at the height of the campaigns when the iron keys whistled at her belt and the snow followed her indoors. He cannot find them. The ocean has no reliable records. He keeps looking.

Young gives him the detail of valuing his dignity more than his life: the conservative figure, the man victimized by routine itself. He is stuck, as all rigidly conventional thinking is stuck, between the thing that must hold and the line that must not be crossed. He cannot act, cannot speak fully, cannot tell Catherine what he saw, because to do so would be to surrender the arrangement that has kept him functional. His silence about Cousin Hannah's death is not cruelty. It is the only form of containment available to a man who has no other.

Hannah, Hamid, Al Hamid, the Great Moor

Chapter 42 gives Cousin Hannah multiple identities in a way the novel has been building toward: she is Hannah in Boston, Hamid in the East, Al Hamid, the Great Moor. These are not simply aliases. They are the different selves a person accumulates when they travel far enough and long enough and cross enough of the boundaries that were supposed to contain them.

Al Hamid is one of the Beautiful Names of Allah in Islamic tradition: the praiseworthy one, the laudable, the object of all praise. That Young weaves this into Cousin Hannah's accumulated identities, following the implication that she may have converted to Islam during her travels and eventually departed from all faith entirely, is precise. Hannah is the New England suffragette. Hamid is the traveler who crossed into other worlds. Al Hamid is the name given to the most praiseworthy. The Great Moor is the mythic figure she became in the imaginations of those who watched her go.

One person. Many names. Many selves acquired through a life lived without regard for the borders that were supposed to keep her on one side.

The unknown bridegroom

The novel surfaces here a rumor it has been keeping: there was a bridegroom. Someone left at the church door. Someone Cousin Hannah arrived for and then, at the threshold of the conventional life, turned away from. Or did not turn away from: the details are obscure, buried in the lacunae Mr. Spitzer's research keeps finding.

There are also rumors of a secret man, a caliph, someone who supported her cause from the shadows and whose relationship to her was never made legible.

Young does not resolve these rumors. She is not interested in resolving them. What she is interested in is the fact of their existence: that behind the public armor, behind the iron keys and the whistling and the suffrage campaigns and the masculine virtues and the refusal of self-pity, there was a private life that nobody fully knew. That Cousin Hannah was not only the figure she presented. That the woman who had no time for intimate revelations had intimacies she chose not to reveal.

The bridegroom left at the door is Young's sharpest emblem of this. An epithalamium, a poem written for the bride on her way to the marital chamber, was written, or deserved to be written, for someone who did not walk through that door. The poem was never delivered. The chamber was never occupied. And yet the fact of it (the door, the waiting, the choice made at the threshold) is part of who Cousin Hannah was.

What war has truly accomplished

Mr. Spitzer's meditation on war and legacy is one of the chapter's most politically charged passages, and one of the most honest.

He looks at the list of Cousin Hannah's battles: “the Peloponnesian Wars, the Siege of Rome,” Custer's Last Stand, Sherman's March, the Boer War, Paul Revere's Ride, the Siege of Atlanta, “the burning of Carthage, the taking of Venice,” the Balkan Wars (and then at her own campaign, the hundred-year war for women's suffrage) and he asks: what did it achieve? She is dying of a broken heart, forgotten by the world she fought to change. Her battles have passed into oblivion. The movement she devoted her life to has not finished its work. The rights it won are not secure. They are still being contested.

To All My Darlings notes something that lands with particular weight: suffrage for women took a hundred years, and many rights for women were not in place while Young was alive. And To All My Darlings goes on to observe that we have seen the erosion of these rights, the incompleteness of them worldwide, the organized attempts to roll them back. One can see, from this vantage, where there has to be a complete love, an obsession, for something that might never be achieved in one lifetime.

Cousin Hannah had that love. That obsession. She gave it everything. Young does not tell you it was enough. Young tells you it was what she had and she gave it and the work is not finished and she died sobbing in a tent that smelled like a desert and a pagoda, and Mr. Spitzer sat beside her and kept vigil and could not explain to Catherine what he saw.

Nietzsche's moment, and amor fati

The chapter draws on Nietzsche's concept of eternal recurrence: the thought experiment from Thus Spoke Zarathustra that asks: if you had to live this life over again, exactly as it was, with every pain and every joy, every loss and every failure and every moment of glory, for all eternity, could you love it? Would you choose it?

For Cousin Hannah, the answer implied by the novel is yes. She lived without compromise. She lived at full intensity. She gave herself entirely to the cause that claimed her. The secret wish for marriage, the bridegroom left at the door, the sobbing at the end; these are not refutations of the life. They are the life's full texture. Nietzsche called this amor fati: love of fate. Not acceptance of what happened, not resigned tolerance of what couldn't be changed, but love. Choosing it again. Saying yes to all of it.

Mr. Spitzer, watching from the bedside, cannot achieve this. He has been unable to love his fate for as long as we have known him. He survives. He does not choose.

Dying in obscurity: Hannah and Shulamith Firestone

To All My Darlings makes a connection that Young could not have made (Firestone died in 1970, and Young's novel was published in 1965) but that rings with the force of prophecy: Cousin Hannah dying in obscurity, forgotten by the world she helped create, is reminiscent of Shulamith Firestone's funeral, where mourners grappled with the idea that a whole generation of feminists had been unable to thrive in the world they had done so much to build.

This is one of the oldest and most painful patterns in the history of movements for justice: the people who do the foundational work do not live to see the harvest, and often die in conditions that bear no relationship to the magnitude of what they gave. Cousin Hannah is fictional, but she is made of real people. Her death in that white tent, reduced and sobbing and unrecognized for what she was, is a death Young knew how to write because she had seen its shape in the world.

What to hold onto

Chapter 42 is where the novel delivers what it has been holding back: the actual death of the great suffrage captain, rendered not as myth or legend but as a body in a room, failing, with one faithful if inadequate witness.

Mr. Spitzer could not tell Catherine. He could not find the words, or could not bear to say them, or knew that no description would be adequate to what he saw. The woman who had no time for self-pity died with all the self-pity her body could produce. The woman who “played a mean game of cards” showed all her cards at the end. This is not a failure. This is what dying does to even the most formidable people, and Young shows it to you without flinching because she believes you can bear the truth and that you deserve it.

Catherine made her farewell in a glass carriage, through sleeping cities, in the interior space where all her real relationships lived. It was a real farewell. It counted. Farewell is “a matter of opinion as to the time, the place, the person.” Young believes this. She means for you to believe it too.
