Definition from research

Chapter 2

  • Lord Byron was an English poet and leading figure in the Romantic movement of his era. He was born with a deformity of his foot which caused him to limp. Lord Byron and Percy Bysshe Shelley along with others, his wife Mary Shelley (Frankenstein) were a close circle of poets and writers.

Chapter 3

  • “the Protestant graveyard where Keats and Shelley slept and who after only one week in Rome had died of the marsh fever”: Keats and Shelley are buried in Rome’s Non-Catholic Cemetery near the Pyramid of Cestius. Marsh fever, or Roman fever, was malaria, whose name comes from the Italian mal aria, “bad air.” In Henry James’s “Daisy Miller” (1878), an American girl dies of Roman fever after a night visit to the Colosseum.

Chapter 4

  • “like a nymph in a mountain cave . . . the fairy grotto where the lone traveller is lured”: In Keats’s “La Belle Dame sans Merci” (1819), a knight is lured by a fairy lady to “her elfin grot” and wakes alone on a cold hillside.

Chapter 9

  • “Sit not there dreaming of Triton among the minnows”: Triton is a Greek god of the sea who later evolved into a merman. He is the messenger of his father, the god Poseidon. Wordsworth, in “The World Is Too Much with Us,” would rather be a pagan and “hear old Triton blow his wreathèd horn.” Miss MacIntosh would have none of it.
  • “the dark Satanic mills”: William Blake’s “Jerusalem” (1804).

Chapter 10

  • “some other bride blushing like a red, red rose”: Robert Burns, “O my Luve’s like a red, red rose” (1794).
  • “Strike up the music! On with the dance”: Byron’s “On with the dance! let joy be unconfined,” from the ball on the eve of Waterloo (Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage, Canto III).

Chapter 11

  • “Rameses I, II, III”: The royal mummies were found in 1881 in a hidden tomb at Deir el-Bahri, where priests had moved and rewrapped them, some in other kings’ coffins. Shelley’s “Ozymandias” (1818) takes its name from the Greek form of Ramesses II’s throne name, User-maat-re.

Chapter 22

  • “the sea is full of wrinkles, though it has neither age nor grey hairs”: “Time writes no wrinkle on thine azure brow: / Such as creation’s dawn beheld, thou rollest now” (Byron, Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage IV, 1818).

Chapter 25

  • “the only music worth hearing was the music he had never heard”: “Heard melodies are sweet, but those unheard / Are sweeter” (Keats, “Ode on a Grecian Urn,” 1819).

Chapter 26

  • “the windy garden where no birds sang”: “The sedge has withered from the lake, / And no birds sing” (Keats, “La Belle Dame sans Merci,” 1819).

Chapter 27

  • “when not one wind would blow through the sails of a ship stuck at the far horizon”: “As idle as a painted ship / Upon a painted ocean” (Coleridge, “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner,” 1798).

Chapter 28

  • “more precious than the cuckoo singing as it flies”: Wordsworth’s “To the Cuckoo” (1807): “No bird, but an invisible thing, / A voice, a mystery.”

Chapter 29

  • “one rose evoke a garden, one sea shell evoke a sea”: “To see a World in a Grain of Sand / And a Heaven in a Wild Flower” (Blake, “Auguries of Innocence,” c. 1803).

Chapter 46

  • “...Ah, bright star of my love, find your way to the lightless star. Find your way to one who has neither wings nor light. For I am dying now… and my love goes down.” - may refer to a poem by John Keats called Bright Star. This passage metaphorically captures the painful awareness of dying while longing for the beloved who is no longer fully present or alive, and the hope that love might find a path even through darkness and loss.

Chapter 68

  • “Water, water everywhere, and not a drop to drink” is a line from the poem by Samuel Taylor Coleridge, The Rime of the Ancient Mariner.

Chapter 71

  • Keats’ Ode to the Grecian Urn was published in 1819. Ode on a Grecian Urn discusses art and art's audience.
