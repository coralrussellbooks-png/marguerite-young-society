Woman in Young's Magnum Opus

Chapter 67

← Chapter 66 · Woman in Young's Magnum Opus · Chapter 68 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Homer Capehorn · Madge Capehorn · Moses Hunnecker · Vera Cartwheel

The bus returns in this chapter, and the interrupted birth returns with it. Moses Hunnecker curses at the wheel and harangues "old Doc," boasting that he is "increasing the population of this town by three" where the doctor's phantom deliveries produce nothing, and the boast measures male reproductive vanity against the empty pregnancy practice the book has run since Chapter 5, birth that will not complete carried again by men. The rabbit tests run for the doctor's girlfriends, all of them "dry as empty pea pods," the wombs that "never burst," are the interrupted birth in a lower register, and the undertaker Vera thinks of as the "best doctor" for a corpse folds birth and death together the way the whole book folds them, the cradle and the grave tended by the same failed hands.

Madge Capehorn laughs her "high, thin, bloodless" laugh at all this male misery, the pregnant woman accustomed to watching men generate chaos around her, and Homer beside her dreams "of a bride his wife might never know," a man idealizing a woman at the expense of the pregnant one present. The gap between his dream and her body is the book's real and dream as one country running through a marriage, his fantasy and her flesh occupying the same seat and never meeting.

Madge gives the chapter its sharpest image, the "crazy house" entered for a dime and never left, its walls all glass and mirror so that "you did not know how to get out, which mirror was a door." The hall of mirrors is the self as mask multiplied until no exit can be told from a reflection, the illusion of the individual turned into a trap a woman cannot walk out of, the going "head first down a clothes shute like a waterfall" the only motion the maze allows. And "Jackie," who Homer says "found the door," has tuberculosis, so that even the woman who finds the way out pays for it with her body, the escape and its cost arriving together, the chapter closing on a door that opens only onto illness.
