Movement VI. The Crossing Point: What One Generation Spends

Part of Exploring Young's Poetry, a reading of The Collected Poems of Marguerite Young (2022).
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Movement V. The Many Selves: The Self That Will Not Stay One · Exploring Young's Poetry · Movement VII. Naming: The Made Word →

Pillar: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point · Across every book

Every person stands at a seam in time where the dead hand the world forward to the unborn, and every choice packs the ground the next ones will walk on. Like the Dubious Ground philosophy, this principle is a later arrival, and it takes the war years to bring it fully out. The war poems name the crime without flinching, "Didactic Poem Against War" reporting that "this century has willed / Men like walrus herds shall all be killed," "The Clinic" turning to the state killing its own, "The Responsibility of Parentage" to what is handed down to a child. Beside them stands the Middle Years' "Guide to New Harmony, Indiana," a walk through a real ruined utopia where "Only the dearly dead remain / To populate a little town," The Past Doesn't Go Away made local and literal. In this vision the one unforgivable move is to trade the future for a present advantage, to cash in the next generation's ground for this generation's gain. This is the movement that reaches hardest toward Harp Song for a Radical, where the same accounting is kept across the whole of one working life, and toward Angel in the Forest, whose whole subject is the New Harmony that "Guide" tours in miniature.

"Archer in a World."

The landscape is terminal, "bare and cold," with "falling hawks" and "withered corn," an earth that has already had its end. The poem stands at the far edge of the Crossing Point, showing the future once it has been spent. This is the ground left for no one, the warning the whole movement turns on.

"Currin Valley."

In "rock-bitten lands" people labor "year after year" for "empty hands," endurance pressed into a merciless valley. The Past Doesn't Go Away is literal here: generations packed into hard ground that gives nothing back. The poem holds the long cost the land keeps of the people who work it.

"The Responsibility of Parentage."

"Being being," and a "mattress-striped whore" name existence at its most exposed, the poem weighing what one life hands to the next. The Crossing Point is in the title: parentage is the moment the world gets passed forward, for better or for ruin. What we bequeath is the whole question.

"Didactic Poem Against War."

"In pathos of distance, this century has willed / Men like walrus herds shall all be killed," the killing was named without euphemism. War is read as a built arrangement that spends the next generation for a present advantage, the one unforgivable move. This is the poem that reaches hardest toward Harp Song for a Radical, where Eugene Debs went to prison rather than sign his name to that spending.

"The Clinic."

A six-year-old prisoner faces death in "an airless room" behind glass, watched by cold observers, the state killing the most defenceless in its care. The poem indicts the arrangement that calls this justice. The Crossing Point condemns any order that balances its books with a child's life.

"The Long War."

The sweet pea "does not close its fragile heart again," and "invader space" is a city besieged, small and delicate, bearing the weight of history's violence. The poem measures war by what it does to the vulnerable rather than the powerful. The Past Doesn't Go Away keeps the record of who paid.

"The Trains."

"Glassy sparrows" wheel in "polar winds" over a "steel season," a chilling machinery of transit and disappearance. In the war years these trains carry more than the poem says aloud, the movement of the doomed through history's cold. It sits between grief and the Crossing Point, the loss and the machine that causes it in one image.

"Summer Day."

A "star wanderer" strikes the sun, and the earth forms and takes its orbit, the white heron moving along a pattern set in deep time. The poem reaches back to the world's making, The Past Doesn't Go Away at cosmic scale. It also touches the sacred ground of Movement I, creation held as both ancient and holy.

"Legend in Grass: Of Innocence and Evil."

A "drunken general" leers "vastly underground / As if he were unburied still," the war-dead refusing to stay buried under the field. The Past Doesn't Go Away made ghostly: the past is not behind the present but beneath it, pressing up. History's violence keeps surfacing in the grass that grows over it.

"Guide to New Harmony, Indiana"

A tour of the ruined utopia: the ferry over the Wabash, "a Rappite door," "Gabriel's footprint in blue chalk," the arch-angel "Father / Rapp saw sudden, terrible and bright," the Owenite and the Duke of Saxe-Weimar, and then "Only the dearly dead remain / To populate a little town." This is The Past Doesn't Go Away made local and literal, a built paradise sunk into Indiana ground until only the dead hold the town, and no arrangement that once seemed destined survived. It is the single poem that reaches most directly into Angel in the Forest, whose entire subject is this same New Harmony, the two utopias tried and lost on one patch of earth.
