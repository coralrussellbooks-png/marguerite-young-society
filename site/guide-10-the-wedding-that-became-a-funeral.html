Chapter 10: The Wedding That Became a Funeral

On Chapter 10 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of A Guide to Reading Miss MacIntosh, My Darling.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 29, 2026

← Chapter 9 · Guide Home · Chapter 11 →

Research notes: Chapter 10

Characters: Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Solomon the Turtle · Vera Cartwheel

Themes: Dreams and Imagination · Women, Gender, and Marriage

Symbols: Weddings and Funerals

Philosophy: Augustine and Christian Theology · Marguerite Young on Her Craft

Allusions: Astronomy · Genesis · Milton · The Bible · The Book of Job

In Chapter 9, Vera danced alone at her birthday party and declared she was dancing with Death. In Chapter 10, she falls asleep thinking about marriage and has one of the strangest, funniest, and most quietly heartbreaking dreams in the novel. Young does not always announce when the narrative shifts from waking to dreaming. You may not realize at first that the wedding ceremony Vera is witnessing is a dream. That is entirely intentional. The dream and the waking world use the same prose, the same density of attention, the same quality of presence. Learning to move between them without a handrail is part of reading this book.

What actually happens in Chapter 10

Vera, still awake after the birthday, is thinking about what Miss MacIntosh said: that you don’t truly know the person you marry until after the ceremony, when it is too late. She is thinking about marriage in general, what it is, what it requires, who she might someday know.

And then she is in a dream.

The dream is a wedding. Mr. Spitzer is there, and Miss MacIntosh is there, and a preacher is presiding. But the roles have been inverted: Mr. Spitzer is the bride, in a veil, and Miss MacIntosh is the bridegroom. The preacher’s words begin as wedding vows from the old Sarum rite an ancient medieval English ceremony and then slide, almost without notice, into a funeral oration. The wedding is becoming a funeral. The vows are becoming last rites. The guests are few and vague and the preacher seems to be dissolving as he speaks.

Biblical figures appear in the preacher’s litany: Gorgons and Hydras and Chimeras, Lucifer who was brightest and fell farthest, Beelzebub, the Day of Judgment. The cosmic and the domestic are sitting in the same room, which is what Young’s dreams always do.

Vera wakes or surfaces from the dream feeling not amused but troubled. She has been thinking unkindly of Miss MacIntosh in the dream, seeing her as colorless, as someone “none would have.” And now, awake, she feels the pity of it: this woman who has given her whole life to practicality and discipline and has never been loved the way she deserved, who has aged into a kind of invisibility that the world imposes on women who do not marry and do not perform the expected ceremonies.

Vera is hungry. She wants to go to Miss MacIntosh. She has been warned not to wander at night. She does not go.

She stays in her room, hungry in more ways than one, and the chapter ends.

The dream wedding, and why it matters

This dream is doing several things at once, and they are all worth naming.

First, it is doing something with gender. Mr. Spitzer as bride, Miss MacIntosh as bridegroom, the expected roles reversed. Young is not simply playing with visual incongruity. She is asking what it means for a ceremony to require a bride and a groom at all, for those roles to be fixed and assigned rather than chosen or earned. The inversion makes visible something that the normal ceremony hides: that the roles are arbitrary, that they could be otherwise, that we have agreed to pretend they are natural when they are not.

Second, it is doing something with ceremony itself. The wedding vows that slide into funeral liturgy are not an accident of Vera’s dreaming mind: they are a truth the dream is telling. In the era Young is writing about, marriage for women was frequently described as a kind of death: the woman’s legal identity was absorbed into her husband’s, her name changed, her previous self declared over. The Sarum rite, which is the source of many of the phrases in the preacher’s speech, was the medieval English marriage ceremony: old, authoritative, carrying the weight of centuries. Young puts it in a dream and lets it dissolve. She is not respectful of the ceremony’s authority. She is watching it come apart.

Third, and most quietly, the dream is about loneliness. Mr. Spitzer, who has spent his whole life mourning his dead twin brother, is here as a bride with no real groom. Miss MacIntosh, who has spent her whole life resisting the softness the world tried to take from her, is here as a bridegroom with no real bride. Two lonely people, dressed in each other’s costumes, presided over by a preacher who is dissolving. This is not comedy. This is sorrow wearing a costume.

Miss MacIntosh, seen from the outside

One of the most important things Chapter 10 does is show us Miss MacIntosh through Vera’s eyes at a moment when Vera is being uncharitable and then show us Vera catching herself.

In the dream and in the waking thoughts that follow it, Vera notices Miss MacIntosh’s ordinariness. Her colorlessness. The fact that she is aging, that “none would have her,” that she has resigned herself to a life without the love and partnership that the world holds up as the measure of a woman’s worth.

And then Vera stops herself. Because Miss MacIntosh did not fail to marry. She chose or was shaped by her life into choosing a different kind of existence. She gave Vera discipline and geography and the facts of the world. She swam in cold surf. She kept time with a silver dollar watch. She was the truest thing in the house. The world’s judgment of her, that she is a spinster, colorless, someone who never achieved the central ceremony, is not the same as the truth of her. Vera knows this. The guilt of having thought unkindly, even in a dream, is real.

This moment is a small lesson in how the novel works. Young will keep showing you characters who appear, from a certain angle, to have failed and then she will rotate the angle. She is always rotating the angle.

The preacher’s words

The preacher’s speech in this chapter is one of Young’s great set pieces of Biblical ventriloquism, she draws from Job, from Corinthians, from the Book of Common Prayer, from the Sarum rite, weaving them together into a ceremony that is simultaneously a wedding, a funeral, and a cosmic reckoning.

Some of the phrases worth holding:

“This life is very short and full of mortal sorrow” is from Job, the book of undeserved suffering, the book of questions that God does not answer.

“He cometh up, and he is cut down” is also from Job, about the brevity of human life, the flower that blooms and withers.

“Oh, death, where is thy sting? Oh, grave, where is thy victory?” is from Corinthians, the famous challenge to death, which in this context sounds less like triumph and more like a question asked into the dark.

“Ashes to ashes, dust to dust” is the burial rite, the reminder of what we are made of and what we will return to.

Young is not quoting these to comfort you. She is using them to show you how thoroughly the language of endings has been woven into the language of beginnings. The wedding ceremony and the funeral oration are made of the same words. They always have been. We just don’t usually notice.

Vera’s hunger

The chapter ends with Vera hungry. Literally, she has not eaten, it is late, she wants to go to Miss MacIntosh’s room. But the hunger is also something else: the want of connection, the wish to undo the unkind thoughts of the dream, the desire to say something true to this woman who has been trying to teach her truth all along. She does not go. She has been warned not to wander at night. She obeys, and she stays hungry.

This is a small, precisely calibrated moment of regret. The not-going. The obedience that costs something. In a novel where missed connections and delayed understandings accumulate like unpaid debts, this one of Vera lying in her room, wanting to knock on Miss MacIntosh’s door, choosing not to is worth noticing.

What to watch for as you keep reading

Light spoilers: This section hints at what happens later in the novel. Skip it if you want to meet the story fresh.

Mr. Spitzer as bride. The image of Spitzer in a veil is strange and funny and also heartbreaking when you understand him as a man who has spent his whole life in grief for his dead twin, who has never fully inhabited his own identity because so much of it died with his brother. Young dresses him as a bride in Vera’s dream, and the costume fits in its own terrible way. He is always waiting for a ceremony that already happened without him.

The Sarum rite. This was the medieval English marriage service used before the Reformation, the source of many of the phrases we still recognize in wedding ceremonies today as including “to have and to hold,” “till death do us part,” and the question of whether anyone knows of an impediment. Young reaches back to the oldest layer of the ceremony because she is interested in what marriage has always been, beneath the layers of custom and sentiment.

“Familiarity breeds contempt.” This proverb, attributed to Chaucer and Augustine and others, appears in this chapter in the context of Miss MacIntosh’s observation that you don’t know your spouse until it’s too late. Young is building a sustained meditation on intimacy and knowledge, the paradox that the closer you get to someone, the more clearly you see what you didn’t know, and the less romantic the seeing becomes.

Solomon the turtle, still there. He is present in the dream too, slow and ancient, witnessing everything. He does not judge. He does not hurry. He will be there when the ceremony is over.

What to hold onto

Chapter 10 is a dream chapter which means it operates by association rather than by logic, and it is asking you to feel your way through it rather than parse it.

Here is what to hold: Vera is fourteen, she has just danced alone at her own birthday party, she is lying awake thinking about marriage and what it means and whether she will ever truly know the person she marries. She dreams a wedding that turns into a funeral. She wakes feeling she has been unkind to someone she loves. She is hungry and does not go to ask for food or comfort.

And outside, in the darkness, Miss MacIntosh is alone in her room, not knowing she was dreamed.
