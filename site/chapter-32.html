To All My Darlings

Chapter 32

On Chapter 32 of Marguerite Young’s novel Miss MacIntosh, My Darling (1965). Part of To All My Darlings.
By Coral Russell · Updated October 30, 2026

← Chapter 31 · Home · Chapter 33 →

Summary

Catherine remembers Cousin Hannah as a storm of a woman, a suffrage captain with a saber scar and burning eyes who rode through deserts and snowfields. She died not in battle but in bed, after years of frostbite and semi-invalidism, confessing that “the greatest battles were fought in the domestic wars by those who had never left home.” Catherine visited her deathbed only in a dream, and she knew the day Hannah died when a riderless white horse came through the window. Rumor said Hannah once left a suitor at the church door. At Catherine’s own wedding she beat the bridegroom with her white umbrella and tried to carry the bride away. Catherine had married a phantom whose face she never saw: “For weddings are funerals, and funerals are weddings.” She always wanted to die while travelling, for “Death is that which is caused by our partial knowledge.”

Key passages

From the research

  • The opening lines immediately establish Cousin Hannah as a dominant, unyielding figure: “Cousin Hannah, a lady who had forcefully expressed herself, a lady who had never drawn in the reins or paused to hear another person’s nugatory opinion…” This sets the stage for her powerful presence.
  • Her dedication is described in almost mythical terms: she “had fought against vanquished competitors when there were none except those of my mother’s drugged dreams… For this consanguineous relative had been interested in her overwhelming suffrage battles for woman on every front, her conquests which could never have been satisfied by temporary victories or the overthrow of man in this world.” This passage is crucial because it introduces her primary cause (woman’s suffrage) but simultaneously casts doubt on the reality of her battles, linking them to Catherine’s internal, dreamlike state. It also suggests her ambitions were absolute, perhaps even unattainable or self-defeating (“the ultimate victory would have been the ultimate defeat”).
  • The novel explicitly contrasts Cousin Hannah with Catherine: “Cousin Hannah, unlike my mother with her superfluity of bewildering pretenses… was not one to drag her standards in the dust… Cousin Hannah had preferred to take the offensive, to advance by open hostilities.” This highlights Cousin Hannah’s directness, unwavering principles, and aggressive approach, standing in stark opposition to the mother’s evasiveness, dissimulation, and passive nature. This contrast is fundamental to their dynamic throughout the chapter.
  • Vera questions if Cousin Hannah could be a “misogynist” or “woman hater” despite describing herself as a “high-souled lover of woman, whom she had come to rescue from slavery to man.” This paradox is explored through her opposition to traditional feminine “frivolities” like elaborate hats, silks, veils, and parasols.
  • A significant passage describes her urging women to adopt a more combative stance. She “had urged woman to use her parasol as her sword or her shield, her greatest weapon of offense or defense,” and “to take off her great hoop skirts flounced with ribbons and lace, to show her little bird feet, to fly her skirts as her banners in her war against tyrannical man.” This passage vividly illustrates her radical vision of female empowerment, demanding a rejection of conventional femininity and an adoption of martial imagery and action.
  • Catherine’s perception of Cousin Hannah is key: “My mother believed that Cousin Hannah had been the image of that past which never was, that future which might never be realized. She had been extemporal, sudden and abrupt… brief and meteoric, hastening through this life as if it were a journey one should make with the utmost quickness and dispatch.” This portrays Cousin Hannah as existing outside normal time and reality, a fleeting, powerful force that appears and disappears unpredictably, emphasizing her almost mythical status in Catherine’s mind.
  • Her association with storms and cold further emphasizes her disruptive power: “She was certainly the greatest courier of every storm… The barometer dropped when she came… colder than a sea of the icebergs.” Her presence is linked to dramatic, chilling environmental changes, underscoring the profound, unsettling impact she had on Catherine’s world.
  • The description of Cousin Hannah’s death is perhaps the most important passage, as it drastically re-frames her life. My mother believed “that Cousin Hannah’s should have been a hero’s death.” But “she had died in no foreign war… She had suffered for years from frostbite. She had died after many years of semi-invalidism… The greatest battles were fought in the domestic wars by those who had never left home… It was always an unequal battle, for woman reigned supreme.” This passage subverts the heroic narrative built throughout the chapter. The mighty warrior dies quietly after years of decline, revealing a profound shift in her understanding of life’s true conflicts. The declaration that domestic wars are the greatest and that woman reigns supreme within them is a startling conclusion from the champion of public, external battles.
  • The possibility that Catherine’s visit to the dying Cousin Hannah occurred only in a dream, or even after death, is stated explicitly: “my mother had visited her only in a dream when she was riding in her carriage driven by the dead black coachman… Perhaps a visit was all the reality there was… One was dead, and one was dying… He certainly hoped that my mother’s visit had not occurred after she was dead.” This passage dissolves the boundary between reality and Catherine’s internal landscape, suggesting that the most significant interactions, including the deathbed confession, may have occurred entirely within her mind or dreams, reinforcing the subjective and possibly unreliable nature of the narrative.
  • The chaotic wedding scene where Cousin Hannah intervenes is highly symbolic: “Cousin Hannah, beating with her white umbrella upon the bridegroom, screaming like ten thousand demons in the cold midnight air, had tried to cause havoc, a riot… She had tried to intrude, to carry my mother away as the robber bridegroom carries the swooning bride.” This passage portrays the wedding as a battleground, where Cousin Hannah attempts to rescue (or abduct) Catherine from a phantom marriage, using her signature white umbrella as a weapon. It encapsulates her lifelong fight against Catherine’s entanglement with (or illusion of) men and traditional roles.

Questions and observations

Reading closely

  • “Perhaps, in fact, the ultimate victory would have been the ultimate defeat.” What would Cousin Hannah have lost by winning?
  • Catherine visited Hannah “only in a dream.” Why does Mr. Spitzer hope the visit “had not occurred after she was dead”?
  • “For weddings are funerals, and funerals are weddings.” What dies at a wedding?

Characters

  • Hannah confesses that “the greatest battles were fought in the domestic wars by those who had never left home.” Is she renouncing her life or seeing it clearly?
  • Catherine says she married “merely in order to proclaim her independence, to defy Cousin Hannah.” Can a marriage be an act of independence?
  • Hannah left her suitor “at the church door with a white bouquet in his hands,” and afterward he “subscribed heavily to the suffrage movement.” What did he understand about her?

Connections

  • Hannah urges women “to fly her skirts on tallest mountain peaks.” How does this echo the lost skirt of Chapters 30 and 31?
  • Catherine’s husband is “that great traveller who never would return,” and Catherine wants “to die while travelling.” How does this connect to Vera’s bus journey in Chapter 1?
  • Mr. Spitzer’s epitaph in Chapter 31 says Hannah was “not fallen in a foreign field.” Here she dies “while sleeping, perhaps while dreaming.” How do the two chapters tell her death?

Reflection

  • Are the hardest battles fought at home?
  • What do you know only in part?
  • Can you fight for someone who does not want to be rescued?

Research: Characters

Cousin Hannah

  • Cousin Hannah is portrayed as a powerful, uncompromising woman who never concedes or softens her standards, even for strategic reasons. She advances by open hostility and brandishes her symbolic sword like a cane, even when there is no opponent.
  • She is deeply committed to the cause of women’s suffrage and liberation from male oppression. Cousin Hannah fights against man as the cruelest oppressor of beauty and womanhood, advocating for women to use their own “weapons” such as parasols and skirts as symbols of resistance.
  • She is described as a swift, meteoric figure, always on the move and engaged in battles and journeys that span deserts, mountains, and foreign lands. She is likened to a great courier of storms and a weathervane signaling change.
  • Despite her vigor, Cousin Hannah is also seen as ancient, worn by time, and somewhat removed from the natural cycles of life and love. She is described as never having fully experienced youth, romance, or the mating seasons of life.
  • She dies not on a distant battlefield but in obscurity after years of frostbite and semi-invalidism. Her deathbed confession reveals a shift in belief that the greatest battles are fought domestically, in the struggles between man and woman at home.
  • She left her suitor at the church door with a white bouquet in his hands, and “it was said that after that he had subscribed heavily to the suffrage movement.”
  • She had “ravaged, burning eyes, a saber wound upon her cheek, a grizzled chin.”

Catherine (Vera’s mother)

  • Catherine is described as bedridden, veiled in white lace and surrounded by an atmosphere of drifting snowflakes, candle flames, and reflections. She is physically confined and vulnerable, a “creature of such fragility” who is easily overwhelmed by the presence of Cousin Hannah and the harshness of reality. Her bed is a “floating swan bed in a dazzling cloud,” which evokes both fragility and an ethereal, almost otherworldly quality. This imagery underscores her physical and emotional weakness.
  • Catherine’s essential loneliness and isolation is emphasized. She is abandoned by the “mysterious bridegroom” and all her loves are imaginary: pharaohs, captains, and other ethereal figures who “had left her, and they had never been.” This abandonment extends to emotional desolation, as no one, “not even Cousin Hannah,” has ever truly touched her “cold heart,” which is “cold and hard as the mountain rock.” This coldness suggests an emotional deadness or protective numbness born from pain.
  • Catherine lives largely in a world of dreams, illusions, and shifting realities. She imagines weddings to phantoms, sailboats, stars, and icebergs, reflecting a detachment from conventional life and relationships. Her marriage is portrayed as an ambiguous event, possibly symbolic or imaginary, rather than a concrete historical fact. The wedding imagery (“blushing behind her billowing wedding veils,” “married to a phantom”) conveys a sense of being caught between life and death, reality and fantasy.
  • Catherine’s mental state is described as “changeful and capricious,” with a wandering attention and vague memories. She is inconsistent in her accounts and perceptions, which may be interpreted as a symptom of her illness, age, or psychological state. This instability contrasts strongly with Cousin Hannah’s forceful and direct manner, highlighting Catherine’s passivity and internal turmoil.
  • Catherine’s relationship with Cousin Hannah reveals much about her character. Hannah is vigorous, militant, and confrontational, while Catherine simulates emotions she does not feel and “dissemble her feelings” when Hannah visits. Catherine’s indifference masks inner panic and vulnerability. Whereas Hannah is a symbol of active struggle and outward battle, Catherine is an embodiment of internal retreat and quiet suffering. Yet, Catherine admires Hannah’s energy and persistence, even as she is overshadowed by it.
  • Catherine is associated with death-like sleep and a liminal state between life and death. The novel suggests that she is “already dead” in a metaphorical sense, living in a “pretense of death.” Her life is marked by a profound stasis, and she may be seen as a “sleeping lady” awaiting awakening or final rest. The imagery of snow, cold, and fading light reinforces this theme of suspended animation and the passage between worlds.
  • Catherine’s marriage and attitude toward men are ambiguous. She married “that great traveller who never would return,” possibly to defy Cousin Hannah’s militant feminism or to assert independence, but the marriage was doomed. Her symbolic wedding to various natural and mystical entities rather than a man suggests rejection or transcendence of traditional romantic or marital relationships.
  • Catherine is depicted with a strong aesthetic sensibility: her fingers wear jewels that “gleaming like northern lights,” and her surroundings are rich in poetic symbolism (flutes, harps, snowflakes, candle flames). She is a “beautiful femme fatale,” but her beauty is cold and distant, more a vision than a presence. This artistic and ethereal quality adds to her complexity as a character who inhabits a realm between reality and dream.

Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer serves as the “only go-between” between Cousin Hannah and Catherine, facilitating communication during times when direct contact was difficult or impossible. This intermediary role is underscored by his musical whispering (“musically whispering as he had thought what to say, what not to say”) which suggests a careful, delicate handling of sensitive matters and emotions. His interactions hint at an awareness of the emotional and existential weight carried by these women, especially Cousin Hannah nearing the end of her life.
  • Mr. Spitzer appears as a figure who exists somewhat on the threshold between life and death, reality and dream. The chapter notes that Catherine had visited Cousin Hannah only in a dream, riding a carriage driven by a “dead black coachman,” evoking an otherworldly atmosphere. Mr. Spitzer’s presence in this liminal space, with his dark cape and snow “like ermine upon his collar,” symbolizes a mediator between the living and the fading, between the tangible and the ephemeral. This imagery suggests that Mr. Spitzer is linked to the inevitable passage of time and mortality that frames the chapter.
  • There is a tone of respect and perhaps even reverence in Mr. Spitzer’s attitude toward Cousin Hannah. He “certainly hoped that my mother’s visit had not occurred after she was dead,” indicating his sensitivity to timing and propriety in moments of transition between life and death. His role is not intrusive but rather one of discreet support and careful facilitation.
  • While Cousin Hannah and Catherine are enmeshed in their struggles (whether political, personal, or metaphysical) Mr. Spitzer’s character represents a connection to the external, practical world. His visits to the mansion, his knocking and waiting in the vestibule, contrast with the dreamlike, shifting realities inhabited by the women. This contrast shows him as a bridge between the ordinary and the extraordinary, the real and the imagined.
  • He questions the visit “vaguely as if he were not himself” and wonders whether “a visit was all the reality there was.”

Jock Cartwheel

  • Catherine “had married that great traveller who never would return.”
  • She “had never seen his face.”

The Black Coachman

  • On Catherine’s dream visit to Cousin Hannah, he is “the dead black coachman driving the black horses who wore black plumes and knew the way.”

Research: Themes

  • The novel explores the feminist theme through Cousin Hannah’s militant suffrage activism and her advocacy for women to wield traditional symbols of femininity as weapons in their fight for freedom.
  • Catherine’s life is marked by dreams, illusions, and a sense of unreality, contrasted with Cousin Hannah’s dramatic, tangible battles. The blurred line between dream and reality is emphasized by descriptions of ghostly visits, fading cities, and spectral imagery.
  • Both women embody different aspects of solitude: Cousin Hannah through her relentless battles and eventual physical decline, and Catherine through her emotional coldness and unfulfilled romantic desires.
  • The imagery that weddings are funerals and funerals are weddings suggests a cyclical transformation between life and death, union and separation. Catherine’s ambiguous marriage to a phantom reflects this theme.
  • Cousin Hannah is seen as ancient and time-worn, while Catherine’s memory of her is vague and fleeting. The passage of time is also symbolically represented by the changing weather, candle flames, and shifting landscapes.
  • The confession that the greatest battles are fought at home between man and woman reframes the traditional idea of heroism and war, placing emphasis on personal and domestic struggles rather than grand military conquests.
  • Swords, battles, sieges, and military metaphors are used extensively to describe Cousin Hannah’s activism and life philosophy.
  • Snowflakes, winter winds, desert storms, and mating birds symbolize the passage of time, change, and emotional states.
  • Candle flames, reflections in mirrors, and the interplay of light and dark underscore the theme of illusion and the duality of the characters.
  • Journeys to foreign lands, the Pony Express metaphor, and references to faraway cities emphasize Cousin Hannah’s restless spirit and Catherine’s longing for escape or transformation.
  • Catherine’s wedding veils, billowing skirts, and parasols symbolize both concealment and empowerment, linking femininity with both fragility and strength.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Cousin Hannah as a Warrior and Adventurer

See: Women’s Suffrage

  • Cousin Hannah is depicted as a forceful, uncompromising woman, a “great captain” who went “brandishing her sword like a cane even when there was no one” and preferred “the strident clash of arms, the pitched battle against man.”
  • Her sword symbolizes her militant feminism and unyielding fight against patriarchal oppression. The sword, usually a weapon of war and honor, here is also described metaphorically as a cane, suggesting both her authority and perhaps a certain defiance despite solitude. The image of her as a “veteran of so many winter campaigns, desert marches through burning sands” evokes the harshness of her battles, both literal and metaphorical.

Winter, Snow, and Cold Imagery

See: Snow, Ice, and Winter

  • The persistent references to coldness, snow, frostbite, and icicles create a chilling atmosphere that underlines both Cousin Hannah’s harshness and isolation and the emotional coldness surrounding the characters. For example, the barometer dropping “colder than a sea of the icebergs,” and the “great icicles hanging from the ceiling” on the day she died symbolize the emotional frost and the chilling impact she has on Catherine.
  • Snow and ice also symbolize purity, whiteness, and unattainable beauty, reflecting Cousin Hannah’s idealized vision of womanhood as “unsullied honor” and beauty “as white as virginal snow.”

The Riderless White Horse and Rider Imagery

See: Horses and Carriages

  • The image of a riderless white horse “white as snow” coming through the window, bringing snow and cold with it, is a powerful symbol of death or the passing of Cousin Hannah.
  • The white horse traditionally symbolizes a harbinger of death or a psychopomp figure in literature, and here it represents the transition from life to death for this warrior woman. Its entrance is supernatural, blending reality and dream, reinforcing the theme of liminality between life and death.

The Veils, Parasols, and Skirts as Symbols of Femininity and Defense

See: The Umbrella · Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

  • The chapter richly describes feminine accouterments like “great garden hats decked with billowing plumes,” “secretive veils,” “frilled parasols,” and “great hoop skirts flounced with ribbons and lace.” These symbolize traditional femininity, societal expectations, and also the subtle weapons and shields women use in social and gender conflicts.
  • Cousin Hannah’s urging for women to wield their parasol “as her sword or her shield” and to “fly her skirts as her banners in her war against tyrannical man” transforms these delicate objects into instruments of rebellion. The parasol becomes a metaphor for female agency in a world dominated by men.

The Journey and the Northwest Passage

See: The Bus and the Journey

  • Cousin Hannah’s life is described in terms of vast, almost mythical journeys through “desert cities,” “impregnable Asiatic fortresses,” “the great Wall of China,” “Manitoba, Hudson’s Bay,” and “ten thousand singing islands of snow and ice.” This geographical imagery symbolizes her relentless quest for freedom and truth, as well as the impossibility of fully conquering the patriarchal world.
  • The reference to the “Northwest Passage” metaphorically represents the ultimate, elusive goal of life or freedom or “that which cannot be found.” It connotes a spiritual or existential journey, echoing themes of quest and discovery.

The Contrast Between Cousin Hannah and Catherine

  • Catherine is portrayed as fragile, veiled in lace and illusions, “bed-ridden,” “cold heart, cold and hard as the mountain rock,” and largely passive. In contrast, Cousin Hannah is active, combative, and direct. This contrast is symbolized by Catherine’s white umbrella and lace, symbols of delicacy and retreat, versus Cousin Hannah “beating with her white umbrella upon the bridegroom” like a weapon.
  • Catherine’s world is one of dreams, illusions, and absence, while Cousin Hannah’s is one of harsh realities and confrontations.

Death as a Wedding and Wedding as Death

See: Weddings and Funerals

  • The narrative blurs the boundaries between life milestones and death: “weddings are funerals, and funerals are weddings.” This imagery underscores the theme of transformation and loss inherent in marriage and life.
  • Catherine’s vague, elusive descriptions of her marriage (to a phantom, a porpoise, a star, an iceberg) symbolize the illusory or absent nature of her union and love. Marriage here is a symbolic death of the self or an eternal separation, while death is the final union.

The Ethereal and Dreamlike Atmosphere

See: Illusion and Reality · Dreams and Imagination

  • The chapter often blurs reality and dream: visits occur “only in a dream,” and there are “ambient ghosts of all dead loves.”
  • The use of mirrors, candle flames, flickering stars, and shifting reflections invokes a dreamscape where time and space are fluid. This imagery conveys the fragile mental and emotional state of Catherine and the elusive, almost mythical nature of Cousin Hannah.

The Military and Siege Imagery

  • Cousin Hannah’s life is described metaphorically as a “long siege” with strategic withdrawals and renewed assaults. This symbolizes her relentless struggle for women’s rights and independence, never defeated but always adapting.
  • The castle symbolizes societal structures opposed to her, and the sea eroding the shore suggests slow but inevitable change wrought by persistent effort.

The Symbolism of Light and Darkness

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light

  • Cousin Hannah is described as “standing in a beam of sudden light,” a figure of revelation and defiance.
  • Yet the chapter also has Mr. Spitzer’s “dark cape,” the ladies’ “already shadowed snow-white faces,” and Catherine’s “clouded looks,” symbolizing mystery, obscurity, and the unknown. This interplay of light and dark reflects the duality of the characters and the ambiguous outcomes of Cousin Hannah’s battles.

Research: Resources

  • “to invite her enemy to a banquet, to ease his suspicions with goblets of wine”: Judith feasted with Holofernes until he lay drunk, then “smote twice upon his neck with all her might, and she took away his head from him” (Judith 13:8, Apocrypha, King James Version). See The Bible.
  • “the misogynist, the woman hater”: A misogynist is a person who dislikes, despises, or is strongly prejudiced against women. Female misogynists (internalized sexism/oppression) are characterized by exalting the feminine ideal, favoring traditional gender roles, looking down on women who are not feminine, self-loathing, and a belief that she is superior to other women or in constant competition with them. There is a three-tiered system for patriarchy. Patriarchy is the system of social organization that favors and empowers men. Sexism is the code of conduct or rules that are established for women. Misogyny is the policing or enforcement of these rules on women (Kate Manne, Down Girl: The Logic of Misogyny, Oxford University Press, 2017). Thus women can be misogynist if they are helping to police or enforce what women can and cannot do. Looking at all of this it can be determined that no, Cousin Hannah was not misogynist for wanting women to have independence or the right to vote. See Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “worse than enchantment by an Eastern drug or spiritous liquor or witches’ brew”: The Eastern drug is opium, Catherine’s own enchantment. The witches’ brew is the cauldron of Macbeth: “Double, double toil and trouble; / Fire burn and cauldron bubble” (4.1). See Opium and Shakespeare.
  • “to fly her skirts on tallest mountain peaks”: Young’s Alpine story of the two bloomer girls who shed their skirts on a summit, one lost going back for hers. See the Chapter 30 resources, the Chapter 31 resources, and Women’s Suffrage.
  • “should have been employed by the old Pony Express”: Relays of riders carried the mail between St. Joseph, Missouri, and Sacramento, California, from April 1860 until the telegraph replaced them in October 1861. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “She should have brought the message to Garcia”: A Message to Garcia is an essay by Elbert Hubbard about the value of individual initiative and conscientiousness in work. Published in 1899, it praised Lieutenant Andrew Rowan, who in 1898 carried President McKinley’s message to the Cuban general Calixto García. It invokes the ideal of dutiful action and responsibility amid the novel’s complex interplay of dreams, realities, and fragmented identities.
  • “colder than the Weddell Sea”: The Weddell Sea is part of the Southern Ocean off Antarctica. It is named for the sealer James Weddell, who sailed into it in 1823, and its pack ice crushed Shackleton’s Endurance in 1915. See Exploration and the Poles.
  • “colder than the lunar sea”: The dark plains of the moon are dry basalt. They were named seas, maria, by Giovanni Riccioli on his map of 1651. See Astronomy.
  • “Her face had not been attractive, certainly”: Miss MacIntosh and Cousin Hannah are both depicted as not attractive or feminine and virgins.
  • “as my mother could have endured no beautiful or potent rival”: In this chapter the rivalry mirrors Madge Capehorn at the beginning of the book because she could not tolerate Jacqueline White. See the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “be she even a dead bride with her face covered by a veil of snowflakes”: It recalls the lost companion of Chapter 31, “a body covered by the snow.” See the Chapter 31 resources.
  • “challenger of Foreign Legions”: The French Foreign Legion was founded in 1831 for foreign volunteers.
  • “as she had lived by the sword, should have died by one sharp blow”: “All they that take the sword shall perish with the sword” (Matthew 26:52). See The Bible.
  • “had slain minotaurs”: Theseus killed the Minotaur in the Labyrinth of Crete. Here Death, who “had overthrown great kings,” is given the hero’s deeds. See Greek Mythology.
  • “the black horses who wore black plumes”: Victorian hearses were drawn by black horses wearing tall plumes of black ostrich feathers. See The Black Coachman and Horses and Carriages.
  • “the walls might crumble at a lonely trumpet’s sound”: “When the people heard the sound of the trumpet, and the people shouted with a great shout, that the wall fell down flat” (Joshua 6:20). See The Bible.
  • “as Boston faded into fog and water from which it came”: Much of Boston stands on made land. The Back Bay was filled from tidal flats between 1857 and the 1880s.
  • “a riderless horse white as snow came through the window”: A riderless horse follows the coffin in state funerals. Lincoln’s horse, Old Bob, was led behind his hearse in Springfield in 1865, and Black Jack followed John F. Kennedy’s coffin in 1963. See Horses and Carriages.
  • “No one had awakened the sleeping lady to life and love”: This characterization of Catherine resembles the folktale of Sleeping Beauty which is a classic fairy tale about a princess who is cursed to sleep for a hundred years by an evil fairy, to be awakened by a handsome prince at the end of that time. The good fairy, realizing that the princess would be frightened if alone when she awakens, uses her wand to put every living person and animal in the palace asleep, to awaken when the princess does. Early origins and versions of the tale involve rape, cannibalism and Sleeping Beauty giving birth to children while she is asleep (similar to noncanonical books, as in the Gnostic Hypostasis of the Archons, in which the rulers of the world rape Eve). Catherine embodies the Sleeping Beauty archetype as a woman caught in a protracted, enchanted sleep between life and death, isolated in a dreamlike world filled with phantoms, awaiting an uncertain awakening. Her physical stillness, the canary of Chapter 2, the grand but empty house, and the thematic concerns with time, death, and unfulfilled love strongly evoke the folktale’s motifs and deepen the novel’s exploration of illusion versus reality. Through Catherine, Young reimagines the Sleeping Beauty myth, using it to probe themes of mortality, memory, and the elusive boundary between waking life and dreams. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
    • In Giambattista Basile’s “Sun, Moon, and Talia” (1634), a king rapes the sleeping Talia, she bears twins without waking, and his wife later orders the children cooked.
  • “the limelight of publicity which was so very different from the light of a halo”: Limelight, a block of lime heated white-hot in a gas flame, was developed by Thomas Drummond in the 1820s and lit theater stages from 1837.
  • “picketing in a cold, sleet rain in front of a skirt factory”: The Silent Sentinels of the National Woman’s Party picketed the White House in all weathers from January 1917, and many were jailed. In the 1912 Lawrence, Massachusetts, “Bread and Roses” strike, women mill workers marched through the New England winter. Charles Studin’s suffragist, Young’s source for Cousin Hannah, “chained herself to a lamppost in front of the White House” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Women’s Suffrage.
  • “challenging the conductor to a duel”: Studin’s suffragist “challenged a British prime minister to a duel and the prime minister refused to bear arms against the lady” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). See Cousin Hannah and Women’s Suffrage.
  • “these hollow men, these advocates of lost causes, these men of straw, these things of naught”: “But hollow men, like horses hot at hand, / Make gallant show and promise of their mettle” (Shakespeare, Julius Caesar 4.2). Matthew Arnold called Oxford the “home of lost causes” (Essays in Criticism, 1865), and “a thing of naught” is Flute’s phrase in A Midsummer Night’s Dream (4.2). See Shakespeare.
  • “less than poor street Arabs”: “Street Arab” was a Victorian term for homeless street children, likening them to wandering Bedouin.
  • “the valleys of dry bones”: “The valley which was full of bones… O ye dry bones, hear the word of the LORD” (Ezekiel 37:1, 4). See The Bible.
  • “the sleepy myrmidons who did my mother’s bidding”: The Myrmidons were the soldiers Achilles led to Troy in Homer’s Iliad. Ovid tells that Zeus made them from ants for King Aeacus (Metamorphoses 7). The word came to mean a follower who obeys without question. See Homer’s Epics and Greek Mythology.
  • “the centaurs laying great golden eggs like moons upon the grass”: It recalls Aesop’s fable “The Goose That Laid the Golden Eggs.” See Greek Mythology.
  • “Had she come from Outer Mongolia, the great Wall of China, the kingdom of Prester John”: Three of Cousin Hannah’s far places.
    • Outer Mongolia was a territory of the Qing dynasty. It declared independence in 1911 and was the Soviet-backed Mongolian People’s Republic from 1924.
    • The great Wall of China is a series of fortifications built across the northern borders of Imperial China. Most of the wall that stands today was built under the Ming dynasty (1368-1644).
    • Prester John was a legendary Christian patriarch, presbyter, and king. The legend began with a forged letter that circulated around 1165.
  • “Had she come from Manitoba, Hudson’s Bay, Cape Reprieve, the Laurentian Shield”: The Canadian Far North. See Exploration and the Poles.
    • Manitoba is a province in Canada.
    • Hudson’s Bay is a large body of saltwater in northeastern Canada. The Hudson’s Bay Company was chartered in 1670 to trade furs there.
    • The Laurentian Shield (Canadian Shield/Laurentian Plateau) is a large area of exposed Precambrian igneous and high-grade metamorphic rocks that forms the ancient geologic core of the North American continent.
  • “sapphires which turned to rubies, rubies which turned to sapphires”: Ruby and sapphire are the same mineral, corundum. A trace of chromium turns it red, and that red stone is ruby. See Pearls and Jewels.
  • “Had she found the Northwest Passage”: Northwest Passage is the sea route between the Atlantic and Pacific oceans through the Arctic Ocean. Sir John Franklin’s expedition was lost seeking it in 1845, and Roald Amundsen first sailed through it in the Gjøa, 1903-1906. See Exploration and the Poles.
    • Northwest Passage (1937) is a novel by Kenneth Roberts. “On every side of us are men who hunt perpetually for their personal northwest passage, too often sacrificing health, strength, and life itself to the search; and who shall say they are not happier in their vain but hopeful quest than wiser, duller folks who sit at home, venturing nothing and, with sour laughs, deriding the seekers for that fabled thoroughfare?” It functions as a rich symbol of adventurous exploration, loss, and the quest for elusive truths. It aligns with the thematic concerns of Roberts’ book but is integrated into the novel’s unique mystical and psychological landscape, emphasizing human vulnerability and the unknowability of fate.
  • “Did she bring a message from a great king to a great khan”: Kublai Khan sent the Polo brothers to the Pope with a letter asking for a hundred learned men, and they set out back to him in 1271 with the young Marco Polo, reaching his court about 1275.
  • “the seed-times and the golden seasons”: “While the earth remaineth, seedtime and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease” (Genesis 8:22). See Genesis.
  • “the stately mating dance of the whooping cranes”: Whooping cranes court with leaps, bows, and wing-flapping. By 1941 only about fifteen wild birds remained. See Birds.
  • “She had left him at the church door with a white bouquet in his hands”: Young told Studs Terkel that when Cousin Hannah “abandons the bridegroom at the church door,” “she goes to a numerologist who advises her as to how she should spell her name in the future and adds the hyphen just at the time when she dramatically becomes a hyphenated personality” (Marguerite Young, interview with Studs Terkel, WFMT radio, 1965). The hyphen is the one in Freemount-Snowden. See Cousin Hannah and Women, Gender, and Marriage.
  • “She had been the veteran of so many winter campaigns”: It recalls the painting Liberty Leading the People (Eugène Delacroix, 1830). This phrase captures the essence of Cousin Hannah’s life as one of heroic endurance, battling through the metaphorical winters of life and fate, and serves to underline her strength and the epic scale of her personal journey.
  • “like that faint heart which never won fair lady”: An old proverb, which Gilbert and Sullivan made a song, “Faint heart never won fair lady,” in Iolanthe (1882).
  • “she had been married on the tennis court”: Tennis and archery were among the first sports open to women. Charlotte Cooper won the first women’s Olympic title, in tennis, at Paris in 1900.
  • “as the robber bridegroom carries the swooning bride”: In the Grimms’ “The Robber Bridegroom” (1812), a bride discovers that her bridegroom murders young women. Eudora Welty retold it as a novel, The Robber Bridegroom (1942). See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “weird huntsmen and barking dogs”: The Wild Hunt of European folklore, a ghostly troop of huntsmen and hounds riding the storm at night. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “her wedding gown had been torn to shreds by brambles”: In the Grimms’ “Little Briar Rose,” a hedge of thorns grows around the sleeping castle, and princes who try to pass die caught in it. See Fairy Tales and Folklore.
  • “For weddings are funerals”: In this chapter the idea of weddings and funerals mirrors Madge Capehorn at the beginning of the book because of her situation. In Chapter 6 “she had no bridal trousseau, no negligee ribbons, no clothes to come to child-bed with, no dance programs, no suitors, no husband.” See Weddings and Funerals and the Chapter 6 resources.
  • “this uninvited wedding guest”: In the Grimms’ “Little Briar Rose” (1812), the thirteenth wise woman, not invited to the feast, curses the child. Coleridge’s Ancient Mariner also stops a Wedding-Guest at the door. See Fairy Tales and Folklore and Romantic Poets.
  • “that pale cavalier who, with disheveled locks, with foaming mouth, is driven by the mare”: In Plato’s Phaedrus the soul is a charioteer with two horses, one noble and one unruly, who must master the wild one with the bit. See Plato and Platonism.
  • “Death is that which is caused by our partial knowledge”: “For we know in part, and we prophesy in part. But when that which is perfect is come, then that which is in part shall be done away” (1 Corinthians 13:9-10). See The Bible, Ecclesiastes, and Death and Mortality.
    • Our incomplete understanding of life, death, and the universe can lead to poor decisions and actions that ultimately contribute to our own demise. It uses the narrator’s introspective and metaphysical musings to convey that death is not a simple event but a complex, multifaceted experience deeply connected to memory, identity, and the limits of human understanding. It portrays death as both an ending and a continuation, filled with ambiguities that challenge the very notions of self and reality.
    • “For in much wisdom is much grief: and he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow” (Ecclesiastes 1:18). The paradox of knowledge challenges the common belief that knowledge is always a positive thing. It acknowledges that wisdom, while valuable, might also bring greater awareness of the problems, and limitations of human existence. It serves to underscore the novel’s exploration of wisdom as a double-edged sword: enlightenment brings clarity but also increases one’s sensitivity to life’s tragedies and existential sorrows. It expresses the tragic insight that knowledge and sorrow are intimately linked, and that to be truly wise is to be deeply aware of life’s inherent griefs. The verse encapsulates a key philosophical reflection in the novel: that the pursuit of wisdom and knowledge, rather than providing comfort, often exposes one to the profound sorrows of human existence.
