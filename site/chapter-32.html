Summary

Catherine remembers Hannah as a storm of a woman with a saber scar and burning eyes, who tried to break up Catherine's wedding by beating the bridegroom with her white umbrella and carrying the bride away. Catherine's marriage itself is spectral: a faceless bridegroom, a wedding to a sailboat, a porpoise, a star, a church bell. Hannah died old and alone and never married, though Catherine believes she still visits. Catherine always wanted to die while traveling, believing that death comes from partial knowledge.

Key passages

From the research

  • The opening lines immediately establish Cousin Hannah as a dominant, unyielding figure: "Cousin Hannah , a lady who had forcefully expressed herself, a lady who had never drawn in the reins or paused to hear another person’s nugatory opinion...". This sets the stage for her powerful presence.
  • Her dedication is described in almost mythical terms: "...had fought against vanquished competitors when there were none except those of my mother’s drugged dreams... For this consanguineous relative had been interested in her overwhelming suffrage battles for woman on every front, her conquests which could never have been satisfied by temporary victories or the overthrow of man in this world." This passage is crucial because it introduces her primary cause (woman's suffrage) but simultaneously casts doubt on the reality of her battles, linking them to Catherine's internal, dreamlike state. It also suggests her ambitions were absolute, perhaps even unattainable or self-defeating ("the ultimate victory would have been the ultimate defeat").
  • The novel explicitly contrasts Cousin Hannah with Catherine: "Cousin Hannah, unlike my mother with her superfluity of bewildering pretenses... was not one to drag her standards in the dust... She was not one to invite her enemy to a banquet... Cousin Hannah had preferred to take the offensive, to advance by open hostilities...". This highlights Cousin Hannah's directness, unwavering principles, and aggressive approach, standing in stark opposition to the mother's evasiveness, dissimulation, and passive nature. This contrast is fundamental to their dynamic throughout the chapter.
  • Vera questions if Cousin Hannah could be a "misogynist" or "woman hater" despite describing herself as a "high-souled lover of woman, whom she had come to rescue from slavery to man". This paradox is explored through her opposition to traditional feminine "frivolities" like elaborate hats, silks, veils, and parasols.
  • A significant passage describes her urging women to adopt a more combative stance: "...had urged woman to use her parasol as her sword or her shield, her greatest weapon of offense or defense—had often urged woman to take off her great hoop skirts flounced with ribbons and lace, to show her little bird feet, to fly her skirts as her banners in her war against tyrannical man...". This passage vividly illustrates her radical vision of female empowerment, demanding a rejection of conventional femininity and an adoption of martial imagery and action.
  • Catherine's perception of Cousin Hannah is key: "My mother believed that Cousin Hannah had been the image of that past which never was, that future which might never be realized. She had been extemporal, sudden and abrupt... brief and meteoric, hastening through this life as if it were a journey one should make with the utmost quickness and dispatch...". This portrays Cousin Hannah as existing outside normal time and reality, a fleeting, powerful force that appears and disappears unpredictably, emphasizing her almost mythical status in Catherine's mind.
  • Her association with storms and cold further emphasizes her disruptive power: "She was certainly the greatest courier of every storm... The barometer dropped when she came, it was colder than a sea of the icebergs..." Her presence is linked to dramatic, chilling environmental changes, underscoring the profound, unsettling impact she had on Catherine's world.
  • The description of Cousin Hannah's death is perhaps the most important passage, as it drastically re-frames her life: My mother "still believed—that Cousin Hannah’s should have been a hero’s death... But she had died in no foreign war... She had suffered for years from frostbite. She had died after many years of semi-invalidism—in her old age confessing to my mother that she no longer believed in the glory of these foreign conquests—that these great battles had been as nothing to her... The greatest battles were fought in the domestic wars by those who had never left home... It was always an unequal battle, for woman reigned supreme." This passage subverts the heroic narrative built throughout the chapter. The mighty warrior dies quietly after years of decline, revealing a profound shift in her understanding of life's true conflicts. The declaration that domestic wars are the greatest and that woman reigns supreme within them is a startling conclusion from the champion of public, external battles.
  • The possibility that Catherine's visit to the dying Cousin Hannah occurred only in a dream, or even after death, is stated explicitly: "...my mother had visited her only in a dream when she was riding in her carriage driven by the dead black coachman... Mr. Spitzer asked vaguely... Perhaps a visit was all the reality there was... One was dead, and one was dying... He certainly hoped that my mother’s visit had not occurred after she was dead." This passage dissolves the boundary between reality and Catherine's internal landscape, suggesting that the most significant interactions, including the deathbed confession, may have occurred entirely within her mind or dreams, reinforcing the subjective and possibly unreliable nature of the narrative.
  • The chaotic wedding scene where Cousin Hannah intervenes is highly symbolic: "Cousin Hannah, beating with her white umbrella upon the bridegroom, screaming like ten thousand demons... had tried to cause havoc, a riot... She had tried to intrude, to carry my mother away as the robber bridegroom carries the swooning bride...". This passage portrays the wedding as a battleground, where Cousin Hannah attempts to rescue (or abduct) Catherine from a phantom marriage, using her signature white umbrella as a weapon. It encapsulates her lifelong fight against Catherine's entanglement with (or illusion of) men and traditional roles.

Questions and observations

Research: Characters

Cousin Hannah

  • Cousin Hannah is portrayed as a powerful, uncompromising woman who never concedes or softens her standards, even for strategic reasons. She advances by open hostility and brandishes her symbolic sword like a cane, even when there is no opponent.
  • She is deeply committed to the cause of women’s suffrage and liberation from male oppression. Cousin Hannah fights against man as the cruelest oppressor of beauty and womanhood, advocating for women to use their own "weapons" such as parasols and skirts as symbols of resistance.
  • She is described as a swift, meteoric figure, always on the move and engaged in battles and journeys that span deserts, mountains, and foreign lands. She is likened to a great courier of storms and a weathervane signaling change.
  • Despite her vigor, Cousin Hannah is also seen as ancient, worn by time, and somewhat removed from the natural cycles of life and love. She is described as never having fully experienced youth, romance, or the mating seasons of life.
  • She dies not on a distant battlefield but in obscurity after years of semi-invalidism caused by frostbite. Her deathbed confession reveals a shift in belief that the greatest battles are fought domestically, in the struggles between man and woman at home.

Catherine (Vera’s mother)

  • Catherine is described as bedridden, veiled in white lace and surrounded by an atmosphere of drifting snowflakes, candle flames, and reflections. She is physically confined and vulnerable, a "creature of such fragility" who is easily overwhelmed by the presence of Cousin Hannah and the harshness of reality. Her bed is a "floating swan bed in a dazzling cloud," which evokes both fragility and an ethereal, almost otherworldly quality. This imagery underscores her physical and emotional weakness.
  • Catherine’s essential loneliness and isolation is emphasized. She is abandoned by the "mysterious bridegroom” and all her loves are imaginary: pharaohs, captains, and other ethereal figures who "had left her, and they had never been". This abandonment extends to emotional desolation, as no one, "not even Cousin Hannah," has ever truly touched her "cold heart," which is "cold and hard as the mountain rock". This coldness suggests an emotional deadness or protective numbness born from pain.
  • Catherine lives largely in a world of dreams, illusions, and shifting realities. She imagines weddings to phantoms, sailboats, stars, and icebergs, reflecting a detachment from conventional life and relationships. Her marriage is portrayed as an ambiguous event, possibly symbolic or imaginary, rather than a concrete historical fact. The wedding imagery ("blushing behind her billowing wedding veils," "married to a phantom") conveys a sense of being caught between life and death, reality and fantasy.
  • Catherine's mental state is described as "changeful and capricious," with a wandering attention and vague memories. She is inconsistent in her accounts and perceptions, which may be interpreted as a symptom of her illness, age, or psychological state. This instability contrasts strongly with Cousin Hannah’s forceful and direct manner, highlighting Catherine’s passivity and internal turmoil.
  • Catherine’s relationship with Cousin Hannah reveals much about her character. Hannah is vigorous, militant, and confrontational, while Catherine simulates emotions she does not feel and "dissembles her feelings" when Hannah visits. Catherine’s indifference masks inner panic and vulnerability. Whereas Hannah is a symbol of active struggle and outward battle, Catherine is an embodiment of internal retreat and quiet suffering. Yet, Catherine admires Hannah’s energy and persistence, even as she is overshadowed by it.
  • Catherine is associated with death-like sleep and a liminal state between life and death. The novel suggests that she is "already dead" in a metaphorical sense, living in a "pretense of death". Her life is marked by a profound stasis, and she may be seen as a "sleeping lady" awaiting awakening or final rest. The imagery of snow, cold, and fading light reinforces this theme of suspended animation and the passage between worlds.
  • Catherine’s marriage and attitude toward men are ambiguous. She married "that great traveller who never would return," possibly to defy Cousin Hannah’s militant feminism or to assert independence, but the marriage was doomed. Her symbolic wedding to various natural and mystical entities rather than a man suggests rejection or transcendence of traditional romantic or marital relationships.
  • Catherine is depicted with a strong aesthetic sensibility: her fingers wear jewels that "gleam like northern lights," and her surroundings are rich in poetic symbolism (flutes, harps, snowflakes, candle flames). She is a "beautiful femme fatale," but her beauty is cold and distant, more a vision than a presence. This artistic and ethereal quality adds to her complexity as a character who inhabits a realm between reality and dream.

Mr. Spitzer

  • Mr. Spitzer serves as the "only go-between" between Cousin Hannah and Catherine, facilitating communication during times when direct contact was difficult or impossible. This intermediary role is underscored by his musical whispering ("musically whispering as he had thought what to say, what not to say") which suggests a careful, delicate handling of sensitive matters and emotions. His interactions hint at an awareness of the emotional and existential weight carried by these women, especially Cousin Hannah nearing the end of her life.
  • Mr. Spitzer appears as a figure who exists somewhat on the threshold between life and death, reality and dream. The chapter notes that Catherine had visited Cousin Hannah only in a dream, riding a carriage driven by a "dead black coachman," evoking an otherworldly atmosphere. Mr. Spitzer's presence in this liminal space, with his dark cape and snow "like ermine upon his collar," symbolizes a mediator between the living and the fading, between the tangible and the ephemeral. This imagery suggests that Mr. Spitzer is linked to the inevitable passage of time and mortality that frames the chapter.
  • There is a tone of respect and perhaps even reverence in Mr. Spitzer’s attitude toward Cousin Hannah. He "certainly hoped that my mother’s visit had not occurred after she was dead," indicating his sensitivity to timing and propriety in moments of transition between life and death. His role is not intrusive but rather one of discreet support and careful facilitation.
  • While Cousin Hannah and Catherine are enmeshed in their struggles (whether political, personal, or metaphysical) Mr. Spitzer’s character represents a connection to the external, practical world. His visits to the mansion, his knocking and waiting in the vestibule, contrast with the dreamlike, shifting realities inhabited by the women. This contrast shows him as a bridge between the ordinary and the extraordinary, the real and the imagined.

Research: Themes

  • The novel explores the feminist theme through Cousin Hannah’s militant suffrage activism and her advocacy for women to wield traditional symbols of femininity as weapons in their fight for freedom.
  • Catherine’s life is marked by dreams, illusions, and a sense of unreality, contrasted with Cousin Hannah’s dramatic, tangible battles. The blurred line between dream and reality is emphasized by descriptions of ghostly visits, fading cities, and spectral imagery.
  • Both women embody different aspects of solitude: Cousin Hannah through her relentless battles and eventual physical decline, and Catherine through her emotional coldness and unfulfilled romantic desires.
  • The imagery that weddings are funerals and funerals are weddings suggests a cyclical transformation between life and death, union and separation. Catherine’s ambiguous marriage to a phantom reflects this theme.
  • Cousin Hannah is seen as ancient and time-worn, while Catherine’s memory of her is vague and fleeting. The passage of time is also symbolically represented by the changing weather, candle flames, and shifting landscapes.
  • The confession that the greatest battles are fought at home between man and woman reframes the traditional idea of heroism and war, placing emphasis on personal and domestic struggles rather than grand military conquests.
  • Swords, battles, sieges, and military metaphors are used extensively to describe Cousin Hannah’s activism and life philosophy.
  • Snowflakes, winter winds, desert storms, and migrating birds symbolize the passage of time, change, and emotional states.
  • Candle flames, reflections in mirrors, and the interplay of light and dark underscore the theme of illusion and the duality of the characters.
  • Journeys to foreign lands, the Pony Express metaphor, and references to faraway cities emphasize Cousin Hannah’s restless spirit and Catherine’s longing for escape or transformation.
  • Catherine’s wedding veils, billowing skirts, and parasols symbolize both concealment and empowerment, linking femininity with both fragility and strength.

Research: Symbols and Imagery

Cousin Hannah as a Warrior and Adventurer

  • Cousin Hannah is depicted as a forceful, uncompromising woman, a "great captain" who "brandishing her sword like a cane even when there was no one" and preferred "the strident clash of arms, the pitched battle against man".
  • Her sword symbolizes her militant feminism and unyielding fight against patriarchal oppression. The sword, usually a weapon of war and honor, here is also described metaphorically as a cane, suggesting both her authority and perhaps a certain defiance despite solitude. The image of her as a “veteran of so many winter campaigns, desert marches through burning sands” evokes the harshness of her battles, both literal and metaphorical.

Winter, Snow, and Cold Imagery

See: Snow, Ice, and Winter

  • The persistent references to coldness, snow, frostbite, and icicles create a chilling atmosphere that underlines both Cousin Hannah’s harshness and isolation and the emotional coldness surrounding the characters. For example, the barometer dropping "colder than a sea of the icebergs," and “great icicles hanging from the ceiling” during Cousin Hannah’s visits symbolize the emotional frost and the chilling impact she has on Catherine.
  • Snow and ice also symbolize purity, whiteness, and unattainable beauty, reflecting Cousin Hannah’s idealized vision of womanhood as “unsullied honor” and beauty “as white as virginal snow”.

The Riderless White Horse and Rider Imagery

See: Horses and Carriages

  • The image of a riderless white horse "white as snow" coming through the window, bringing snow and cold with it, is a powerful symbol of death or the passing of Cousin Hannah.
  • The white horse traditionally symbolizes a harbinger of death or a psychopomp figure in literature, and here it represents the transition from life to death for this warrior woman. Its entrance is supernatural, blending reality and dream, reinforcing the theme of liminality between life and death.

The Veils, Parasols, and Skirts as Symbols of Femininity and Defense

See: The Umbrella · Clothing, Hats, and Skirts

  • The chapter richly describes feminine accouterments like “great garden hats decked with billowing plumes,” “secretive veils,” “frilled parasols,” and “great hoop skirts flounced with ribbons and lace”. These symbolize traditional femininity, societal expectations, and also the subtle weapons and shields women use in social and gender conflicts.
  • Cousin Hannah’s urging for women to wield their parasol “as her sword or her shield” and to “fly her skirts as her banners in her war against tyrannical man” transforms these delicate objects into instruments of rebellion. The parasol becomes a metaphor for female agency in a world dominated by men.

The Journey and the Northwest Passage

See: The Bus and the Journey

  • Cousin Hannah’s life is described in terms of vast, almost mythical journeys through “desert cities,” “impregnable Asiatic fortresses,” “the great Wall of China,” “Manitoba, Hudson’s Bay,” and “ten thousand singing islands of snow and ice”. This geographical imagery symbolizes her relentless quest for freedom and truth, as well as the impossibility of fully conquering the patriarchal world.
  • The reference to the “Northwest Passage” metaphorically represents the ultimate, elusive goal of life or freedom or “that which cannot be found”. It connotes a spiritual or existential journey, echoing themes of quest and discovery.

The Contrast Between Cousin Hannah and Catherine

  • Catherine is portrayed as fragile, veiled in lace and illusions, “bed-ridden,” “cold heart, cold and hard as the mountain rock,” and largely passive. In contrast, Cousin Hannah is active, combative, and direct. This contrast is symbolized by Catherine’s white umbrella and lace, symbols of delicacy and retreat, versus Cousin Hannah’s white umbrella that she “beat with” like a weapon.
  • Catherine’s world is one of dreams, illusions, and absence, while Cousin Hannah’s is one of harsh realities and confrontations.

Death as a Wedding and Wedding as Death

See: Weddings and Funerals

  • The narrative blurs the boundaries between life milestones and death: “weddings are funerals, and funerals are weddings”. This imagery underscores the theme of transformation and loss inherent in marriage and life.
  • Catherine’s vague, elusive descriptions of her marriage (to a phantom, a porpoise, a star, an iceberg) symbolize the illusory or absent nature of her union and love. Marriage here is a symbolic death of the self or an eternal separation, while death is the final union.

The Ethereal and Dreamlike Atmosphere

The chapter often blurs reality and dream

  • visits occur “only in a dream,” and there are “ambient ghosts of all dead loves”.
  • The use of mirrors, candle flames, flickering stars, and shifting reflections invokes a dreamscape where time and space are fluid. This imagery conveys the fragile mental and emotional state of Catherine and the elusive, almost mythical nature of Cousin Hannah.

The Military and Siege Imagery

  • Cousin Hannah’s life is described metaphorically as a “long siege” with strategic withdrawals and renewed assaults. This symbolizes her relentless struggle for women’s rights and independence, never defeated but always adapting.
  • The castle symbolizes societal structures opposed to her, and the sea eroding the shore suggests slow but inevitable change wrought by persistent effort.

The Symbolism of Light and Darkness

See: Lamps, Candles, and Light

  • Cousin Hannah is described as “standing in a beam of sudden light,” a figure of revelation and defiance.
  • Yet there are also references to “dark cape,” “shadowed faces,” and “clouded looks” symbolizing mystery, obscurity, and the unknown. This interplay of light and dark reflects the duality of her character and the ambiguous outcomes of her life’s battles.

Research: Resources

  • Northwest Passage is a book by Kenneth Roberts. “On every side of us are men who hunt perpetually for their personal northwest passage, too often sacrificing health, strength, and life itself to the search; and who shall say they are not happier in their vain but hopeful quest than wiser, duller folks who sit at home, venturing nothing and, with sour laughs, deriding the seekers for that fabled thoroughfare?” It functions as a rich symbol of adventurous exploration, loss, and the quest for elusive truths. It aligns with the thematic concerns of Roberts’ book but is integrated into the novel’s unique mystical and psychological landscape, emphasizing human vulnerability and the unknowability of fate.
  • “She had been the veteran of so many winter campaigns…” - Reminds one of the painting, Liberty Leading the People, with the bird being the bald eagle. This phrase captures the essence of Cousin Hannah’s life as one of heroic endurance, battling through the metaphorical winters of life and fate, and serves to underline her strength and the epic scale of her personal journey.
  • Ecclesiastes 1:18 “For in much wisdom is much grief: and he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow” The paradox of knowledge - challenges the common belief that knowledge is always a positive thing. It acknowledges that wisdom, while valuable, might also bring greater awareness of the problems, and limitations of human existence. It serves to underscore the novel's exploration of wisdom as a double-edged sword: enlightenment brings clarity but also increases one's sensitivity to life's tragedies and existential sorrows. It expresses the tragic insight that knowledge and sorrow are intimately linked, and that to be truly wise is to be deeply aware of life's inherent griefs. The verse encapsulates a key philosophical reflection in the novel: that the pursuit of wisdom and knowledge, rather than providing comfort, often exposes one to the profound sorrows of human existence.
  • Death and partial knowledge - Our incomplete understanding of life, death, and the universe can lead to poor decisions and actions that ultimately contribute to our own demise. It uses Mr. Spitzer’s introspective and metaphysical musings to convey that death is not a simple event but a complex, multifaceted experience deeply connected to memory, identity, and the limits of human understanding. It portrays death as both an ending and a continuation, filled with ambiguities that challenge the very notions of self and reality.
  • Consanguineous means people who are descended from the same ancestor.
  • A misogynist is a person who dislikes, despises, or is strongly prejudiced against women. Female misogynists (internalized sexism/oppression) are characterized by exalting the feminine ideal, in favor of traditional gender roles, looks down on women who are not feminine, has self-loathing for herself and other women, believes she is superior to other women and/or in constant competition with other women. There is a three tiered system for patriarchy. Patriarchy is the system of social organization that favors and empowers men. Sexism is the code of conduct or rules that are established for women. Misogyny is the policing or enforcement of these rules on women. Thus women can be misogynist if they are helping to police or enforce what women can and cannot do. Looking at all of this it can be determined that no, Cousin Hannah, was not misogynist for wanting women to have independence or the right to vote.

It invokes the ideal of dutiful action and responsibility amid the novel's complex interplay of dreams, realities, and fragmented identities. Miss MacIntosh exemplifies the essay’s virtues, serving as a counterpoint to the novel’s more ambiguous and elusive characters, and reinforcing the theme that despite the chaos and illusion surrounding them, there remains a call to purposeful engagement with life.

  • This characterization of Catherine resembles the folktale of Sleeping Beauty which is a classic fairy tale about a princess who is cursed to sleep for a hundred years by an evil fairy, to be awakened by a handsome prince at the end of that time. The good fairy, realizing that the princess would be frightened if alone when she awakens, uses her wand to put every living person and animal in the palace asleep, to awaken when the princess does. Early origins and versions of the tale involve rape, incest, cannibalism and Sleeping Beauty giving birth to children while she is asleep (similar to non-canical books that depict Eve being raped by angels and giving birth). Catherine embodies the Sleeping Beauty archetype as a woman caught in a protracted, enchanted sleep between life and death, isolated in a dreamlike world filled with phantoms, awaiting an uncertain awakening. Her physical stillness, the symbolic presence of the bird, the grand but empty house, and the thematic concerns with time, death, and unfulfilled love strongly evoke the folktale’s motifs and deepen the novel’s exploration of illusion versus reality. Through Catherine, Young reimagines the Sleeping Beauty myth, using it to probe themes of mortality, memory, and the elusive boundary between waking life and dreams.
