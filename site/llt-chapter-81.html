Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

Chapter 81

← Chapter 80 · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 82 →

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh

Pillars: Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary · Unfinished Is Honest

The Questions

Esther is “cheerful, not at all discouraged,” sure “everything which had been unreal in the past would be real in the future.” Is her hope delusion, or the open construction held with joy?

She insists she is “not the ship which had no cargo, the sky which had no star, the carriage which had no horse.” What is she claiming to be full of?

“She never would be buried, though.” What is a life that is deathless in its incompletion?

Why does the book let its figure of the everlasting unfinished be, in the end, happy?

This is the chapter that turns the everlasting pregnancy from affliction to joy, and it is the culmination of Esther’s meaning. “Esther Longtree was cheerful, not at all discouraged by the future which would be so different from the past, she hoped, for everything which had been unreal in the past would be real in the future.” She is not despairing over her undelivered years; she is cheerful, hopeful, expectant, sure that something will come, that the unreal will be made real. This is Unfinished Is Honest held not as tragedy but as hope, Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary lived with cheer, the woman who never finishes facing her endless future not with dread but with expectancy. The open condition, in Esther, is happy.

She insists she is full, not empty. “She was not the ship which had no cargo, the sky which had no star, the carriage which had no horse,” and the triple denial is her claim to fullness: she carries, she is freighted, she is not the empty vessel but the laden one. This matters against everything the book has shown of empty shells and sealed vacancies, the finished selves hollow at the center. Esther, the unfinished one, is the full one, pregnant with “snow and wind and starlight,” carrying always, laden forever. Her incompletion is not emptiness but its opposite, a permanent fullness, a cargo that never has to be unloaded, a sky that keeps its star. The open construction, here, is the full one.

And she is deathless. “She never would be buried, though,” the chapter says, and the deathlessness follows from the incompletion: a life that never finishes is a life that never ends, an expectancy that cannot die because it never delivers, never completes, never closes into the finished thing that could then be buried. Esther is immortal precisely because she is unfinished, forever pregnant and so forever alive, the everlasting expectancy that outlasts every sealed and completed life around it. The book has shown that the finished is the dead thing; here it shows the last consequence, that the unfinished is the deathless one, and Esther, never delivered, will never be buried.

The book lets its figure of the everlasting unfinished be happy for the deepest reason it has. Every sealed figure in the book has failed and suffered, Catherine in her tomb of dream, Miss MacIntosh hollow behind her plainness, Cousin Hannah collapsing at her deathbed, Mr. Spitzer unable even to rest; and Esther, the one who does not seal, the one who holds herself open and unfinished and full, is the one the book grants joy. Her cheer is not naivety and not delusion; it is the affirmative face of the argument, the demonstration that the open construction, the unfinished life, the permanent expectancy, is not a sad condition to be endured but a full, deathless, hopeful one. The woman who will never deliver and never be buried is the happiest figure in the book, and that is the book’s final claim about the unfinished.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Esther’s cheer for pitiable delusion, a broken woman deceived about a future that will never come, and the reading that takes her deathlessness for mere pathos, a curse of never resting. Neither the delusion nor the curse is what the chapter shows.

The footing is that the everlasting pregnancy is held here with joy rather than despair, Unfinished Is Honest lived cheerfully, the endless future faced with expectancy; that Esther is full, not empty, the laden ship and the starred sky, her incompletion a permanent fullness against every hollow sealed self; that she is deathless precisely because she is unfinished, forever expectant and so forever alive, never delivered and so never buried; and that the book grants its figure of the open construction the happiness it denies every sealed one. The unfinished is full, deathless, and glad. Esther will never deliver and never be buried, and the chapter lets her be, in the end, the happiest figure in the book.

For Reflection

Can a permanently unfinished condition be held with cheer and hope rather than endured as a lack?

Is the open, incomplete, expectant life empty, or is it the full one, the laden ship, the sky that keeps its star?

What would it mean that the thing which never finishes is also the thing which never dies?
