Life, Love, and Truth That Doesn’t Fail

The Figures and What They Carry

← Truth · Life, Love, and Truth · Chapter 1 →

Pillars: Not One Self · Reality Is Constructed · Grief Doesn't End · Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act · Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary · Unfinished Is Honest · The Margins Reveal the Truth · The Past Doesn't Go Away · The Sacred Is Here · The Crossing Point

This is a map, meant to be returned to. These figures are not allegories, each fixed to one meaning and no other. A person in this novel is a crowd, as Not One Self insists, and the figures carry their planks the way a person carries moods, several at once and never for good. What follows names the plank each figure carries most, and where a figure carries more than one, it says so.

Catherine carries Reality Is Constructed, and she is the book’s warning about what happens when an invention is sealed shut. Everyone here lives in an invention; that is the ordinary condition, not the error. Catherine’s error is the sealing. She has built a world of opium dreams, imaginary guests, and a shuttered house by the sea, and she has forgotten that she built it. She believes “her life was this play of illusion,” that there should be “nothing certain but uncertainty,” and she has taken that belief as final and unrevisable. The construction has become a tomb she mistakes for the only room there is. She is the frozen blueprint in the register of a single mind: not condemned for imagining, which the novel never condemns, but shown as a caution for having stopped.

Miss MacIntosh is the seduction of the finished blueprint. Her common sense, her honesty, her plainness read like the novel’s own answer. Her groundedness is itself a construction, and the novel puts it under pressure until it cracks. Her “old-fashioned values” harden into rigidity; she “would submit to no luxurious temptation”; and the same steadiness that steadies Vera also costs her compassion and shuts her to what she cannot already explain. The revelation of her baldness, her wig, her secret and wandering past, is the novel lifting the plain surface to show it was a made up thing all along.

She is double, and the map has to hold both halves. She genuinely carries The Sacred Is Here, the sea shells and bean pods and the clear reality of simple things, and in that she is nearly a teacher. But she also shows what The Sacred Is Here becomes when it is finished and sealed: a blueprint frozen, an answer that fails because it will no longer move. The novel loves her and warns through her at once. She is a figure the story examines, not the lesson it hands down.

Mr. Spitzer carries the interior planks more fully than anyone. He is Not One Self walking: he cannot tell where he ends and his dead brother begins, so that he is two men in one and one man in two, and this is not his breakdown but the truth of what a self is. He carries The Past Doesn't Go Away, living with the past pressed into his present, wearing his dead brother’s watch, moved by the dead who go on shaping the living. He carries Grief Doesn't End, the perpetual mourner whose grief never closes, so that his sorrow for the old clam-digger can run deeper than his sorrow for Catherine and neither is discharged. And he carries Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act: he stays in the room with Catherine’s illusions and does not correct them, unwilling “to disturb the dreamer,” attending to a life without moving to fix it. Where the book needs someone to show a plank from the inside, it usually reaches for him.

Esther Longtree stands at The Margins Reveal the Truth. A waitress judged ugly and mulish, an outcast at the dead end of a small town, she is positioned exactly where the seams of the ordinary world show, and from that margin she sees what the settled center cannot. She carries The Sacred Is Here too, mothering everything within reach, rooted in the here-and-now of the body and the greasy-spoon world, finding the sacred in what is underfoot rather than in any beyond.

Her everlasting pregnancy is the living image of Unfinished Is Honest and of Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary. Forever expectant and never delivered, she is the unfinished held as honesty rather than confessed as failure, the construction that will not close because closing would be the lie. Her stillborn children are not proof that she has failed to make something. They are the novel’s insistence that the incomplete is the truthful shape, and that a thing kept open is worth more than a thing sealed.

Vera is the seeker who learns the practice. She carries the book’s question, the search for life, for love, for a truth that does not fail, and across the novel she moves from wanting a fixed answer and loving the image of things toward the harder work the system asks: learning to witness, learning to keep the construction open, learning that the truth that holds is the one kept under revision. She is the reader’s proxy, and her education is the reader’s.

She is also The Crossing Point. She stands where everyone who came before, Catherine and Miss MacIntosh and Mr. Spitzer and the inherited weight of the dead, meets everyone who will come after. The past does not lie behind her; it is pressed beneath her, and her choices pack down the ground the next ones will stand on. She is the inheritor of the book’s griefs and inventions, and the place where they are handed forward.

Secondary carriers sound the same planks in a minor key, and the chapters name them the same way. Cousin Hannah is another finished blueprint, her lifetime of suffrage battles and public certainties which, at her death, she calls as nothing beside the domestic wars fought at home, an edifice of conviction shown to have sealed too soon. Moses the bus driver is Reality Is Constructed gone rigid, certainty hardened into dogma. Madge is the refusal of The Sacred Is Here, choosing artifice over the growing fact of her body. And the deaf, the blind, the mute, the hangman, and the wandering salesmen belong with Esther at The Margins Reveal the Truth, positioned where the ordinary world’s seams are easiest to see. None of these needs a fresh vocabulary. They carry the eleven terms the rest of the book already uses.
