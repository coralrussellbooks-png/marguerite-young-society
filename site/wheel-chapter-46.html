Research notes: Chapter 46 · Reading guide: Guide, Chapter 46

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer

Pillar 1 Not One Self

Pillar note: Pillar 1 Not One Self

Mr. Spitzer is never singular. He exists as executor, musician, mourner, census-taker, ghost, shepherd, and wanderer, often within a single sentence. He explicitly acknowledges he was never fully present:

"Never yet had he been present at a climax."

This is not biographical accident. It is ontological condition. He moves from "sleep to sleep, dream to dream" which means the self is not a continuous entity arriving at moments of clarity, but a series of overlapping, irresolvable states. His twin brother haunts the margins of the chapter, the "checkered career and butterfly life among the shadows", suggesting that even the boundary between self and other-self is porous.

Cousin Hannah herself is plural: suffragette, mountain-climber, secret coward, great lover, dead beauty, wandering planet. The chapter refuses to resolve her into a single identity. She is simultaneously the boldest adventurer and "weaker than woman was". This is not contradiction to be resolved. It is, per Pillar 1, what she is.

Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

Pillar note: Pillar 2 Reality Is Constructed

The chapter is saturated with the language of construction and surface. Beauty itself is described as irreducibly masked:

"There was nothing but this clothing, that there was nothing but this great mask of life and love, even of death."

Mr. Spitzer does not mourn the mask as a fallen substitute for some truer reality beneath. He accepts the mask as reality. The cloud upon the face of beauty is not concealing beauty; it is beauty:

"The cloud upon the face of beauty was beauty itself. So never would he lift the cloud."

This is Pillar 2 stated almost directly. There is no sunlit world outside the cave. The Alps that Cousin Hannah climbed are simultaneously literal mountains and "mountains of illusion". The alpenglow, "the reillumination of refracted sunlight", is itself a secondary light, a reflection of a reflection, and it is this refracted, constructed light that illuminates the sacred peaks. Reality is not found. It is made of refractions.

Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

Pillar note: Pillar 3 Grief Doesn't End

This pillar may be the most completely embodied in the chapter. Grief here is not a stage Mr. Spitzer passes through. It is the climate of his entire existence. The very structure of the prose (its endless subordinate clauses, its refusal to arrive) enacts grief as accumulation rather than progression.

The snow that covers Cousin Hannah does not melt:

"That snow melting not but increasing like that oblivion decreasing not with time's illusive passage, for time passes not, and only man passes."

This is the grammar of permanent grief. Time does not heal because time does not pass, only the person passes through a static accumulation of loss. The iceberg that "seemed to move into the room when Cousin Hannah died" does not leave. The frost on the mirrors remains. The candle flames are "frozen into minor stars". These are not metaphors for temporary shock. They are descriptions of a permanent atmospheric condition.

Even his walks on the foggy beach (blowing the alpenhorn, hearing the bells, being followed by sheep) are not acts of grief moving toward resolution. They are grief as habitation. He lives inside the weather of loss.

Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Pillar note: Pillar 4 Witnessing Is The Core Ethical Act

Mr. Spitzer's entire function in this chapter is that of witness. He is not Cousin Hannah's lover, not her savior, not her judge. He is the one who stays in the room, or rather, who arrives before or after but never at the climax which means he is constitutionally the witness rather than the participant. This is not failure. It is his ethical role.

His witnessing is without flinching. He sees her granite façade and her secret weeping:

"Perhaps they would have loved her if they had known her weeping when a snowflake fell upon her mottled hand cold as the skin of a frog."

He does not simplify her into hero or fraud. He holds the full complexity: the charlatan and the coward who was "more heroic than greatest heroism". He asks questions in the fog, "Have you seen my lady — or my lord?", not to resolve the mystery but to continue the act of attention. The census-taker who counts and recounts, always finding one missing is the figure of the Witness who refuses to declare the count complete.

Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

Pillar note: Pillar 5 Build Anyway, Knowing It's Temporary

The prose itself is the primary evidence for this pillar. The chapter does not move forward linearly. It circles, returns, adds another layer, circles again. Mr. Spitzer builds his understanding of Cousin Hannah through recursive passes; each pass adding sediment rather than replacing what came before.

He appropriates the alpenhorn, carries it to the beach, blows it in the fog, is followed by sheep, and from this provisional act of mourning, builds something:

"So from this old suffragette he had learned something, after all — she had brought him, by her dying, to reality."

But this learning does not conclude. It opens into further questions, further wanderings, further encounters with strangers in the fog. The building is never finished. The old steeplejack's speech does not answer Mr. Spitzer's questions; it multiplies them, returning him to the beginning with more material, not more resolution.

The lute-makers are dead, but their lutes remain. The musical instruments Mr. Spitzer collects are themselves recursive objects: things that carry forward the work of hands that no longer exist, requiring new hands to make them sound again.

Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

Pillar note: Pillar 6 Unfinished Is Honest

This pillar is perhaps most explicitly thematized in the chapter's treatment of Mr. Spitzer's music:

"He should compose, if not his elegy which was impossible so long as he lived, impossible to end, then his aubade for the evening star."

The elegy cannot be finished because the mourner is still alive. Completion would be a violence against the ongoing experience of loss. The "ongoing sentence" is the integrity of the chapter itself: a prose that never fully stops, that opens new subordinate clauses just as it seems to approach a period.

Cousin Hannah's will, dated "many years before her death" did not mention the alpenhorn. This incompletion, the gap between what was recorded and what existed, is not an administrative failure. It is the honest acknowledgment that no document can fully capture a life. Mr. Spitzer steps into that gap as executor, carrying what the will could not contain.

Her suffrage work, too, is unfinished; she was a "trail-blazer blazing like a rising star, falling star" but the trail continues beyond her. The incompletion of her project is its integrity, not its defeat.

Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Pillar note: Pillar 7 The Margins Reveal the Truth

Cousin Hannah is the marginal figure par excellence. She is:

  • A woman in a world that did not record women's mountain-climbing in its "stud

books"

  • A suffragette whom some called "a charlatan, a mere poseur, a mountebank"
  • A secret coward behind a granite façade
  • One "forgotten by mountain-climbers now" commemorated by "no coin" no tomb, no winged horse's head

"She was already old, obsolete as some old coin which had gone out of circulation long before the age of Hugh De Capet."

Her marginality is not incidental. It is structural evidence of how the categories of history are constructed to exclude. The stud books that record great stallions but not great women climbers reveal, by their exclusion, the constructed nature of what counts as worthy of record. Cousin Hannah is the evidence that the categories are wrong.

The old steeplejack in the fog (tall, lurching, amputated, speaking in paradoxes) is another marginal figure whose speech from the edges of coherence reveals more truth than official discourse. The census-taker who is "dead as the census-taker", the official counter of persons, is dead, while the uncountable one remains alive in the fog.

Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

Pillar note: Pillar 8 The Past Doesn't Go Away

This is perhaps the pillar most structurally embedded in the chapter's entire cosmology. The past does not recede. It accumulates.

"The sense of the immediate was freighted with the past — and if it were not, then what would be the present but an empty room, a closed door, the hull of an old boat stripped bare of its shrouds, a withered rose, a dead love?"

The present moment without sedimented past is nothing: an empty room. Meaning is entirely constituted by what has accumulated beneath.

The boats that "had disappeared in storms a hundred years ago but had returned" are perfect images of sedimentary time: the past pressing back up through the surface of the present, not as memory but as material presence. The alpenhorn itself is sedimentary: it carries the music of Cousin Hannah's mountains into Mr. Spitzer's flat coastal world, depositing Alpine time into tidal time.

The snow that does not melt is sedimentary grief; each snowfall adding to what came before, building a stratigraphy of loss that the living walk upon without knowing its full depth.

Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

Pillar note: Pillar 9 Language Shapes What's Possible

The chapter is acutely self-aware about language's constructive power. The old steeplejack's speech is the most concentrated demonstration:

"I love because I love not. I love not because I love. I am filled with self-contradictions like heart murmurs."

This is language naming its own location, from inside paradox, from inside the place where ordinary referential language breaks down. The steeplejack does not pretend to speak from nowhere. He speaks from the position of the dead-who-live, the amputated, the fallen.

Mr. Spitzer's own language throughout the chapter performs its constructedness. The long, subordinate-clause-laden sentences do not claim transparent access to truth. They accumulate qualifications, return on themselves, insert "perhaps" and "it seemed" and "Mr. Spitzer had no doubt, though": a language that constantly marks its own uncertainty, its own location in a particular consciousness at a particular moment.

The alpenhorn's notes "did not necessarily usher in the Alps": the signifier does not automatically produce its referent. Language (and music as language) constructs rather than reveals. The "ranz des vaches" played in flat coastal fog does not summon mountains. It constructs a longing for mountains, which is a different and more honest thing.

Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

Pillar note: Pillar 10 The Sacred Is Here

The chapter explicitly refuses transcendence-as-escape while insisting on the sacred within the immanent. Mr. Spitzer does not look for Cousin Hannah in heaven. He looks for her in:

  • The fog
  • The waves that come "to pasture like sheep with moony eyes"
  • The fireflies in the fog
  • The crackling stars under his feet
  • The pigeon murmurs and crane cries

"Music arose from this chaos, it seemed to him in his old age, whatever might be the end of music — that things divine were things secular."

This is Pillar 10 stated directly: things divine were things secular. The sacred is not elsewhere. It is in every "corruscating pebble or spark shining through the iris-eyed fog". The alpenglow (refracted, secondary, earthly light) illuminates the peaks more truly than any supernatural radiance could.

Even the dead moon, which is dead, is "the ensign of deathless love throughout history". The sacred does not require the living. It inhabits the dead, the marginal, the obsolete coin, the forgotten mountain-climber. This is immanent transcendence: the sacred pointing deeper into the world, not away from it.

Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

Pillar note: Pillar 11 The Crossing Point

This final pillar finds its fullest expression in the chapter's closing meditation and in Mr. Spitzer's structural position throughout.

Mr. Spitzer is constituted by what was deposited before him, by Cousin Hannah's life, by the music of dead lute-makers by the boats that sank a hundred years ago and returned by the mountains Cousin Hannah climbed and that now "resurrect themselves" in her dying. He did not choose this inheritance. It is structural.

And he is, in turn, depositing ground for those who come after. He carries the alpenhorn. He asks the questions in the fog. He performs the musical post-mortem. He is the executor: literally, the one who carries out, who continues the action of the dead into the living world.

Cousin Hannah's final words in the chapter make this explicit:

"Ah, bright star of my love, find your way to the lightless star. Find your way to one who has neither wings nor light."

She is asking him to carry her forward, not into heaven, but into the continuing immanent world. He is the crossing point between her life and whatever comes after. The "carrying continues" not because he chose it but because he is structurally positioned at the intersection of her time and his.

The sheep that follow him after he blows the alpenhorn (though he is lost, though he is no shepherd) are the image of this structural obligation. He did not ask to be followed. He is followed because he is where the path crosses itself.
