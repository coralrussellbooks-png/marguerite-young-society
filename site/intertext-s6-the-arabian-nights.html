The Arabian Nights

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Esther Longtree · Joachim Spitzer

Allusions: Fairy Tales and Folklore

Chapters discussed: 39, 44, 52, 79

Definition from research

Category: optional. Ali Baba is named once and "open sesame" appears four times, and two more tales of the Nights stand behind Catherine's drug bottle and Esther's dream of a palace.

The Thousand and One Nights gathers stories from Persia, India, Iraq, Syria, and Egypt inside one frame: Scheherazade tells a tale each night to a king who means to kill her at dawn, and stops before the ending so that he lets her live another day. Two tales now among its best known, "Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves" and "The Talking Bird, the Singing Tree, and the Golden Water," were told in Paris in 1709 by Hanna Diyab, a young Maronite storyteller from Aleppo, to the French translator Antoine Galland, who added them to his Mille et une nuits (1704 to 1717). Ali Baba overhears the thieves' password, "Open, Sesame," and the rock door of their treasure cave opens to him.

In Young's novel the password never works. In a hidden place walled by clouds, Mr. Spitzer "felt as if he were Ali Baba in the cave of forty thieves—cave which had been closed by a door of stone, and there was no open sesame unless it was himself walled by these clouds" (chapter 52). Thinking of all the letters people wrote to Cousin Hannah, he finds the same lock: "There was no open sesame to these mysteries he felt" (chapter 44). Catherine's porcelain drug bottle "contained the purple fog and the glowering genii, spirits of fire or air" (chapter 39), like the jinni a fisherman lets out of a sealed brass jar from the sea. And Esther Longtree, promised a farm, "immediately had thought must be a golden palace in a garden of talking birds and silver fountains" (chapter 79), the treasures the princess Parizade wins in Diyab's tale.
