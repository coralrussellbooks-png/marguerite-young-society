Chapter Five: Build Anyway, Knowing It’s Temporary

Characters: Cousin Hannah · Vera Cartwheel

The fifth pillar is where Young's philosophy becomes livable.

The first four pillars are descriptions of a world that is genuinely difficult to inhabit: the self is plural and unstable, reality is constructed and contested, grief is permanent, and the only honest ethical stance requires a quality of attention that most of us cannot sustain for long without relief. It would be reasonable, having followed Young this far, to ask, what exactly is being offered? If there is no bedrock, no resolution, no arrival, no foundation that holds, then what is the point of building anything at all?

Young's answer is the fifth pillar, and it is the most quietly radical thing her system contains.

You build anyway.

Not because the building will last or because the foundation is solid or because the finished structure will be what you imagined when you started. You build because the recursive attempt is the only form of integrity the system allows. You build because building is what it means to keep going, and keeping going is what the witness does. The building is provisional. That is not a concession. That is the design.

The word recursive is worth unpacking fully. A recursive process is one that refers back to itself, that uses its own output as part of its input, that loops through its own structure in order to proceed. A recursive story is one that keeps returning to its own beginning, not to repeat it but to find something new in it on each return, to discover that the beginning contains more than it appeared to the first time through.

"Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" by Marguerite Young is a recursive novel. It returns to its own images and characters and weathers in the way that a mind returns to what it cannot let go of, finding new layers on each return, deepening rather than resolving. The novel does not move from beginning to middle to end in the linear sense. It moves the way memory moves, the way grief moves, the way the plural self moves: returning, deepening, finding that the thing you thought you understood contains more than you thought.

This formal recursion is Young's argument about construction. You do not build once and stand back. You build, and the building reveals what you didn't know when you started, and you build again incorporating what you've learned, and that building reveals more, and you build again. The structure is always under construction. That is not its failure. It is its honesty.

Young said as much about her own career: “All the books I have written have been one book.” Each book returns to the ground of the last and finds more in it. The utopians of "Angel in the Forest" are the pillar’s historical model. Their houses were “built to endure as long as the earth did,” and the communities failed, and the book still finds “nothing so beautiful as trial and error, this side of paradise.”

Alfred North Whitehead's process philosophy, developed across a body of work culminating in Process and Reality, is the most rigorous available philosophical framework for the world Young's fiction describes. Whitehead's central claim is that reality is not composed of things but of processes. The fundamental units of existence are not stable objects that persist through time but events, occasions of experience, moments of becoming that perish as soon as they are complete and give rise to the next occasion. What we call things, including persons, are patterns of process, relatively stable configurations of becoming that maintain enough continuity to be recognizable across time without ever being literally the same from moment to moment. This is Young's plural self in the language of metaphysics. The self in Whitehead is not a thing that has experiences. It is a process that is its experiences. The self is not the noun. It is the verb. Young says this through fiction. Whitehead says it through formal metaphysics. They are saying the same thing.

The fifth pillar resolves the apparent paradox of Young's system. If there is no foundation, how do you build? If there is no arrival, why journey? If the self is plural and grief is permanent and reality is constructed, what is the point of making anything? The answer is that the making is the point. Not the made thing, which is always provisional, always already in need of revision, always containing more than its maker intended and less than its maker hoped. The act of making, which is the recursive attempt, the return to the beginning with what you learned on the last pass, the building that knows it is building and keeps going anyway.

This is what Young does across eighteen years and 1,198 pages. She builds a novel that is about the impossibility of the foundations that novels conventionally depend on, and she builds it anyway, and the building is magnificent, and it does not resolve, and it does not arrive, and it is complete in the only sense that matters: it is honest about what it is and what it cannot be and what it is for.

It’s there in Young’s early poems, where a song is an “edifice” that “Will crumble apart in the wild blue weather / Of other springs,” through the New Harmony utopias and the novel’s nestless birds, in "Harp Song for a Radical" the hope is smaller and more exact: “It might not be heaven on earth, it might only be an eight-hour day.” In her late interviews Young spoke of writers wanting to be “known and loved beyond their time,” a real wish for permanence that sits beside her image of the bridge “which might end before you reach the other side.”

Phase One, Childhood and the Early Poems (1908 to 1934)

Pillar 5: Build Anyway, Knowing It’s Temporary

"Song's Dissemination" states the pillar outright about Young's own art. The song is an "edifice" that "Will crumble apart in the wild blue weather / Of other springs," and "Wherever it is, it will not stand." "Flame" (1928) gives the human case. An inland daughter who longs for the sea "got herself a wagon / To steer through waves of wheat," and on her last, sick morning she goes out anyway, because "she knew she would have gone / Through rain or sleet or snow." "I Heard a Bird," written at nine, already has the provisional nest: "the nest was put into a museum / And it is there now / Unless someone / Has taken it away."

Complication: "Jonathan's House" shows the built thing betraying the dream. The second husband builds the house in stone, gets every detail right, and leaves out the secret door: "No panel gave. The wide, unyielding stone was there." "The Seeker" builds a life on letters that never come.

Phase Two, The Chicago Years, Prismatic Ground, and the Graduate Essays (1935 to 1937)

Pillar 5: Build Anyway, Knowing It’s Temporary

"Currin Valley" is the phase's clearest case. Settlers farm "rock-bitten lands," come away "Year after year with empty hands," and "could never tear / The tenuous roots they had set / In lands too beautiful to leave." "Spring" (1935) "Contrives her delicate masonry" before she blooms. "Archer in a World" (1935) shoots on in "a world which has known its ending," because "where his arrow strikes there is / This extending him, this skill." "Heroics" (1937): "Nevertheless, he will come home."

Phase Three, The Philosophical Poems (1938 to 1945)

Pillar 5: Build Anyway, Knowing It’s Temporary

"Guide to New Harmony, Indiana" (1941) brings in the utopians who would become "Angel in the Forest": Rapp's angel came "To bring the many sheaves of gold together / And colonize the darkness with this light," and now "Only the dearly dead remain." "Farewell at the Station" (1943): "For love, we are all voyagers on seas unknown." "Proem to Reality" adds that "god was the only project admitting of completion," so human building is always unfinished.

Complication: "Death by Rarity" says "the perfect bird / Never will be born."

Phase Four, "Angel in the Forest" and “Complex Life and Complex Letters” (1945 to 1946)

Pillar 5: Build Anyway, Knowing It’s Temporary

The book states the pillar: "In imperfection itself there might be many values," and "nothing so beautiful as trial and error, this side of paradise." Rappite houses were "built to endure as long as the earth did," and the Owenites ask whether "all mankind" is "ephemeral as those who had nailed the beams together." Young later said the utopians left "beautiful workmanship" and "the education of women," and that the whole thing "is still a possibility for the future." Against the ancient sandpipers who "build no nests," the utopians "attempted to build nests."

Complication: William Allen gives the opposing view: "How foolhardy to build a village on any human frontier." Young also later judged that "In Utopias, no matter what they are, women are sacrificed."

Phase Five, "Miss MacIntosh, My Darling" (written from the mid 1940s, published 1965)

Pillar 5: Build Anyway, Knowing It’s Temporary

The novel opens by refusing the pillar. Vera was taught it was better "to kiss the rod" than "to imagine we were free and our own architects... We were the nestless birds." She adds "Or so I thought then," and the refusal is undone. Cousin Hannah's keys come with "even the wounded must seek a nesting place." The book ends on the lightest of nests, a misspelled sign in a window: "bee bak in a whale."

What the novel adds is the change of mind. The utopians of "Angel in the Forest" built and failed. Vera begins by believing she is a nestless bird and ends among people who still seek a nesting place. The pillar is lived as something learned.

Phase Six, The Occasional Prose, Interviews, and Self-Interpretation (1940s to 1995)

Pillar 5: Build Anyway, Knowing It’s Temporary

The bridge "which might end before you reach the other side." In thirty or forty thousand years it will all be gone, "not even a memory of Shakespeare." "All the books I have written have been one book."

Complication: people write to be "known and loved beyond their time," "remembered 100 years from now." "The Doll People" ends on "the permanence of things, even of dolls." In 1989 she rejoiced in being dug up "from what well might have been my obscure grave like that of a neglected Lazarus... a Lazarus laughing."

Phase Seven, "Harp Song for a Radical" (written in her last decades, published 1999)

Pillar 5: Build Anyway, Knowing It’s Temporary

The bridge returns, with a smaller hope: "out of every dreamer something good comes. It might not be heaven on earth, it might only be an eight-hour day." The millennial dreams "seemed to fail, only seemed to fail." Debs worked knowing "the tragic ground on which the future must flower."

Complication: Debs "would cling to the illusions of progress through reason as long as he could, no doubt longer than he could."
