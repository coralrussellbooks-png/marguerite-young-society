Characters: Miss MacIntosh

Marguerite Young published Miss MacIntosh, My Darling in 1965 after eighteen years of writing it. It is 1,198 pages long. It refuses to end. Almost no one read it. It was filed under eccentric maximalism and left there.

That filing was an error, and its cost has been ongoing. Because what Young built was not only a novel. It was a set of formal discoveries about how art can hold the truth of a life without lying about that truth. And those discoveries did not disappear when the novel was ignored. They kept appearing, independently, in the work of artists who had no idea they were working in a tradition, because the tradition had no name.

It has one now.

The lineage that runs from Young is not a school. It is not a set of shared influences or a manifesto or a movement. It is a set of artists who arrived, by different routes, at the same fundamental commitment: that the work must be honest about the nature of the self and the nature of time and the nature of grief, and that this honesty has formal consequences. It changes what the work looks like. It changes how long it takes. It changes what counts as finished.

Here is where the lineage goes from Young forward.

The Literary Line

Ann Quin (1936-1973) was working in Britain at almost the same moment as Young, with the same formal instinct and almost no shared audience. Her novels dissolve the boundary between interior and exterior by refusing to mark it in prose. Berg (1964) and Passages (1969) move through consciousness the way Young moves through consciousness: recursively, with accumulation rather than resolution, with grief that does not process but deepens. Quin drowned at Brighton in 1973 at thirty-seven. The parallel with Young's Miss MacIntosh walking into the water is not symbolic. It is what happens to women working at this level of formal honesty when the culture has no place for them.

Clarice Lispector (1920-1977), writing in Brazil through the same decades, arrived at the same place from a different starting point. Her novel The Passion According to G.H. (1964) is a single character's encounter with a cockroach that becomes a sustained investigation of the self's plurality and the constructed nature of reality, held in long interior sentences that circle and refuse to conclude. Lispector understood what Young understood: that the honest account of consciousness cannot be made brief without being falsified.

Renata Adler (Speedboat, 1976; Pitch Dark, 1983) brought Youngian formal instincts into the fragmentary mode, where the refusal to resolve becomes structural rather than accumulated. The sentences arrive and do not connect in conventional ways, because the plural self does not connect its experiences in conventional ways. The grief is present between the fragments, in the white space, in what cannot be said.

Marilynne Robinson (Housekeeping, 1980) moved the formal project into lyric realism, where the Youngian long sentence finds its quietest expression. The novel's narrator, Ruth, is a witness in the precise sense the pillars define: someone who stays in the room with contradiction and does not conclude. The lake that runs under everything in the novel is Young's permanent weather made literal: the grief that does not process, the loss that does not resolve, the world that keeps becoming something other than what you imagined.

W.G. Sebald (1944-2001), writing in German and published in English translation through the 1990s, worked the sedimentary time pillar more explicitly than any other writer in the tradition. The Rings of Saturn (1995) and Austerlitz (2001) move through the accumulated weight of historical loss with a quality of attention that is precisely what the witness requires: the refusal to make the difficulty manageable, the insistence that the grief be carried at its full weight. The photographs embedded in the prose are formal devices for the same purpose: here is evidence. Here is what was deposited. This is not a symbol. It is a record.

Toni Morrison built the whole tradition into the American novel and extended it beyond where the tradition had been. Beloved (1987) is the sedimentary time pillar in full: the past that does not recede, the grief that is older than any individual self, the loss that will not stay in the past because it is the substrate of the present. Morrison understood that the formal demands of Radical Immanence and the formal demands of telling the truth about American history were the same demands. The ghost in Beloved is not supernatural. She is geological.

Jenny Offill (Dept. of Speculation, 2014; Weather, 2020) brought the fragmentary form to its most contemporary expression, where the incompletion is built into the structure at the sentence level. The permanent weather pillar is present in both titles, and in both books: Dept. of Speculation about the grief of a marriage failing to be what it was supposed to be, Weather about the grief of living in a climate that is becoming uninhabitable. Both books refuse to resolve. Both hold their difficulty without falsifying it.

Ocean Vuong (On Earth We're Briefly Gorgeous, 2019) extended the tradition into the epistolary mode and into the explicit address of intergenerational transmission. The novel is a letter from a son to a mother who cannot read it, which is the crossing point made structural: the vertical axis of what was given and what cannot be returned, held in the horizontal spread of the present moment. The plural self, the inherited grief, the sedimentary time: all eleven pillars are present.

The Visual Art Line

Agnes Martin (1912-2004) is the painter who most fully worked the Radical Immanence project without the words for it. Her paintings are not about presence. They are presence, achieved through the recursive application of a simple mark, the horizontal line, repeated across a surface with the full attention the witness brings. The slight imperfections in the lines are not failures. They are the evidence of a human being making the mark, which is the recursive construction pillar in its most visible form. The work is never finished in the sense of being resolved. It is finished in the sense of being honest.

Ana Mendieta (1948-1985) worked the body as crossing point before that phrase existed. Her Silueta series, made in Mexico and Iowa through the 1970s and early 1980s, placed the outline of her body in the landscape and documented what happened when natural processes, fire, water, earth, moved through it. The work is the intergenerational witness in its most literal form: the body returning to the ground, the self as deposit, the transmission line made visible through the earth's reception of it. That Mendieta's death in 1985 remains unresolved is its own unfinished sentence.

Cy Twombly (1928-2011) worked the long sentence in paint. His canvases carry marks that are legible as writing without being readable as language, which is the honest form of the experience the marks are trying to hold: something is here that exceeds the capacity of conventional description. The works accumulate rather than resolve. They are sedimentary in their formal logic: layer on layer, mark over mark, nothing erased, everything carried forward.

Kara Walker works the sedimentary time pillar with more sustained attention than any other visual artist in the contemporary tradition. Her silhouettes are made from the formal vocabulary of nineteenth-century decorative art, the very vocabulary that decorated the period of American slavery, and the displacement of horror into decorous form is itself the argument: this is what happens when the grief is not witnessed. The work witnesses it. The scale at which Walker works, the room-sized installations, is the Youngian answer to the demand for compression: the truth requires this much space.

Doris Salcedo makes furniture-sized sculptures from the material remains of disappearance, from the clothes of the disappeared, from the chairs and tables of people who will not return. The work is the permanent grief pillar made three-dimensional: this is what grief looks like when it is held at its full weight rather than processed into something more manageable. The objects are not memorials. They are witnesses. They stay in the room.

Wolfgang Tillmans works the sacred ordinary pillar in photography. His images give the same quality of attention to a piece of crumpled paper or a friend asleep on a couch as to a political demonstration or a landscape. The formal commitment is the philosophical argument: everything is worthy of this attention. Nothing is more sacred than this. The immanent transcendence pillar is present in every frame.

The Music Line

Morton Feldman (1926-1987) was working in the same New York downtown scene that produced much of the American avant-garde, but his music moved away from the structural logic of that scene and toward something closer to Young's formal commitments. His late works, some lasting four and five and six hours, refuse the satisfactions of conventional musical form. They accumulate and recede and refuse to resolve, because resolution is the lie the music is built to expose. For Philip Guston (1984) and the String Quartet No. 2 (1983) are the Youngian long sentence in sound: the permanent weather of a music that will not conclude.

Pauline Oliveros (1932-2016) developed Deep Listening as both a compositional practice and a philosophical stance. The witness pillar is the center of her work: the quality of attention that does not look away, that stays present to what is actually sounding rather than what the ear expects to hear. Her pieces are scores for a different relationship to time, which is the sedimentary time pillar in practice: listening into the accumulated present rather than moving through it toward a destination.

Julius Eastman (1940-1990) worked at the intersection of minimalist structure and Black radical aesthetics with a fury that the tradition could not hold. His pieces, Crazy Nigger (1978), Evil Nigger (1979), Gay Guerrilla (1979), are recursive in the Feldman sense but carried at a different register of urgency: the permanent grief of a life held inside multiple categories of erasure, made audible in music that will not be quiet about what it is carrying. Eastman died homeless in 1990. The transmission of his work was nearly broken. It is being repaired.

Nils Frahm is not a maximalist, but his work carries the sacred ordinary pillar in a way that Feldman and Oliveros carry the witness pillar: with such full attention to the specific sound of a specific instrument in a specific room that the ordinary becomes charged with exactly the significance the tenth pillar describes. The immanent sacred requires no transcendent guarantee. It requires this quality of listening.

The Film Line

Chantal Akerman (1950-2015) built Jeanne Dielman, 23, quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles (1975) as 201 minutes of a woman doing housework, at real time, with a camera that does not move to make the work more interesting. The formal commitment is the philosophical argument: this life is worthy of this attention. The sacred ordinary pillar is present in every static shot of dishes being washed and potatoes being peeled. The film is the witness in its most demanding form: it asks you to stay in the room when everything in you wants to leave.

Terrence Malick worked the crossing point pillar more explicitly than any other filmmaker in the tradition. The Tree of Life (2011) moves between the scale of a family in 1950s Texas and the scale of geological time, between the particular grief of a child's death and the sediment of the universe's first moments, because the crossing point requires both axes simultaneously. The film is not about transcendence. It is about the vertical line of transmission, felt in the body of a man returning to his childhood, reading the sediment of what made him.

Kelly Reichardt works the witness pillar in the quietest possible register. Her films give full attention to lives that American cinema has consistently failed to see: the ordinary, the provincial, the people moving through days that do not resolve into plots. Wendy and Lucy (2008) and First Cow (2019) are Youngian in their formal insistence that the ordinary life is worthy of this quality of attention, and that the attention itself is the argument.

Radu Jude is working in Romanian cinema with the sedimentary time pillar at the explicit center. I Do Not Care If We Go Down in History as Barbarians (2018) and Bad Luck Banging or Loony Porn (2021) press on the gap between what a nation's official narrative deposits in the historical record and what was actually happening. The films are formally aggressive in their insistence on the honest reading: here is the sediment. Here is what is in it. Here is what you have been pretending was not there.

The Performance and Dance Line

Pina Bausch (1940-2009) built Tanztheater from the same commitment Young brought to the novel: that the honest account of human experience cannot be made tidy without being falsified. Her pieces accumulate images and repetitions and contradictions the way Young's sentences accumulate clauses: nothing is resolved, everything is carried forward, the grief is present at its full weight. The permanent weather pillar is in every work. The witness pillar is in every performer who stays in the body of what they are doing without reducing it to something more manageable.

Lin Hixson and Matthew Goulish (Goat Island) worked the recursive construction pillar in performance through the 1990s and 2000s. Their pieces were built through a process of accumulation and return, where each rehearsal added layers to a structure that the performers could not see from outside. The incompletion ethics pillar is built into their method: the work does not arrive at a finished state. It arrives at the point where it is honest about what it is.
