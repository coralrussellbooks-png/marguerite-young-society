Don Quixote and the Windmill as Structural Motif in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling

Don Quixote's place among the six named quest traditions embedded in this novel. This chapter addresses something different: the windmill as a motif that Young distributed across 22 appearances in 12 chapters, assigned to every major consciousness in the novel, and used as a structural device that exceeds its source in Cervantes. All passages are drawn directly from the novel.

I. The Object in the Landscape

Chapter 6 contains the first physical windmill in the novel. It appears in the landscape through which the bus is traveling:

A derelict windmill stood in the midst of a flooded and vaporous field, its broken silver arm turning furiously in the wind.

This windmill is nobody's metaphor. It is an object in a flooded field, broken, turning furiously because the wind is turning it and not because any human intention has set it in motion. Its arm is broken. It is derelict. It turns anyway.

This is the windmill before it becomes anyone's symbol: a piece of machinery that has outlasted its purpose, still responding to external forces, unable to stop because stopping is not something windmills can choose. Young places this object in Chapter 6, after Moses has been called a mad Don Quixote chasing windmills in Chapter 1, and before the windmill becomes entangled with every other major consciousness in the novel. The derelict windmill in the flooded field is the motif's foundation: not a giant, not a delusion, not a metaphor yet. Just the thing itself, broken and still turning.

II. The Four Characters and What the Windmill Means to Each

The windmill attaches to four characters as an explicit descriptive or comparative device. In each case the attachment is made by a specific consciousness observing another character, or by the character observing themselves. The four attachments are not interchangeable. They say four different things about what a windmill is.

Moses Hunnecker: The Mad Don Quixote

The first explicit windmill attribution in the novel is Vera's assessment of Moses in Chapter 1:

Was he, after all, a bachelor, perhaps even some mad Don Quixote chasing windmills, a virgin spirit, nobody—and his family life, an emanation of my over-active imagination, really, my desire for established human relationships?

Vera is asking herself whether Moses is real. Whether his wife waiting at home is real. Whether the destination he is driving toward exists. The Don Quixote attribution is not an insult. It is a genuine question about the nature of the man at the wheel. Don Quixote's error was that he saw giants where there were windmills: he mistook the ordinary for the extraordinary, the mechanical for the monstrous. Vera is asking whether Moses has made the reverse error: whether he has mistaken the extraordinary for the ordinary, whether the life he believes he is driving toward is itself an illusion, whether he is going nowhere and calling it home.

Moses himself, later in Chapter 8, does not mention windmills. He talks about his hair, his politics, his quarrel with the doctor, Job's leviathan. The windmill attribution is Vera's reading of him, not his own self-image. This matters: Moses does not know he is Quixote. The observer applies the emblem. The character lives without it.

Cousin Hannah: The Human Windmill

Mr. Spitzer applies the windmill to Cousin Hannah twice in Chapter 52, and the application is intimate rather than dismissive. The first time:

Mr. Spitzer, turning the pages of old newspapers with almost faded print, had wondered what kind of a skirt this human windmill was searching for.

The second time, after her death:

She had been the human windmill, surely, turning even when the wind blew not—and in his absence had turned a page of the little book she had been reading when she died—had turned a page after she had died.

This is the most extraordinary windmill attribution in the novel. Cousin Hannah is the human windmill: she turns even when the wind blew not. She is not powered by external force the way the derelict windmill in Chapter 6 is. She generates her own motion. And she keeps turning after death: the page of the book she was reading turned after she died. The windmill does not stop when its operator dies. It has its own momentum.

Where Quixote saw a windmill as a giant, Mr. Spitzer sees Cousin Hannah as a windmill. He is not diminishing her. He is describing the nature of her energy: self-generating, continuous, not dependent on external conditions, still in motion after the body that housed it has stopped. The human windmill is the highest form of the motif in the novel. It is the windmill that has become autonomous.

Miss MacIntosh: The Windmill She Never Knew She Was

In Chapter 65, deep in the novel's second volume, another character says of Miss MacIntosh:

What had Miss MacIntosh been doing in Chicago? Chasing windmills. Miss MacIntosh had always been chasing windmills.

The windmill cards appear earlier, in Chapter 19, in Miss MacIntosh's own bedroom: almost translucent playing cards with curled edges and Dutch windmills on their silken backs. She plays with windmill cards and does not notice. She chases windmills and does not know it. The woman who embodied practical certainty, who trusted facts and geography and the clear reality of simple things, was all along a Quixote: she had a secret that contradicted her entire public performance, and the pursuit of the life she was performing, the sensible, the grounded, the undeceived, was itself the windmill she could never reach because she was not who she presented herself to be.

The windmill on the playing cards in Miss MacIntosh's bedroom sits on the backs of the cards: the side you do not see when you are playing the game. The windmill is on the hidden side of every card she holds.

Mrs. Hogden: The Woman Who Sold Her Windmill

Mrs. Hogden is the novel's fourth and most unexpected windmill figure. She does not chase windmills. She does not embody them. She lived in one. Her home on upper Long Island before she came to work for Catherine was an old Dutch windmill with winking eyes and broken arms. She sold it to take the job. Chapter 70 returns to this windmill seven times, more than any other single chapter in the novel, and the returns are increasingly elegiac.

The final windmill reference in Mrs. Hogden's arc is Mr. Spitzer's offer to restore her to her former position: "He called on Mrs. Hogden and, in the course of a long, somewhat painful interview, offered to restore her to her former position. She should have everything that she had lost, her Dutch windmill at a higher price than she had sold it for, a four-postered bed where she might sleep surrounded by effigies, everything, and enough money to last her the rest of her natural life and even perhaps a little longer. He was generous but not sure that Mrs. Hogden, smiling and beaming, had heard him rightly. She was so excited, he could not tell whether she was sad or happy. He believed that she was hardly sober."

While she lived in the windmill, an Arctic tern visited her every year. Young describes the tern as compelled by his own nature to circle the earth from pole to pole as if he were some kind of minor planet on its course in the starry endless heavens. The Arctic tern is one of the great natural questers: it makes the longest migration of any animal on earth, from Arctic to Antarctic and back, every year, without stopping. It visited Mrs. Hogden in her windmill annually for years and she found it distressing. She could not understand why it singled her out.

The windmill inhabitant received the world's greatest quester every year at her door and sold her home to go work in an opium house. The relationship between the windmill and the quest is most compressed here: the person who could have stayed in the windmill left it, and the quester who found the windmill every year kept circling.

The distribution is not random. The windmill is absent from the novel's opening bus chapters after Chapter 6, absent from the chapters in which Vera is most directly addressing her own consciousness, and concentrated in the Mr. Spitzer chapters of the second half, where it appears five times across Chapters 47 through 61. Mr. Spitzer is the character who sees windmills everywhere in the novel's second movement: in seagull wings, in rain drops, in book pages, in the woman he watched over for decades.

The windmill appears once in Catherine's vision in Chapter 37: there would be suddenly a whirling windmill. It materializes without explanation in her opium bedroom and disappears just as quickly. Catherine is the one character for whom the windmill is purely visionary: it is not applied to her as a description, and she does not live in one. It simply appears in her room.

IV. What Cervantes Built and What Young Did With It

Don Quixote's windmill scene is one of the most famous moments in Western literature precisely because it encodes a permanent question: who is deluded, the man who sees giants or the man who sees only windmills? Sancho Panza sees windmills. Quixote sees giants. The reader is invited to decide which perception is the error. Most readers decide Quixote is wrong. Cervantes arranged the scene so that certainty is harder to sustain than it appears.

Young took this question and distributed it across an entire novel. Every character in Miss MacIntosh, My Darling is making the same determination Quixote made: is this thing in front of me what it appears to be, or is it something else? Miss MacIntosh insisted it was what it appeared to be and was wrong. Moses drove toward a home that may have been imaginary. Cousin Hannah fought for a cause with the energy of a windmill that turned without wind. Mrs. Hogden sold her windmill and went to serve an opium dreamer.

The windmill in Cervantes is a single object in a single scene. In Miss MacIntosh it is a thread woven through the entire novel from Chapter 1 to Chapter 70, touching every major consciousness, appearing in a flooded landscape as a physical object before it becomes anyone's metaphor, and finally resolving not into a symbol but into a home that could have been kept and was sold.

The derelict windmill in the flooded field of Chapter 6 is the novel's answer to both Quixote and Sancho. It is neither a giant nor merely a machine. It is a broken thing still turning. It has no operator and no purpose and it turns furiously anyway. That is not Quixote's delusion. That is not Sancho's practicality. That is what Young found when she looked at the windmill without either of them: a broken arm, a flooded field, and motion that has no reason to stop.

V. The Novel as Tissues, Not Issues

It could be that Miss MacIntosh, My Darling like Don Quixote is not a book of issues but of tissues: like an onion, layer after layer revealed on subsequent rereadings. The windmill distribution is the clearest demonstration of what this means at the level of a single motif.

On a first reading, the windmill in Chapter 1 is a passing metaphor for Moses's erratic driving and uncertain domestic claims. On a second reading it connects to Chapter 6's broken windmill in the landscape the bus is passing through. On a third reading it connects forward to Miss MacIntosh's playing cards in Chapter 19 and Cousin Hannah's posthumous page-turning in Chapter 52 and Mrs. Hogden's sold home in Chapter 70.

None of these connections are announced. Young does not say: remember the windmill from Chapter 1. She places the windmill and lets it accumulate. By the time Mr. Spitzer is walking through rain in Chapter 49 walking like a living windmill, the image has been alive in the novel for forty-eight chapters and has already been attached to Moses and will later be attached to Cousin Hannah and Miss MacIntosh and will appear seven times in Mrs. Hogden's story.

This is the tissue argument demonstrated: the windmill is a thread running through the novel's entire length, gathering meaning with each appearance, never announcing itself as a symbol, never asking the reader to stop and notice it. It turns continuously, like Cousin Hannah. It turns even when the wind blew not.
