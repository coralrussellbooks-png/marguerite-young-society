Chapter 12

Other versions: Chapter hub · Research notes · The Wheel and the Spokes

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Miss MacIntosh · Vera Cartwheel

Pillars: The Margins Reveal the Truth

The Questions

Big Ben keeps strict time; Catherine’s clocks are all stopped. Is either the true time, or are both constructions of it?

Miss MacIntosh says “omniscience had not been given to us” and turns to practical work. Is that humility, or a blueprint sealing itself against too much?

What does the garden of the blind let its keepers know that the sighted cannot?

If “what does not exist exists if we believe in it,” how solid is the line between the built and the real?

Time in this chapter is two constructions facing each other. The Big Ben alarm clock keeps strict measured time, the servants’ rigid schedule, the day cut into countable units; Catherine’s clocks are all stopped, frozen at scattered hours, time refused rather than counted. Neither is time itself. Both are ways of building it, and the chapter sets the counted against the frozen so that the reader sees the counting as a construction too. Measured time is not more real than stopped time; it is only the other agreement.

Miss MacIntosh meets the vertigo of this with her blueprint. “Omniscience had not been given to us,” she says, and turns to the sandbag dummy and the mile along the beach, insisting that to reach for the full scope of reality is to court madness. On its face this is humility, and there is truth in it. But it is also the finished blueprint defending itself: rather than hold the too-muchness open, she seals it off and calls the sealing common sense. Her practicality here is a wall built against the dream, and she names the wall a virtue.

The garden of the blind is the chapter’s edge. Designed for touch and smell, the flowers read like Braille, the blooms all odor, it lets its keepers and its visitors know the world through senses the sighted let go slack. Vera closes her eyes and reads the garden with her hands. This is The Margins Reveal the Truth in its gentlest form: a place where the ordinary sensory world is remade from a different position, and from that position things show that daylight sight had flattened.

And under it runs a line that unsettles the distinction the chapter seems to draw: “what does not exist exists if we believe in it.” Belief builds. The “erroneous dream” can be a bridge between life and death, a truth precisely in its error. If the believed-in comes to exist by being believed, then the solid line between the built and the real that Miss MacIntosh guards so carefully was never as solid as her sandbag.

What the Chapter Refuses, and the Footing It Offers

The chapter refuses the reading that takes Miss MacIntosh’s practicality as its steadying truth against Vera’s dreamy excess, and the reading that takes the dream as simply superior to the ordinary. It is not choosing measured time over stopped, sight over touch, sense over dream.

The footing is that time is built, whether counted or frozen; the senses are constructions of the world as much as reports of it; and belief itself builds, so that what does not exist exists if we believe in it. Miss MacIntosh’s turn to the practical is a real steadiness and also a seal, humility and wall at once. The book trusts the stance that holds the too-muchness open rather than walling it, and that reads the edge, the blind garden, as a place of knowledge rather than lack. The measured and the plain are constructions too, not the bedrock they claim.

For Reflection

Which of one’s ways of keeping time is mistaken for time itself?

When someone calls a limit humility, is it always humility, or sometimes a wall?

What would the world disclose if read with the hands, or the nose, instead of the eyes?
