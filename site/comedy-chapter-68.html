Definition from research

The landlord and the hangman resume their macabre comedy, and the chapter runs on their exchange of grim confidences. The hangman, Mr. Weed, tells his life story in a "stifled voice as if there were a rope being drawn around his neck," a respectable family man who dances at the edge of the lamplight and wipes his forehead with the black flag he waves when a knot has done its work. The landlord grumbles, plays up his deafness, and swaps prophecies of drowning against the hangman's prophecies of hanging. The relief is entirely in tone, two men treating the gallows and the grave as ordinary trades to be complained about over a counter.

Doctor O'Leary, the mad country physician, is a comic-grotesque figure who recurs through the late chapters and earns an entry of his own. His method is to diagnose the living as dead and the dead as merely sleeping. He tells Vera she has "carried a marble statue in your womb," a child turned to stone "except for its pink heel," and delivers the verdict with cheerful finality, "you are in your grave, my dear, and it is a cherub sleeping in this pool." The same logic surfaces when he delivers a real baby while convinced he is dreaming, refuses the father's offered cigar, and renounces medicine on the spot: "Life is a dream, and I have been wrong and much wronged, and I must give up this practice." Like the Egyptian scene, the comedy comes from a literal, professional man forever mistaking the dream for the diagnosis.
