The Floor and the Framework

Constitution of the United States, Revised Draft

← On the Economic Preconditions of a Philosophically Whole Life · The Floor and the Framework · A Note on These Documents →

Preamble

We, the people of this nation (all of us, without exception of race, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, national origin, religion, disability, class, or condition of birth) establish this Constitution. We do so not to hand our power to representatives and trust that it will be used wisely. We do so to hold that power ourselves, delegating it where delegation is practical and reclaiming it where it is not. Government answers to the people directly. This document is how we make that true.

Article I

The People Are the First Branch

Before any structure of elected government, the people hold sovereign legislative power directly. This is not a metaphor. It is a mechanism.

National initiative: Any proposed law or constitutional amendment may be placed before the full voting public by petition. A petition signed by two percent of registered voters triggers a national referendum. A majority vote enacts the measure into law, with the same force as any act of Congress. Constitutional amendments by initiative require sixty percent approval.

National referendum: Congress must submit any law that significantly restructures voting rights, civil rights protections, or the powers of government itself to a public referendum before it takes effect. The people confirm or reject it. A simple majority decides.

Popular recall: Any elected federal official may be removed from office before their term ends by a recall petition signed by ten percent of voters in their district or state, followed by a recall election. The replacement is elected simultaneously on the same ballot, so no office is vacant and no appointment fills a democratic seat.

Participatory budgeting: A fixed percentage of the federal discretionary budget, no less than five percent, is allocated annually through direct public vote. Citizens propose, deliberate, and decide how these funds are spent. The process is open, multilingual, accessible to all, and conducted both digitally and in person.

On manipulation: Direct democracy is only as good as the information available to voters. Corporate spending, foreign interference, and coordinated disinformation campaigns in referendum contests are treated as election fraud. Independent public media, funded by the government and free to report without government direction, is a constitutional obligation, not an option. That public media is further obligated to represent the full plurality of human voices and experiences, including those historically excluded from the version of reality held by those already in power, rather than to reproduce a single dominant version of public reality. Democracy requires a commons of expression adequate to the actual diversity of the people it serves.

On civic capacity: The ability to participate meaningfully in self-governance is a skill, not an instinct. The government is obligated to support civic education at every level: education in the practice of thinking through disagreements together, in holding competing perspectives simultaneously, in recognizing the difference between the assumptions and stories we take for granted and the versions held by others. Citizens who can witness, who can stay in the room with complexity without demanding premature resolution, are the foundation of functional democracy. That capacity must be cultivated, not assumed.

Article II

The Legislature: Making Law on Behalf of the People

Congress makes law between elections and referendums. It has two chambers. The House of Representatives seats are allocated by full population, with every person counted as a whole person. Senate seats are allocated by state population; no state may block the national majority simply because it is small.

Members of Congress are elected by ranked-choice voting in districts that elect more than one representative, so that minority communities and third parties achieve meaningful representation proportional to their numbers. No gerrymandering: district maps are drawn by independent commissions, with members drawn equally from all parties and from citizens who belong to none, subject to public comment and judicial review.

Any law passed by Congress may be challenged by popular referendum within two years of passage. A petition of three percent of registered voters triggers the vote. If the public rejects the law, it is repealed. This is the legislative check that the original Constitution never provided: the people may undo what their representatives do.

On money: Campaign contributions are capped and publicly disclosed in real time. No private entity may spend more than a citizen on elections. Elected officials may not receive income from private sources during their term. Any meeting or communication between a lobbyist and an elected official is a matter of public record within 48 hours.

Article III

The Executive: Carrying Out What the People Decide

The President executes law. The President does not make law, does not suspend law, and does not stand above it. The President is elected by national popular vote, every vote weighted equally regardless of geography.

The President's financial records, tax returns, and any situation where the President's personal finances could benefit from a government decision must be publicly disclosed. No person may serve as President while holding private financial stakes in government contracts, industries overseen by the federal government, or foreign governments. Violation is automatic grounds for removal.

The President may not pardon themselves, members of their administration, or family members for acts committed while in office or related to their official duties. Emergency powers are limited to ninety days without being renewed by Congress and are subject to immediate judicial review. The people may trigger a recall referendum against the President by petition of fifteen percent of registered voters.

Article IV

The Judiciary: Protecting Rights Against Majorities

The courts interpret this Constitution. Their specific function is to protect the rights listed in Article VII, especially when those rights are threatened by popular majorities. Direct democracy does not override fundamental rights. The people may not vote away another group's personhood, equal protection, or dignity.

Supreme Court justices serve fixed eighteen-year terms, with one appointment every two years, so that no single administration appoints too many justices. Justices may not accept gifts, travel, financial benefits, or any compensation from anyone with a case before the court. A justice must step aside from any case where they have a personal or financial interest. Failure to step aside when required is grounds for removal by a two-thirds vote of the full Congress, confirmed by public referendum.

Judicial nominees are confirmed by a public hearing process that is fully transparent, with written records available to every citizen, followed by Senate confirmation by a majority vote.

Article V

States, Localities, and the Federal Structure

States govern themselves in matters of local concern. Cities and localities hold genuine governing authority over local matters; state governments may not strip local governments of their power to respond to their own communities' needs.

No state may deny any person the rights guaranteed in Article VII. The rights of people do not end at state lines. States may expand rights beyond the minimum rights this Constitution guarantees; they may never contract below it.

Direct democracy mechanisms apply at every level of government. States and localities are encouraged to develop their own initiative, referendum, and recall systems consistent with this Constitution's principles. Federal funding supports the tools and systems people need to participate in democracy, including internet access, polling locations, and civic education, so that participation is not limited by wealth, geography, or access to technology.

Article VI

Amending This Constitution

This Constitution can be changed by two paths: the traditional path, requiring two-thirds of Congress and approval by three-quarters of states measured by population; or the direct path, requiring a national initiative approved by sixty percent of voters in a national referendum, with at least fifty percent of registered voters participating.

No amendment may remove the rights guaranteed in Article VII, strip the vote from any citizen, or eliminate the direct democracy mechanisms established in Article I. These are the floor. They may be expanded but never removed.

Article VII

The Rights of All People: These Are the Floor, Not the Ceiling

Equal protection: Every person is equal before the law. No law may treat people differently based on race, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, religion, national origin, disability, or economic status.

The vote: Every person sixteen or older votes, with full and equal standing. Voting is a right, not a privilege. It may not be restricted by criminal record, immigration status, cognitive or physical disability, guardianship status, ID requirement designed to exclude, or any administrative barrier. A person under guardianship or held in prison keeps the full right to vote. Registration is automatic at sixteen. Election Day is a national holiday. The Electoral College is abolished.

Free expression: Speech, press, religion, and peaceful assembly are protected. No government may suppress dissent, restrict journalism, or establish any religion as official.

Due process: No person may be imprisoned, punished, or deprived of property without fair legal process. This applies to every person on this soil, regardless of citizenship status.

Economic floor: Every person has the right to adequate food, housing, healthcare, and education. The government is obligated to ensure no one falls below a dignified minimum. This floor is not charity. It is the condition of possibility for everything else in this Constitution. A person whose entire capacity is consumed by survival cannot participate in self-governance, cannot develop the plural self the culture requires, cannot grieve honestly, cannot witness, cannot build. The economic floor is the material prerequisite for the kind of citizenship this Constitution envisions. Courts may enforce it.

Education: Every person has the right to public education from early childhood through post-secondary or vocational training, free at the point of use. Public education is the shared space where a plural society learns to be in a room together, and private schools that select their students by wealth, religion, or any other criterion of exclusion are inconsistent with this principle. Funding is equalized across all public schools, and federal funding is obligated to close resource gaps wherever they exist, so that the accident of zip code does not determine the quality of education a child receives. Students are entitled to the complete record of human history and experience, including the histories of communities historically excluded and the uncomfortable parts. Public education is obligated to develop the capacity for complexity: for holding multiple perspectives at once and staying in the room with difficulty rather than demanding its premature resolution. Education that produces compliance rather than witnesses is a constitutional failure.

Bodily autonomy: Every person has sovereignty over their own body. Medical decisions belong to the individual. The state may not compel pregnancy, compel medical treatment, or police private consensual conduct.

Environmental right: Every person has the right to clean air, clean water, and a livable climate. Future generations hold this right as well. The natural world carries what human civilization produces; its capacity to carry is not unlimited and its degradation is not reversible on human timescales. The government is obligated to act; inaction in the face of documented environmental harm is a constitutional violation.

Privacy: Every person has the right to privacy in their communications, data, and personal life. Surveillance requires a judicial warrant. No government or corporation may collect personal data without informed, revocable consent.

Freedom from caste: No hereditary privilege, title, or structural caste system may be recognized or enforced by law. Wealth may not be used to purchase rights unavailable to others. Equal justice is not a metaphor.

Freedom from cruel punishment: No person may be subjected to cruel, degrading, or disproportionate punishment. The state is obligated to witness the suffering it produces: to see it, name it, and be accountable for it. Solitary confinement beyond seventy-two hours, the death penalty, and conditions of incarceration that destroy physical or psychological health are constitutional violations. The carceral system is not outside the frame of equal protection. Every person held by the state retains full human dignity. Punishment that requires looking away from the person being punished is not justice. It is the demand that suffering be made invisible so that the system producing it need not reckon with itself.

Historical repair: This nation was built on losses that have not resolved. The theft of indigenous land, the centuries of enslaved labor, the systematic exclusion of women, queer people, disabled people, and immigrants from the promises this nation made about itself: these are not events in the past. They are conditions in the present, carried forward in bodies, in communities, in landscapes, and in the accumulated structures of who has access to what. The Earth holds what the political system has demanded be called finished. The people most directly descended from these losses cannot be asked to perform a recovery they have not experienced. The federal government is obligated to establish and fund a permanent truth commission and a process of acknowledgment and repair for historical wrongs: not as punishment, not as the final word, but as the honest institutional acknowledgment that the grief is cumulative, the carrying is collective, and the work of reckoning does not end on a deadline set by law. That process must be ongoing, multilingual, community-rooted, and subject to direct democratic oversight.

Rights retained by the people: The rights listed in this Constitution do not constitute an exhaustive list. The people retain all rights not explicitly prohibited by this document. No branch of government may use the absence of explicit listing to deny or disparage a right held by the people. Rights are not granted by constitutions: they are recognized by them, and recognition is always subject to expansion as the people's understanding of their own dignity deepens.

Each right listed above is a floor. Companion documents explain how each right is put into practice in full.

Article VIII

Citizenship and Belonging

Citizenship is established by three paths: birthright, for every person born on this soil regardless of the status of their parents; applying for citizenship, through a process that is fair, accessible, and not designed as an obstacle course; and long-term residency, for every person who has lived here, worked here, raised families here, and been subject to the laws here for a defined continuous period. Achieving a legal status through bureaucratic process is not the measure of belonging. Presence and subjection to law are the measure of belonging.

Voting rights extend to all residents subject to the laws of this nation, not citizens alone. The person who pays taxes here, who sends children to schools here, who is arrested under laws made here, has a stake in who makes those laws. The demand that people be governed without voice is the original grievance of this nation's founding and it has never been fully resolved. It is resolved here.

The asylum and immigration system is redesigned around the principle that people do not abandon their homes and communities without cause, and that the causes are knowable and often traceable to the decisions of this government. The nation that destabilized the country you fled has an obligation toward you that exceeds the current framework. Processing is humane, timely, and conducted with the full recognition that the person seeking entry is a full person with a history and a life, not a category to be sorted. No person is held in detention under conditions that violate the freedom from cruel punishment provision of Article VII.

Indigenous peoples' relationship to this land precedes the nation that now administers it. Tribal sovereignty is a constitutional fact, not a federal concession. Treaties are honored. The long-term residency path to citizenship does not apply to people whose nations predate this one: they are not immigrants on their own land.

Article IX

Security and the Common Defense

Security means the protection of people's actual ability to live full lives: to have food, housing, healthcare, education, bodily autonomy, environmental safety, and freedom from violence. It does not mean the extension of military or economic dominance over other countries. The defense of this nation is the defense of its people and their rights, not the defense of the interests of its most powerful institutions.

The use of military force requires a declaration of war by Congress. No President may commit forces to sustained combat without approval from Congress. Emergency deployments are permitted for up to thirty days without Congressional approval, after which Congress must vote to authorize the continuation of the deployment or it ends. The people may not be taken to war by executive decision alone. The decision to use violence in the name of the nation belongs to the nation's representatives, accountable to the nation's people, and subject to the nation's referendum mechanisms when the people demand it.

Companies that profit from military contracts may not purchase influence over security policy. Contracts, lobbying contacts, and financial relationships between these companies and elected or appointed officials are subject to the full transparency provisions of this Constitution. The movement of military leaders directly into employment with defense companies is a conflict of interest subject to the same prohibitions as any other. Security policy is made in the public interest, not the interest of the industry that profits from its expansion.

Peace-building, conflict prevention, and the systems and institutions that support negotiation and peaceful resolution of conflict are constitutional obligations, funded at a ratio defined by law relative to military spending. A nation that invests only in the capacity for violence and not in the conditions that make violence unnecessary has chosen a particular construction of how the world works and called it realism. It is not realism. It is a system that produces the very conflicts it claims to prepare for. Security spending that comes at the cost of the economic floor, the environmental right, or the education provision is itself a security failure: a population without a floor is not a secure population regardless of the force deployed in its name.

Existing military capacity is progressively redirected toward the obligations this Constitution has already established: engineering and infrastructure toward the environmental right and climate resilience; the military's ability to move people and supplies toward disaster response and public health; research and development toward the shift to clean and renewable energy; medical infrastructure toward the universal healthcare obligation. Conversion is not abolition. It is the redirection of enormous existing capacity toward the purposes a government of the people actually requires.

Article X

The Economic System: What It Is For

The economy exists to serve the people. The people do not exist to serve the economy. How we structure the economy is a political choice that democracy can change, not a natural law exempt from democratic accountability. The market price is not the measure of value. Care, community, the natural world, and the interior life have value that the market does not count and cannot count. The failure to count them is a political choice, not an objective fact about what has value.

No person's access to the economic floor established in Article VII may be contingent on what the market currently values or demands. The person who is grieving, caring for another, disabled, young, old, or simply not currently demanded by the market retains full access to food, housing, healthcare, and education. Survival is not a reward for productivity. It is the condition of possibility for everything else.

The care economy is counted. The work of raising children, tending the sick, maintaining households and communities, and sustaining the relationships on which all other economic activity depends is recognized as productive work in every measure of national wellbeing. An economy that does not count this work has decided in advance whose labor matters. That decision is reversed here.

Wealth beyond what any person can meaningfully use is taxed at rates that fund the floor for everyone. The accumulation of that wealth was only possible because of the collective infrastructure: the educated workforce, the legal system, the public research, the stable society that the floor provision maintains. Taxation of concentrated wealth is not redistribution. It is the return of what the commons made possible to the commons that made it possible.

Public options exist in every domain where the market systematically fails to serve the floor: banking, healthcare, housing, energy, broadband, and any other domain where taking profit from people's basic needs produces outcomes inconsistent with the rights listed in Article VII. The public option does not prohibit private enterprise. It ensures that private enterprise competes on the basis of genuine value rather than on the basis of having no competition.

Businesses owned and governed by their workers are supported by law and by public investment as alternatives to the concentration of ownership that produces concentrated power. The person who works in a place has a stake in how that place is governed that the current system does not count. It is counted here.

National wellbeing is measured by whether people can live full lives: by the health of the floor, the quality of the air and water, the condition of care relationships, the time available for rest and community, the distribution of security across the full population. Growth in the market price of things is not the measure. The good economy is the one that operates within the floor and the limits of what the natural world can sustain simultaneously, in the space where human life can actually be lived.

Article XI

What This Constitution Is For

This document exists because power concentrates. It exists because majorities have harmed minorities. It exists because the original promises of this nation were written with exclusions built in, and those exclusions had to be torn out over generations at enormous cost in blood and suffering. We do not pretend that writing a better document ends that struggle. We write it anyway, because the struggle needs a map.

Every provision here is meant to be used. Rights that cannot be enforced are decoration. Democracy that cannot reach actual decisions is theater. The people are not the audience. The people are the government.

This Constitution is not finished. No constitution ever is. The amendment process exists because revision is integrity, not failure. What this document builds cannot be torn down. What it has not yet imagined can always be added. The floor is permanent. The ceiling keeps rising.

This Constitution implies a set of companion documents that do the work it can only gesture at: on patriarchy, sexism, and misogyny; women, children, and the unpaid economy; education; the abolition of private schooling; the economic system; voting and electoral systems; citizenship and immigration; healthcare; historical grief and reparative justice; criminal justice and the carceral system; environmental rights and obligations; media and information; and security and the common defense. Those documents follow.

Closing note

This document is written to be readable by the people it governs, including the sixteen-year-olds who hold full voting rights under it. Companion documents and civic education materials exist to support full engagement with every provision. No one is expected to arrive at this document already knowing everything it contains. That is what the education obligation is for.
