Movement Two: The Pillars in the Traditions

Characters: Catherine Cartwheel · Cousin Hannah · Joachim Spitzer · Miss MacIntosh · Peron Spitzer

Philosophy: Taoism and Zhuangzi · Hinduism · Buddhism

Pillars: Pillar 1 (Not One Self) · Pillar 2 (Reality Is Constructed) · Pillar 10 (The Sacred Is Here)

Chapters discussed: 2, 14, 18, 25, 28, 29, 37, 38, 39, 41, 43, 45, 48, 49, 50, 51, 52

Perhaps there had never been actuality. Perhaps this world was the spectre of the spectre, the dream of the dream.

Miss MacIntosh, My Darling, Chapter 49

Sources Without Names

Young rarely names her sources. She takes a saying or an idea and puts it in her own words, often dropping the name and changing the famous phrase. That habit hides some of the oldest thinking in the novel, and much of it comes from China and India. The novel never names Zhuangzi, the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita, or the Buddha. Nirvana appears once, in a list. Yet when To All My Darlings traced the lines one by one, these traditions turned up in chapter after chapter: Hindu thought in eighteen chapters, Buddhist thought in twelve, Zhuangzi and the Tao in ten.

They deserve the same weight in this book as Plato and Descartes. And they matter for a particular reason. Plato tells us to climb out of the dream into the sun. These traditions say something closer to what Young's people live: the dream and the world are woven together, and wisdom is learning to see that while you go on living in it.

The Butterfly

Zhuangzi was a Chinese philosopher of the fourth century BCE, one of the founders of Taoism. His most famous story is short: “Once upon a time, I, Chuang Tzŭ, dreamt I was a butterfly, fluttering hither and thither, to all intents and purposes a butterfly.” Then he woke. “Now I do not know whether I was then a man dreaming I was a butterfly, or whether I am now a butterfly, dreaming I am a man” (Chuang Tzŭ, translated by Herbert A. Giles, 1889, chapter 2).

That question runs all through Young's novel. In Chapter 2, Vera says of Catherine: “My mother pretended that the real was the dream, that the dream was the real.” Catherine does not think she is alone in this. For everyone, “the dream must suffice, for reality bears with it always an aspect of fateful disappointment.”

Mr. Spitzer is the novel's butterfly man. In Chapter 28, “Sometimes the butterfly perching upon the flower had seemed as identical to the flower as twin to twin, and the flower had seemed to fly away, and the winged petals had remained, much to his bewilderment.” In Chapter 29 he cannot always tell butterfly from moth, “just as it might at times be difficult to distinguish between brother and brother or between the ecstasy of happiness and that of sorrow.” In Chapter 38 the question is put to him almost in Zhuangzi's own form: “Did he so much as know whether he was a man or a butterfly, a lost psyche drifting down the wind?”

For Mr. Spitzer the butterfly question is also the twin question. Which brother died, and which is dreaming the other? In Chapter 49 he sees “a butterfly pirouetting in a cloud, butterfly which had seemed one butterfly but was always two butterflies conjoining over the suddenly shining wave, one with its image.” And in Chapter 50 he wonders whether he lives “by that dream which might be this extension beyond his death, dream dreamed by another dreamer.” Zhuangzi woke and could not say which one he was. Mr. Spitzer never quite wakes.

Heaven's Net

The Tao Te Ching, the old Chinese book of the Way credited to Laozi, says in chapter 73: “The meshes of the net of Heaven are large; far apart, but letting nothing escape” (translated by James Legge, 1891). In Chapter 18, on the night Vera sees her bald, Miss MacIntosh says it in her own way: “Heaven’s nets are woven very wide,” her voice drifting and high as if she were far away, “but none shall escape them.”

What she says just before it is more startling still. The woman of plain fact tells Vera: “It is all a dream, a poor man’s dream,” her voice common and flat, “and it was always this, but you were living in a fool’s paradise, and now you know there is no answer.” And after it: “We each must die and know that this life was all a dream, and there was nothing.” With the wig off, even Miss MacIntosh speaks the butterfly's language.

The Tao Te Ching also teaches that being comes out of non-being: “All things under heaven sprang from It as existing (and named); that existence sprang from It as non-existent (and not named)” (chapter 40, Legge). In Chapter 41, Catherine's house holds that same turn of thought: “The love which had never come into being was a greater love than love, and there was greater passion in restraint than in passion, greater being in non-being.”

Maya, the World as a Show

In Indian thought, maya is the power that makes the world appear as it does: an illusion, or better, a magic show, where things seem to be present but are not what they seem. The world is not nothing. It is real as a show is real. The scholar Heinrich Zimmer put the old teaching this way: “Life in the cycle of the countless rebirths is like a vision in a dream. The gods on high, the mute trees and the stones, are alike apparitions in this phantasy” (Myths and Symbols in Indian Art and Civilization, edited by Joseph Campbell, 1946). In Hindu story the god Vishnu sleeps on the cosmic ocean, and the world is his dream.

Catherine comes to the same thought from her bed. In Chapter 39: “And was not God dreaming, too, dreaming these opium dreams, so who could be right if God was dreaming?” If the maker is dreaming, then no one in the house holds the one true view. The dreamer in the bed is not further from reality than anyone else.

Chapter 41, the long chapter of Catherine's visitors, is full of lines that sit close to the Upanishads, the old Hindu teachings on the self and the world. The Isha Upanishad says of the one reality: “That One, though motionless, is swifter than the mind. The senses can never overtake It, for It ever goes before.” “It moves and It moves not. It is far and also It is near. It is within and also It is without all this” (verses 4 and 5, translated by Swami Paramananda, 1919). Of Catherine, motionless in her bed, the novel says: “She was swifter than her own mind, swifter than her mercurial thoughts. She was far, yet she was near. She was within, yet she was outside. She was life, yet she was also not life. She was everywhere at once because she was nowhere, diffused through starlight.”

The same chapter calls Catherine's dreams “an action in inaction,” close to the Bhagavad Gita: “He who sees inaction in action, and action in inaction, is wise among men” (4.18, translated by Kashinath Trimbak Telang, 1882). She “could be many persons with many heads, limbs, arms,” like the cosmic being of the Rig Veda, “A thousand heads hath Purusha, a thousand eyes, a thousand feet” (10.90.1, translated by Ralph T. H. Griffith, 1896). And she asks, “were there not many roads to the crown of her head?” That is close to a teaching Swami Vivekananda brought to the World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago in 1893, quoting an old hymn: “As the different streams having their sources in different places all mingle their water in the sea, so, O Lord, the different paths which men take through different tendencies, various though they appear, crooked or straight, all lead to Thee.”

The Chariot, the Doors, and the Upside-Down Tree

Other pictures from the Upanishads and the Gita turn up across the book. In Chapter 14, Catherine pictures herself this way: “Her body was the carriage, and her soul, now it seemed, was the passenger, and her horses were her will, and the coachman was her conscience.” The Katha Upanishad uses the same picture: “Know the Self to be sitting in the chariot, the body to be the chariot, the intellect (buddhi) the charioteer, and the mind the reins. The senses they call the horses” (translated by F. Max Müller, 1884). Plato's Phaedrus has a charioteer too. Young's carriage belongs to both.

In Chapter 48, Mr. Spitzer thinks that his brother Peron “no doubt had gone through the door of the sun as Mr. Spitzer had gone through the door of the moon.” In the Upanishads the wise depart “through the sun to where that immortal Person dwells” (Mundaka Upanishad 1.2.11, Müller), while those who will be born again go by the path of the moon (Chandogya Upanishad 5.10). In Chapter 50 there is “the door of truth which was covered with a golden disc and was never opened by man,” and the Isha Upanishad prays: “The door of the True is covered with a golden disk. Open that, O Pûshan, that we may see the nature of the True” (verse 15, Müller, 1879).

The Bhagavad Gita (15.1) and the Katha Upanishad describe an eternal fig tree with its roots above and its branches below. In Chapter 37 there are “trees of enormous crystal growing with their roots in the clouded sky, their branches under the earth,” and in Chapter 52 Mr. Spitzer remembers “the tree rooted in clouds with its branches growing downward.” In Chapter 51 he is “some old, dreaming tortoise with the stars upon his back,” who “upheld the starry universe,” a picture that belongs to Vishnu's tortoise form, which bears the world, and to the Lenape and other peoples for whom North America is Turtle Island, the earth on a great turtle's back.

And in Chapter 45, in the long meditation around Cousin Hannah's death, the novel says: “Perhaps at the deepest depths there is no individual or image, neither bird nor fish nor flower. Thus at the depths of sleep no face is remembered. All things have flowed into all things. A man may die each night, sinking into the drowned depths of sleep. The deepest sleep is dreamless.” The Mandukya Upanishad names deep, dreamless sleep, where the sleeper desires nothing and dreams no dream, as the third state of the self.

The Other Shore

Buddhism grew from the same Indian ground. The Buddha taught that suffering comes from craving, that nothing has a fixed, lasting self, and that freedom is a crossing to “the other shore.” The Heart Sutra ends with a chant for that crossing: “Gone, gone, gone beyond, gone altogether beyond, awakening, hail.”

In Chapter 43, dying, Cousin Hannah cries out: “A light, a light! I see a light upon the other shore.” Mr. Spitzer, listening, has already told himself where he will be when her ship goes down: “For he would be upon the other shore.” Earlier, in Chapter 29, he imagines a time “when conflicts were resolved at every level of thought, when he was divested of desire.” In the same chapter, Nirvana sits in his list of heavens beside Valhalla and the happy hunting grounds, and the list ends: “God was not God in Himself, Mr. Spitzer believed, but in these pitiful images of God.” And in Chapter 25 he asks, “What was this life, this life which was no life but a temporary incarnation, a mistaken embodiment?”

The Buddha's old parable of the blind men and the elephant (Udana 6.4) tells of men who each touch one part of the animal and each swear that the elephant is only that part. Mr. Spitzer knows the feeling. In Chapter 51: “And if reality had been a horse, he would have known no more of reality than the coachman’s little light or the horse’s tail.” In the same chapter comes a line close to the Buddhist teaching that nothing stands alone, that each thing arises from others in a chain: “We lived by an imperfect concatenation, even as we died—by echoes, tremblings, reverberations, sourceless thoughts.”

Why These Traditions Belong Here

The Western story most of this book has followed starts with Plato: the world of the senses is a cave of shadows, and the wise climb out of it. Young turns that story over, as Flipping the Cave shows. The traditions in this essay never needed turning over. For Zhuangzi, waking and dreaming are both ways the Way moves. For the Upanishads and the Gita, the show of maya is how the one reality appears. For the Buddha, the self that clings is the illusion, and seeing through it does not mean leaving the world but living in it without grasping. Young's Catherine, her Mr. Spitzer, even her Miss MacIntosh with the wig off, keep arriving at these old answers in their own words. The novel is in conversation with China and India as fully as with Athens.

The Radical Immanence Reading

In the language of Radical Immanence, maya and the butterfly are Pillar 2 (Reality Is Constructed): the world is real as a dream or a show is real, made and still worth living in. Mr. Spitzer's two butterflies, “one with its image,” and the Buddha's teaching of no fixed self are Pillar 1 (Not One Self). And the net of Heaven, the door of the sun, and the other shore glimpsed from a deathbed in an ordinary house are Pillar 10 (The Sacred Is Here): whatever holds the world is found inside it, not above it.
